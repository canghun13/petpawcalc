Frequently Asked Questions

Should I clean my pet's ears before the vet appointment?

No — leave them alone for a couple of days beforehand. The discharge is the diagnostic material. A swab of it under the microscope is what tells your vet whether they are dealing with yeast, cocci or rods, and those need different treatment. Cleaning it out first removes exactly what they need to look at and can mean a wasted appointment or a guessed prescription. Photograph the ear if it looks dramatic, then leave it.

Why does my vet need to check the eardrum?

Because a perforated eardrum changes what can safely be put into the ear. Some medications and cleaning solutions can reach the middle ear through a perforation and cause damage including hearing loss, which is why veterinary sources treat otoscopic examination as mandatory before treatment. It also matters diagnostically: middle ear disease acts as a reservoir that reseeds the outer ear, and is reported in over half of chronic or recurrent cases in one summary.

Is it worth asking for a culture?

Sometimes, and your vet is the right person to judge it. Cytology comes first because it is quick, cheap and immediately guides treatment. A culture with sensitivity testing is generally reserved for cases where cytology shows rod-shaped bacteria, where treatment has already failed, or where the infection is chronic — because rods such as Pseudomonas are frequently resistant and picking the wrong drug wastes weeks. Asking whether this case has reached that threshold is a reasonable question.

How do I know the infection has actually cleared?

By having it rechecked, not by looking at it. An ear that looks and smells better on the outside can still have organisms on cytology, and stopping there is one of the most common routes to a relapse a few weeks later. Book the recheck before you leave the first appointment, keep giving medication for the full prescribed course rather than until it looks fine, and treat the recheck cytology as the thing that defines the end of treatment.

When should we see a veterinary dermatologist?

It is reasonable to ask about referral once episodes are frequent, an ear never fully clears, cytology repeatedly shows rods, or the canal has started to narrow or thicken. Dermatologists have equipment and techniques a general practice may not — video-otoscopy and deep flushing under anaesthesia in particular — and they investigate the underlying allergic disease systematically. Asking the question early is not a criticism of your vet; most will raise it themselves at that point.

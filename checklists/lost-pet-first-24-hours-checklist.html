Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do in the first hour a pet goes missing?

Search the house again, then search your own property on foot with a torch, then ask immediate neighbours for permission to search theirs. That is the whole first hour. The urge to get in the car and drive around calling is strong and is usually the least productive thing you can do — particularly for a cat, which is statistically most likely to be hiding silently within a few houses of your door. Posting and phoning come after the physical search, not before it.

How do I update my pet's microchip if they are missing?

Contact the registry that holds the chip number — it is on your adoption or vet paperwork, and a vet or shelter can scan a littermate or check records if you cannot find it. Do two things: confirm the phone number on file is one you actually answer, and flag the pet as missing so anyone scanning the chip sees that immediately. An out-of-date phone number is one of the most common reasons a chipped pet does not get home, and it is fixable in ten minutes.

Should I offer a reward?

Mentioning that a reward is offered is fine and can motivate people to look properly. Naming a large specific amount is less advisable, because it attracts false claims and, occasionally, people who will hold an animal for money. A common approach is to say a reward is offered without specifying the figure, and to withhold one identifying detail about your pet — a scar, a marking, a collar tag — so that you can check whether someone claiming to have your pet actually does.

Is it worth searching at night?

Very much so, and it is the search most people skip. Traffic and noise drop, which makes a frightened animal far more willing to move, and a torch picks up eyeshine from inside dark cavities where an animal is otherwise invisible. For cats in particular, a 2am search of the same ground you covered at noon is not a repeat — it is a different search with much better odds.

How long should I keep looking?

Longer than most people do, while front-loading the effort. Research on missing cats found roughly a third recovered by their owner within seven days and about 56% by two months, with few found alive after 90 days — so the first week deserves the most intense effort, but the following weeks are far from hopeless. Keep the feeding station and camera running, keep re-checking shelters in person, and keep your posting current rather than letting it go stale.

Frequently Asked Questions

When is my dog or cat considered a senior?

It depends mostly on size for dogs: small breeds are often considered senior around 10-12 years old, medium breeds around 8-10, large breeds around 6-8, and giant breeds as early as 5-6. Cats are generally considered senior starting around 10-12 years old, regardless of size. These are general guidelines, not a hard cutoff - your vet can help place your individual pet more precisely.

Does becoming a senior mean my pet is sick?

No - reaching senior age is a normal life stage, not a diagnosis. It's a signal to adjust the level of monitoring and support your pet gets, similar to how care needs shift at other life stages, not a sign that something is wrong. Plenty of senior pets are perfectly healthy and active with the right ongoing care.

Why do senior pets need vet visits twice a year instead of once?

Conditions common in older pets - kidney changes, thyroid changes, arthritis, dental disease - tend to progress gradually and are far easier to manage when caught early. A twice-yearly visit roughly doubles the chances of catching a developing issue while it's still manageable, compared to waiting a full year between checkups.

Is my pet's slowing down just normal aging, or something to get checked?

It's worth getting checked either way. Arthritis and dental disease are two of the most commonly missed conditions in senior pets specifically because the signs - moving more slowly, hesitating on stairs, eating a little less - are easy to write off as "just getting older." A vet visit can tell you whether it's manageable normal aging or a treatable condition that's been quietly affecting your pet's comfort.

My pet seems confused or is having house-soiling accidents - should I be worried?

It's worth mentioning to your vet rather than assuming it's unavoidable. New disorientation, changed sleep patterns, or house-soiling in a previously reliable pet can have several explanations, some medical and treatable, so a vet visit is the way to sort out what's actually happening rather than guessing. Keeping simple notes on when you notice these changes gives your vet more to work with.

What home changes actually make a difference for a senior pet?

Traction and access are the two biggest categories. Non-slip mats on hard flooring reduce the strain and risk of a slip for joints that don't recover as quickly, and ramps or steps for furniture, cars, or a few stairs reduce the jumping and jarring impact that gets harder on aging joints. For cats, a lower-entry litter box matters more than owners often expect once jumping in and out becomes uncomfortable.

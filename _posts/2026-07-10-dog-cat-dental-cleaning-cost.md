A dental cleaning estimate is rarely the final bill. The reason isn't upselling: much of a pet's dental disease sits below the gumline, and nobody can see how many teeth are affected until the pet is under anesthesia and the X-rays are taken.

## What It Costs

National averages from a 2025–2026 study of veterinary practices across all 50 states, run for CareCredit by Synchrony:

| Item | Dog | Cat |
|---|---|---|
| Routine dental cleaning | $379 | $395 |
| Dental consultation | — | $74 |
| Dental X-rays | — | $133 |
| Tooth extraction (each) | — | $99 |
| Chemistry panel (often run before anesthesia) | $164 | $144 |

Prices vary widely by region and clinic, and complex surgical extractions cost more than simple ones. Our [Dental Cleaning Cost Calculator](/tools/dental-cleaning-cost-calculator.html) builds an estimate from the parts.

## What a Professional Cleaning Involves

Under general anesthesia, the vet or technician examines every tooth, probes the gum pockets, takes dental X-rays, scales tartar from above **and below** the gumline, and polishes. Teeth that X-rays and probing show to be badly diseased are extracted. The 2019 AAHA Dental Care Guidelines strongly recommend **full-mouth X-rays** for all dental patients, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual — they're how hidden problems such as tooth resorption in cats are found.

That's why the extraction line is usually the one that changes. Asking your vet to quote X-rays and likely extractions as separate items, based on what they can see at the awake exam, makes the final bill less of a surprise.

## Why Anesthesia Is Part of a Real Cleaning

The American Veterinary Dental College's position statement on scaling without anesthesia says:

- cleaning the tooth surfaces inside the gum pocket — the most important part, where periodontal disease is active — is **impossible in an awake dog or cat**,
- the inner surfaces of the teeth can't be examined, so disease is missed,
- removing visible tartar has little effect on health and is "purely cosmetic",
- anesthesia with a breathing tube prevents pain and protects the airway, and head movement in an awake pet risks injury.

The AVDC also notes that in the US and Canada, dental procedures by anyone other than a licensed veterinarian or a supervised, trained technician are unlicensed veterinary practice. "Anesthesia-free" cleanings may cost less, but they don't treat the disease.

## How Common Dental Disease Is

The Merck Veterinary Manual says **up to 80% of dogs and 70% of cats have some periodontal disease by age 2**, and the AAHA guidelines say most dogs and cats have some by age 3, often without their owners noticing. Toy breeds are at higher risk, and in cats the risk rises with age and body weight. Plaque left on the teeth for more than about two days mineralizes into tartar, which only professional scaling removes.

## Home Care That Slows the Next Cleaning

According to the Merck Veterinary Manual:

- **Brushing daily, or at least every other day,** appears to be the most effective home care. In most pets only the outer surfaces of the teeth need brushing.
- Pets that won't accept a brush can have their teeth **wiped with gauze** at least every second day.
- Firm, fibrous chews can help; **hard items such as bones and hooves can crack teeth**.
- Products that meet plaque and tartar standards are listed by the **Veterinary Oral Health Council** (vohc.org).

The ASPCA adds: use a pet toothpaste, never a human one. Without a change in home care, Merck notes, periodontal disease comes back after treatment.

## Does Pet Insurance Help?

Most accident-and-illness policies don't cover routine cleanings, treating them as preventive care. Some cover dental treatment that results from an accident or a diagnosed disease — an extraction for a broken tooth, for example — and some wellness plans include a cleaning allowance. Read the policy's dental section before assuming either way.

## Worked Example: From a Cleaning Quote to the Final Bill

For a cat, the Synchrony averages above can be added up the way a real visit usually goes. Here is a cat that turns out to need two teeth removed:

| Item | Average |
|---|---|
| Dental consultation | $74 |
| Chemistry panel before anesthesia | $144 |
| Routine dental cleaning | $395 |
| Dental X-rays | $133 |
| Two extractions ($99 each) | $198 |
| **Total** | **about $944** |

The cleaning on its own is well under half of that total. Without the extractions, the same visit comes to about $746; each extra tooth adds about $99 at the average price, and more for a complex surgical extraction. That gap is the reason to ask for the X-rays and possible extractions to be quoted as separate lines before the day.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does dog teeth cleaning cost?**
A routine cleaning averages $379 for a dog, according to a 2025–2026 national study of veterinary prices run for CareCredit by Synchrony. Pre-anesthetic bloodwork, dental X-rays and any extractions are often billed separately, which is why the final invoice is frequently higher than the estimate.

**How much does cat teeth cleaning cost?**
A routine cleaning averages $395 for a cat in Synchrony's study. The same study lists dental X-rays at $133 and extractions at $99 per tooth for cats — relevant because cats are prone to tooth resorption, which usually means the affected teeth have to come out.

**Is anesthesia-free dental cleaning a cheaper alternative?**
Not for your pet's dental health. The American Veterinary Dental College says cleaning under the gumline — where periodontal disease is active — is impossible in an awake dog or cat, and that scraping visible tartar is purely cosmetic and gives a false sense of accomplishment. The Merck Veterinary Manual likewise notes that cleaning an awake animal improves how the teeth look but not periodontal health.

**How often does my dog or cat need a dental cleaning?**
There's no fixed interval — it depends on what your vet finds at checkups. Periodontal disease starts early: the Merck Veterinary Manual says up to 80% of dogs and 70% of cats have some by age 2, and toy breeds are at higher risk. Daily or every-other-day brushing at home is the most effective way to slow it.

**Does pet insurance cover dental cleaning?**
Most accident-and-illness policies don't cover routine cleanings, which count as preventive care. Some cover dental treatment for an accident or diagnosed disease, such as an extraction for a broken tooth, and some wellness plans include a cleaning allowance. Check the policy wording for dental illness and exclusions.

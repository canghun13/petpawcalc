Cataract surgery is one of the few procedures in veterinary medicine that can give a blind dog its sight back, and one of the larger single bills an owner can face. It's done by veterinary ophthalmologists, usually with the same technique used in people: the cloudy lens is broken up and removed, and an artificial lens is put in its place.

The headline price is only part of the cost. Timing, whether one or both eyes are treated, the tests needed beforehand and the months of eye drops afterwards all change the total — and so does diabetes, which makes cataracts far more likely and far faster.

## What It Costs

Typical US price ranges at a veterinary ophthalmologist:

| Item | Typical cost |
|------|--------------|
| Surgery, overall range | About $2,700–$4,900 |
| One eye (published examples) | About $2,800–$3,800 |
| Both eyes (published examples) | About $3,800–$4,900 |
| Initial ophthalmology exam | Around $200, if not included |
| Electroretinogram (ERG), to check the retina works | About $250–$410 |
| Eye ultrasound | About $220–$800 |
| Pre-surgery bloodwork | Around $200 |
| Extra rechecks after surgery | About $75–$170 each |

What's included varies a lot. One practice in the published examples includes its diagnostic tests in the surgery price; another bills them separately; a third includes a set number of rechecks. Ask for a written estimate that lists the tests, surgery, hospital time, medications and rechecks.

**Both eyes usually cost less per eye.** In the published examples the second eye adds roughly $500–$1,200, not the full price again, because anesthesia, testing and follow-up are shared.

## Timing Changes the Outcome — and the Bill

Reported success rates are about **80–90%**, and around **85–90% in uncomplicated cases**. "Uncomplicated" is the important word. Surgery tends to go best:

- **before the cataract is mature.** As a cataract matures, the lens can swell and leak proteins into the eye, causing inflammation called lens-induced uveitis. Very early cataracts that don't affect vision are usually just monitored.
- **before uveitis has developed.** Inflammation raises the risk of complications after surgery.
- **when the retina still works.** If the retina is damaged, removing the cataract won't restore sight. That's what the ERG checks, and why it's done first.

Age alone doesn't rule a dog out, but other health problems may. A dog also has to tolerate several weeks of frequent eye drops and wearing a cone, so temperament is part of the decision.

## The Diabetes Link

Diabetes is one of the most common reasons dogs develop cataracts, and those cataracts tend to develop fast. In a study of **200 diabetic dogs (Beam et al., 1999)**, about **half had developed cataracts by 170 days** after diagnosis and **75% by 370 days**.

Two things follow from that:

1. **Ask for an ophthalmology referral early** if your dog has diabetes, so the eyes are checked before a cataract matures and causes inflammation.
2. **The diabetes has to be well controlled first.** Surgeons generally want stable blood sugar before operating, which can add time and rechecks to the plan.

## Recovery and Aftercare

After surgery, most dogs need:

- **eye drops four or more times a day** for several weeks to a month, then tapering. Some dogs need some eye medication for life.
- **a cone** to stop rubbing.
- **frequent rechecks** at first, then less often. Ophthalmologists generally want to see the dog several times in the first year and at least yearly after that.
- sometimes **oral pain or anti-inflammatory medication**.

Possible complications include inflammation, glaucoma (raised pressure in the eye), corneal ulcers, infection and retinal detachment. Those are why the follow-up schedule matters, and why it's worth budgeting for extra visits.

## What Skipping Surgery Still Costs

Choosing not to operate is a reasonable decision for some dogs, especially older dogs with other health problems. Many blind dogs adapt well, particularly in a familiar home. But it isn't free:

- A mature cataract can cause **ongoing inflammation**, which can lead to **glaucoma** or **retinal detachment** — both painful.
- Dogs that don't have surgery usually still need **anti-inflammatory eye drops** and **regular eye-pressure checks**, sometimes for life.

So the real comparison is surgery and its aftercare against years of drops and checks, not surgery against nothing.

## A Note on Insurance

Accident-and-illness policies often cover cataract surgery if the cataracts weren't diagnosed or showing signs before coverage began. Because cataracts often affect both eyes, some insurers treat them as a **bilateral condition**: a cataract noted in one eye before the policy started can exclude the other eye too. Hereditary cataracts are common in some breeds, so check how your policy treats hereditary conditions. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does cataract surgery cost for a dog?**
Usually about $2,700–$4,900 at a veterinary ophthalmologist. Published examples run about $2,800–$3,800 for one eye and $3,800–$4,900 for both. Pre-surgery tests are often billed separately — an electroretinogram (ERG) at roughly $250–$410 and an eye ultrasound at roughly $220–$800 — and post-surgery medications and extra rechecks may be extra too.

**Is it cheaper to do both eyes at once?**
Usually, per eye. In the published price examples, the second eye adds roughly $500–$1,200 rather than doubling the cost, because anesthesia, pre-surgery testing and much of the follow-up are shared. Whether both eyes should be done depends on whether both have cataracts and both retinas work.

**How successful is cataract surgery in dogs?**
Reported success rates are about 80–90%, and around 85–90% in uncomplicated cases. Results are best when the cataract is operated on before it becomes mature and before inflammation inside the eye develops. A working retina is essential, which is why an ERG is done first.

**What happens if I don't get my dog's cataracts removed?**
The dog may lose vision in that eye, and many blind dogs adapt well at home. But a mature cataract can leak lens proteins and cause ongoing inflammation (lens-induced uveitis), which can lead to glaucoma or retinal detachment. Dogs that don't have surgery usually still need anti-inflammatory eye drops and regular checks, sometimes for life.

**Why do diabetic dogs get cataracts?**
High blood sugar changes the lens chemistry, and diabetic cataracts tend to develop fast. In a study of 200 diabetic dogs (Beam et al., 1999), about half had developed cataracts by 170 days after diagnosis and 75% by 370 days. The diabetes has to be under good control before surgery, so it's worth asking for an ophthalmology referral early.

**Does pet insurance cover cataract surgery?**
Many accident-and-illness policies cover it if the cataracts weren't diagnosed or showing signs before coverage began. Because cataracts often affect both eyes, some insurers treat them as a bilateral condition, so a cataract in one eye before the policy started can exclude the other eye too. Some policies also limit hereditary conditions, so check the wording for your breed.

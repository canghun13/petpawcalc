Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the most common reasons an older cat ends up on long-term treatment. It can't be cured. It can usually be slowed, though, and what that costs depends almost entirely on **how far the disease has progressed when it's found**.

Vets describe that progress with the International Renal Interest Society (IRIS) staging system, from stage 1 (earliest) to stage 4 (most advanced). The stage decides what treatment your cat needs, and so what you'll pay.

## The Stages, in Plain Numbers

IRIS stages cats by two blood tests, measured when the cat is stable and well hydrated:

| IRIS stage | Creatinine (mg/dL) | SDMA (µg/dL) | What it usually means |
|------------|--------------------|--------------|------------------------|
| 1 | Under 1.6 | Under 18 | Kidney changes, but no build-up of waste in the blood yet |
| 2 | 1.6–2.8 | 18–25 | Mild; many cats seem normal or just drink more |
| 3 | 2.9–5.0 | 26–38 | Moderate; weight loss, poor appetite and nausea are common |
| 4 | Over 5.0 | Over 38 | Severe; cats often feel unwell and need intensive support |

If the two tests point to different stages, the IRIS guidance is to consider the cat's muscle mass, retest both in two to four weeks, and use the higher stage if they still disagree. Vets also substage by urine protein and blood pressure, because both change treatment.

## What Each Part Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | What it covers | Typical cost |
|------|----------------|--------------|
| Diagnosis | Blood panel with SDMA, urinalysis, blood pressure; urine culture if needed | $200–$500 |
| Rechecks | Bloodwork, urine and blood pressure, every 3–6 months | About $150–$300 each |
| Renal diet | Prescription kidney diet; canned costs more than dry | About $30–$125 a month |
| Subcutaneous fluids at home | Fluid bags, giving sets, needles | About $30–$100 a month |
| Subcutaneous fluids at the clinic | One session | About $25 or more each |
| Medications | Anti-nausea, phosphate binder, blood pressure, potassium, appetite stimulant | About $15–$150 a month, by stage |
| Hospital stay on IV fluids | Uremic crisis or sudden worsening | About $1,000–$3,000 per episode |
| Ultrasound (not always needed) | Kidney size and structure, stones | About $300–$500 |

## What a Year Costs at Each Stage

Adding up the ranges above, per stage:

| Stage | What's usually involved | Estimated yearly cost |
|-------|-------------------------|-----------------------|
| 1 | 2–4 rechecks a year | **$300–$1,200** |
| 2 | 2–4 rechecks, renal diet, sometimes blood pressure or potassium medication | **$660–$3,300** |
| 3 | 3–4 rechecks, renal diet, home fluids, several medications | **$1,830–$5,580** |
| 4 | All of the above, often more often, plus possible hospital stays | **$200–$500+ a month**, plus $1,000–$3,000 per crisis |

The jump from stage 2 to stage 3 is where most owners feel the cost change, because that is usually when home fluids and daily medications start. Anything that keeps a cat in stage 2 longer saves money as well as helping the cat.

## The Best-Value Spend: The Kidney Diet

A prescription renal diet looks like an expensive way to buy cat food. It is also the treatment with the strongest evidence behind it.

- **Ross et al. (2006)**, a randomized, masked two-year trial: of 22 cats fed a kidney diet, **none had a uremic crisis or kidney-related death**. Among 23 cats on adult maintenance food, six developed uremia and five died from the consequences of CKD.
- **Elliott et al. (2000)**, a non-randomized study: cats that ate a kidney diet had a median survival of 633 days versus 264 days for cats that stayed on their usual food. Cats chose their own diet in this study, so the result is less certain than the trial above.

The IRIS Board suggests considering a renal diet at stage 2 and recommends it at stages 3 and 4. Two practical points from IRIS make the money go further. **Switch slowly** — over at least three weeks, roughly 25% more kidney diet each week — because a cat that rejects the food gets none of the benefit. And **don't switch while the cat is sick or in hospital**: cats that are nauseated can form an aversion to whatever they are fed at the time. Treat nausea and poor appetite first.

Calories still matter more than the label. A cat that eats too little kidney diet and loses weight is worse off, so weigh your cat regularly and tell your vet if intake drops.

## High SDMA, Normal Creatinine: What It Means

This is one of the most common results to come back from senior bloodwork, and one of the least explained.

**Why the tests disagree.** Creatinine comes from muscle, so an older cat that has lost muscle can have a reassuringly normal creatinine even when the kidneys aren't working well. SDMA isn't affected by muscle mass, and it rises earlier. In one cat study, SDMA went above its reference range after about 40% of kidney function was lost.

**Why one result isn't a diagnosis.** SDMA isn't a stand-alone test. Dehydration can push it up, so a single high value needs repeating.

**What vets usually do next:**

1. **Repeat SDMA in about two weeks** to see whether it stays high.
2. **Check urine concentration** (urine specific gravity). Dilute urine alongside a persistently high SDMA points to reduced kidney function.
3. **Check urine protein and blood pressure**, because both are treatable and both speed kidney damage.

If SDMA stays above 14 µg/dL with dilute urine, that is consistent with **IRIS stage 1 CKD** even when creatinine is normal. At this stage the main step is closer monitoring, roughly every three months at first. Renal diets and other kidney-protecting treatments typically start at stage 2. Finding CKD at stage 1 is good news for the budget: it gives you the most time before the expensive stages.

## How Long Cats Live With CKD

Stage at diagnosis is the strongest predictor of survival. In a study of 211 cats with naturally occurring CKD (Boyd et al., 2008), median survival from diagnosis was:

- **Late stage 2:** about 1,151 days (just over three years)
- **Stage 3:** 778 days (about two years)
- **Stage 4:** 103 days

These are medians — half of cats lived longer — and they come from cats diagnosed before SDMA testing and today's treatment were routine. They show why early detection matters for both time and cost.

## Ways to Keep Costs Down

- **Learn to give subcutaneous fluids at home** if your vet prescribes them. Clinic sessions at roughly $25 or more each add up to hundreds a month for a cat needing fluids several times a week. Ask whether you can buy fluid bags and needles from a pharmacy or medical supplier with your vet's prescription.
- **Don't skip blood pressure checks.** High blood pressure is common in cats with CKD and can cause sudden blindness. Treating it is inexpensive; the complications aren't.
- **Ask about generic medications** and whether a human pharmacy can fill them.
- **Keep up with senior bloodwork.** Our [Cat Vet Visit Scheduler](/tools/cat-vet-visit-scheduler.html) shows when checkups are due, and [How Much Does a Cat Vet Visit Cost?](/blog/how-much-does-a-cat-vet-visit-cost/) covers what they cost.

Hyperthyroidism, which is also common in older cats, can mask kidney disease by increasing blood flow through the kidneys. If your cat is being treated for both, see [Cat Hyperthyroidism Treatment Cost](/blog/cat-hyperthyroidism-treatment-cost/) for why vets often start with a reversible treatment first.

## A Note on Insurance

Accident-and-illness pet insurance can cover kidney disease, including rechecks, medications and fluids under many policies, but only if it wasn't diagnosed or showing signs before coverage began. Because CKD is a disease of older cats, cover taken out when a cat is young is what usually pays for it. Check whether a policy covers prescription diets, as many exclude them.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to treat kidney disease in a cat?**
It depends mostly on the stage. Early (IRIS stage 1) disease usually means only rechecks, roughly $300–$1,200 a year.

Most cat weight advice is about obesity, and for good reason — it's common. But a cat that's getting thinner is often the more urgent problem, because unexplained weight loss in cats is frequently the first visible sign of an illness rather than simply "not eating enough."

This guide covers how vets judge whether a cat is too thin, why muscle loss matters as much as fat loss, and which signs mean a vet visit can't wait.

## The Body Condition Check

Vets use a 9-point body condition score (BCS), where **5 is ideal** and **1–4 are under ideal**. These are the descriptions for the underweight end of the WSAVA cat chart:

| Score | What you see and feel |
|---|---|
| 5 — ideal | Well-proportioned; waist visible behind the ribs; ribs can be felt with a slight fat covering; minimal belly fat pad |
| 4 | Ribs can be felt with minimal fat covering; noticeable waist; slight tuck at the belly; no belly fat pad |
| 3 | Ribs **easily** felt with minimal fat; backbones in the lower back obvious; obvious waist; minimal belly fat |
| 2 | Ribs **easily visible** on a shorthaired cat; lower backbones obvious; pronounced belly tuck; no fat to feel |
| 1 | Ribs visible; no fat to feel; severe belly tuck; lower backbones and hip bones easily felt |

To check at home:

- **Ribs:** Run your flat hands along the sides of the chest. You should feel the ribs under a thin layer of padding, not see them.
- **Lower back and hips:** Run a hand along the spine toward the tail. Sharp, obvious bumps and easily felt hip bones point to a score below ideal.
- **From above and the side:** Look for an exaggerated waist and a tucked-up belly.
- **Long-haired cats:** Feel rather than look. A thick coat can hide a lot of weight loss.

Thin isn't harmless. Kara Burns, a veterinary nutrition technician, notes in a review of unintentional weight loss in cats that older cats are more likely to be underweight than obese, and that lifespan decreased in cats with a body condition score below 5.

## Muscle Loss: Why a Cat Can Look Bony at a Normal Weight

Body condition measures fat. Muscle is scored separately, and a cat can lose muscle while its body condition still looks normal — or even while it's overweight. The WSAVA muscle condition score grades muscle as normal, mild, moderate or severe loss, felt over the spine, shoulder blades, skull and hip bones.

As described in a 2025 *dvm360* review by Mansum Yau, DVM:

- **Normal:** the bumps of the spine can't be felt, even with pressure.
- **Mild loss:** they can be felt slightly.
- **Moderate loss:** they stand out without pressure, and the shoulder blades and skull can be felt.
- **Marked loss:** the spine is severely prominent with visible dips beside it, and the shoulder blades and skull are prominent.

Muscle along the spine is usually the first to go. A cat whose face looks sharper, whose shoulder blades stick up, or whose back feels like a ridge is losing muscle, and that deserves the same attention as weight loss.

In older cats there are two broad reasons. **Sarcopenia** is age-related muscle loss in cats around 11 and older without disease. **Cachexia** is muscle loss driven by illness such as chronic kidney disease, cancer or heart failure, and in kidney disease it's a sign of a poorer outlook.

## Weigh, Don't Guess

Weight loss in cats is easy to miss because it's slow and the numbers are small. A 5 kg (11 lb) cat that loses 0.5 kg (1.1 lb) has lost a tenth of its body weight — the same proportion as 15 lb on a 150 lb person.

It also often starts long before a diagnosis:

- In the review by Burns, cats that died of cancer, chronic kidney disease or hyperthyroidism had been losing weight for about **2.5 years** before death.
- The *dvm360* review cites a study in which cats lost weight about **3 years before** they were diagnosed with chronic kidney disease.

That's why a trend matters more than a single number. Weigh your cat at home every month — a baby scale works well, or weigh yourself holding the cat and subtract your own weight — and write it down. Bring the record to checkups, along with your cat's body condition score.

## Common Medical Causes

| Cause | Typical pattern | More on cost |
|---|---|---|
| Hyperthyroidism | Weight loss despite a normal or bigger appetite; common in cats 8+ years old | [Treatment costs](/blog/cat-hyperthyroidism-treatment-cost/) |
| Chronic kidney disease | Weight and muscle loss, more drinking and urinating, often gradual | [Costs by stage](/blog/cat-kidney-disease-cost-by-stage/) |
| Diabetes | Weight loss with a bigger appetite, more thirst and urination | [Treatment costs](/blog/cat-diabetes-treatment-cost/) |
| Dental disease | Weight loss with a smaller appetite, dropping food, bad breath | [Dental cleaning costs](/blog/dog-cat-dental-cleaning-cost/) |
| Intestinal parasites | Weight loss with a normal or bigger appetite | — |
| Inflammatory bowel disease or intestinal lymphoma | Weight loss, sometimes vomiting or diarrhea, often gradual | — |

Two patterns are worth flagging straight away:

- **Losing weight while eating normally or more than usual** — classic for hyperthyroidism, and also seen with diabetes.
- **Losing weight with a smaller appetite** — worth checking for dental pain, kidney disease or gut disease.

With so many common causes being serious, unexplained weight loss in a cat is one of the clearer cases where bloodwork should come before a change of food, not after. Stress, competition at the food bowl in a multi-cat home, and some medications can also reduce how much a cat eats.

## When It's Urgent

Book a same-day visit, rather than a routine appointment, if your cat:

- **has stopped eating**, or is eating very little,
- has **yellow-tinged gums or ears**,
- is **vomiting repeatedly**, weak, or hiding,
- is **losing weight quickly**, or is very thin with obvious muscle loss over the shoulders and skull.

The reason for the hurry is **hepatic lipidosis** (fatty liver disease). According to Cornell's Feline Health Center, it is almost always preceded by a cat refusing food, seems much more common in overweight cats, and in over 90% of cases follows another illness such as diabetes, hyperthyroidism, pancreatitis or kidney disease. It's likely to be fatal without prompt treatment, which usually means feeding through a tube — often continued at home for about a month. Cats that survive an episode rarely have another.

## How to Help a Healthy but Underweight Cat Gain Weight

Once a medical cause has been ruled out or is being treated:

1. **Offer smaller, more frequent meals** — 3–4 small meals often work better than 1–2 large ones for an appetite that needs coaxing.
2. **Ask about a calorie-dense or recovery food**, with your vet's guidance.
3. **Warm wet food slightly** to boost its smell — cats rely heavily on scent to decide whether to eat.
4. **Feed a cat that's being crowded out separately**, so it can eat without competition.
5. **Avoid a sudden large jump in portion size**, which can cause stomach upset without speeding up healthy weight gain.
6. **Weigh every week or two** while you're trying to put weight on, so you know whether it's working.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How can I tell if my cat is underweight?**
Use your hands as well as your eyes. On the WSAVA body condition chart, an underweight cat has ribs that are easy to feel with little or no fat over them, an obvious waist behind the ribs, and backbones in the lower back that are easy to feel; in very thin cats the ribs are visible on a shorthaired cat and there's no fat to feel at all. A healthy cat's ribs can be felt under a slight layer of fat but not seen.

**Why does my cat's face, spine or shoulders look bony?**
That's usually muscle loss rather than fat loss. Muscle is lost first along the spine, and as it gets worse, the shoulder blades and skull become easy to feel and then visibly prominent. Muscle loss can happen even in a cat whose weight looks normal, and in older cats it's often an early sign of illness such as kidney disease, so it's worth a vet check.

**Why is my cat losing weight even though they're eating normally?**
Weight loss with a normal or bigger appetite is a classic pattern of hyperthyroidism, especially in cats over about 8–10 years old, and is also seen with diabetes and intestinal parasites. It should prompt a vet visit and bloodwork rather than a wait-and-see approach.

**What are the most common medical causes of an underweight cat?**
In middle-aged and older cats, hyperthyroidism and chronic kidney disease are the most common, followed by diabetes, dental disease that makes eating painful, intestinal parasites, and inflammatory bowel disease or intestinal lymphoma. Because so many of the common causes are serious, unexplained weight loss in a cat calls for bloodwork before a change of food.

**When is an underweight cat an emergency?**
Get a same-day vet visit if your cat has stopped eating, has yellow-tinged gums or ears, is vomiting repeatedly, is weak or hiding, or is losing weight quickly. Cats that stop eating can develop hepatic lipidosis, a serious liver condition that is likely to be fatal without prompt treatment.

**How do I help my underweight cat gain weight safely?**
Once your vet has ruled out or is treating an underlying cause, offer several small meals a day instead of one or two large ones, ask your vet about a calorie-dense or recovery food, and warm wet food slightly to make it smell stronger. Avoid a sudden large jump in portion size, and weigh your cat every week or two so you can see whether it's working.

**How do I check a long-haired cat for weight loss?**
Feel rather than look, because a long coat hides a lot. Run your palms along the ribs, spine and hips: on a healthy cat these are easy to feel under a thin layer of cover, while on an underweight cat they feel sharp and bare. Regular weighing is especially useful for long-haired breeds such as Maine Coons, Persians and Ragdolls.

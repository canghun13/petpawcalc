Lymphoma is one of the most common cancers in dogs. The most common form usually shows up as firm, painless swellings of the lymph nodes, such as under the jaw or behind the knees.

It's also one of the most treatable cancers in dogs, in the sense that most dogs respond well to chemotherapy. But treatment rarely cures it, so the decision owners face is about **how much good time each option buys, and what it costs**. This guide puts the options side by side.

## The Options at a Glance

| Option | Typical cost | Remission | Expected survival |
|---|---|---|---|
| Multi-drug chemotherapy (CHOP), about 16 weeks | About $3,500–$10,000 | 80–90% complete remission | Median about 12–16 months; less for T-cell |
| Single-drug doxorubicin, 6 doses every 2–3 weeks | Less than CHOP | Remission typically lasts 4–6 months | Shorter than CHOP |
| Prednisone alone | Very little — about $0.15–$0.30 per 10 mg tablet | Short-lived improvement | Median about 1–2 months |
| No treatment | — | — | About 4–6 weeks on average |

Outcome figures are from Iowa State University's oncology service, Penn Vet and VCA Hospitals; cost figures from GoodRx and ImpriMed (details below). Every dog is different, and the type of lymphoma (B-cell or T-cell) and stage change the outlook.

## What Chemotherapy Costs

- **Per treatment:** chemotherapy typically runs about **$150–$600 per dose**, depending on the drug, the dog's size and the clinic.
- **A full course:** about **$1,000–$10,000**, with a typical course around **$5,000**, according to ImpriMed's 2026 summary.
- **CHOP specifically:** GoodRx estimated **$3,500–$10,000** for a CHOP protocol.

CHOP combines several drugs — typically vincristine, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin and prednisone, sometimes with L-asparaginase — given **weekly over about 16 weeks**. The weekly visits, blood counts before each dose, and the drugs themselves are what make it the most expensive option, and the dog's size matters because doses are based on it.

**Prednisone** on its own is very cheap — ImpriMed lists 10 mg tablets at about $0.15–$0.30 — but it's a palliative choice rather than a treatment that controls the cancer for long.

## The Cost of Finding Out

Before treatment, the diagnosis and staging tests add up:

| Test | Why | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Needle aspirate or biopsy of a lymph node | Confirms lymphoma | Aspirate about $25–$200; biopsy more |
| Bloodwork and urine test | Overall health and whether chemo is safe | About $160–$490 with the exam |
| Chest X-rays and abdominal ultrasound | Shows how far it has spread (staging) | Commonly $400–$900 or more with imaging |
| B-cell or T-cell testing | Changes the expected response and drug choice | Ask for a quote |

Not every owner needs every test. If you already know you won't pursue chemotherapy, ask your vet which tests would actually change what you do.

## What Each Option Buys

**CHOP** is the gold standard for the common multicentric form. According to Penn Vet and Iowa State, **80–90% of dogs go into complete remission**, and Iowa State gives a **median survival of 12–16 months**, lower for T-cell lymphoma. Penn Vet and VCA describe average survival with chemotherapy as about a year.

**Single-drug doxorubicin** is given as 6 doses every 2–3 weeks; Iowa State says remission typically lasts **4–6 months**. It means fewer visits and lower cost, with a shorter expected benefit.

**Lomustine with prednisone**, an oral option, produced a response in about half of dogs for roughly 2–3 months in one study cited by Iowa State; it's used mainly as a rescue treatment.

**Prednisone alone** often shrinks the lymph nodes and improves appetite for a while. Iowa State gives a **median survival of about 1–2 months**; VCA puts average survival for untreated dogs or dogs on prednisone alone at **about 4–6 weeks**. If your dog has already been started on prednisone and you're considering chemotherapy, make sure the oncologist knows.

## Remission Isn't a Cure

Penn Vet is clear that relatively few dogs are cured. Most dogs in remission eventually relapse, and many respond again to a **rescue protocol**, though later remissions tend to be shorter. Budgeting for lymphoma means budgeting for the first protocol and, often, a second one.

Rescue options include:

- **Tanovea (rabacfosadine)** — an IV infusion every 3 weeks for up to 5 treatments, conditionally approved by the FDA in 2016 and fully approved in 2021. GoodRx reported about **$600–$700 per treatment** in 2021; current prices may differ. Oncologists Christine Mullin and Craig Clifford describe it as an excellent first rescue drug, particularly for B-cell lymphoma, and note that about **4%** of dogs developed pulmonary fibrosis (lung scarring) as a late complication.
- **Laverdia-CA1 (verdinexor)** — an oral tablet. In its phase 2 study, 55% of dogs had a clinical benefit, lasting a median of 71 days.

## What Chemotherapy Is Like for Dogs

According to Penn Vet, most side effects — **mild nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, poor appetite, tiredness and lower white blood cell counts** — are mild and manageable at home, and **severe reactions needing hospital care are uncommon**. VCA notes that dogs rarely lose their hair, though Penn Vet mentions hair loss in some breeds.

## Signs to Watch For

- **firm, painless lumps** under the jaw, in front of the shoulders or behind the knees,
- reduced appetite, weight loss or tiredness,
- breathing changes if the chest is involved.

Lymphoma most often affects middle-aged and older dogs, and Penn Vet notes that **Boxers, German Shepherds and Golden Retrievers** may be more susceptible. Any new lump deserves a needle test rather than a wait — see [Dog Lump Removal Cost](/blog/dog-lump-removal-cost/).

## Ways to Manage the Cost

- **Ask for a written estimate for each protocol**, including blood counts and rechecks, not just the drugs.
- **Ask whether a single-drug or oral protocol fits your budget and goals** — they cost less and still buy time.
- **Ask which staging tests will change the plan** before running all of them.
- **Check insurance coverage early.** Accident-and-illness policies usually cover cancer diagnosis and chemotherapy if signs weren't noted before coverage began; many have annual limits that a CHOP protocol can reach. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does lymphoma treatment cost for a dog?**
Multi-drug CHOP chemotherapy usually costs about $3,500–$10,000 over the course, and chemotherapy in general runs about $150–$600 per dose, with a typical full course of around $5,000. Single-drug options cost less, and prednisone alone costs very little — roughly 15 to 30 cents per 10 mg tablet. Diagnosis and staging tests are extra.

**How long will my dog live with lymphoma?**
It depends mainly on treatment. With CHOP chemotherapy, 80–90% of dogs go into complete remission and median survival is about 12–16 months, less for T-cell lymphoma. With prednisone alone, median survival is about 1–2 months, and VCA puts average survival without treatment or with prednisone alone at about 4–6 weeks.

**Is chemotherapy hard on dogs?**
For most dogs, side effects are mild. Most of them — mild vomiting, diarrhea, poor appetite, tiredness and lower white blood cell counts — are manageable at home, and severe reactions needing hospital care are uncommon. Most dogs don't lose their coat, though some breeds can lose hair.

**Can lymphoma in dogs be cured?**
Rarely. Chemotherapy usually puts lymphoma into remission, meaning the signs disappear, but most dogs eventually relapse. Many respond again to a second, 'rescue' protocol, though later remissions tend to be shorter. Treatment is about good-quality time rather than a cure.

**What is Tanovea, and how much does it cost?**
Tanovea (rabacfosadine) is an FDA-approved lymphoma drug given as a 30-minute IV infusion every 3 weeks, for up to 5 treatments. GoodRx reported about $600–$700 per treatment, or $3,000–$3,500 for five, in 2021; current prices may differ. It's often used as a rescue drug, especially for B-cell lymphoma, and about 4% of dogs developed lung scarring as a late complication.

**What are the first signs of lymphoma in dogs?**
The most common form causes firm, painless swelling of the lymph nodes — under the jaw, in front of the shoulders and behind the knees. Some dogs also lose appetite, lose weight, drink more or seem tired. Lymphoma most often affects middle-aged and older dogs, and Boxers, German Shepherds and Golden Retrievers may be more susceptible.

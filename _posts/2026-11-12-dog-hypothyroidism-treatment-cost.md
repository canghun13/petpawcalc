Hypothyroidism is one of the most common hormonal diseases in dogs. The thyroid gland stops making enough hormone, usually because it has shrunk or been damaged by the immune system, and the dog's metabolism slows down.

It's also one of the cheapest long-term conditions to treat. The daily pill costs pennies. What costs money — and what can cost a lot more if it's done badly — is the testing: first to be sure the dog really has it, then to get the dose right.

## What It Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | What it involves | Typical cost |
|------|------------------|--------------|
| Exam and basic bloodwork | Exam, blood count, chemistry, urine test | About $160–$490 |
| Total T4 screen | Single thyroid hormone test | Around $50 in lab fees |
| Fuller thyroid panel | Free T4, TSH, sometimes thyroid antibodies | Up to about $150 |
| Levothyroxine tablets | Daily, dosed by weight | About $0.05 (0.1 mg) to $0.12 (0.6 mg) per tablet at one large online pet pharmacy |
| Recheck | Exam plus a T4 taken 4–6 hours after the pill | Roughly $100–$150 |

For comparison, Texas A&M's veterinary diagnostic lab charges vets **$101** for its comprehensive canine thyroid panel (T3, T4, TSH, free T4 by dialysis and thyroglobulin antibodies). Your clinic's price includes collecting the sample and its own fees.

## What a Year Costs

Adding up the ranges above:

| | First year | Each year after |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis | $210–$640 | — |
| Rechecks | 2–4 while the dose is set: $200–$600 | 1–2 a year: $100–$300 |
| Levothyroxine | About $60–$120 at online-pharmacy prices | About $60–$120 |
| **Total** | **About $500–$1,400** | **About $150–$450** |

A 30 kg (66 lb) dog on 0.6 mg twice a day needs two 0.6 mg tablets a day — about $7 a month at the online price above. Prices vary between pharmacies, so it's worth asking whether you can fill the prescription elsewhere.

## The Expensive Mistake: Treating a Dog That Doesn't Have It

Because the pills are cheap and the signs are vague, it's easy to start a dog on thyroid medication that it doesn't need — and then pay for pills and rechecks for the rest of its life. A review in *Today's Veterinary Practice* is blunt: **the diagnosis should never be based on a low T4 alone.**

A low T4 can be caused by things other than thyroid disease:

- **Other illness.** Sick dogs often have low thyroid hormone levels without thyroid disease, so dogs that are unwell for another reason shouldn't be tested.
- **Medications**, including phenobarbital, high-dose prednisone or aspirin, trimethoprim-sulfa antibiotics and clomipramine. If your dog takes phenobarbital for seizures, see [Dog Seizure Medication Cost](/blog/dog-seizure-medication-cost/).
- **Breed.** Sighthounds such as Greyhounds and Salukis normally have lower thyroid levels, so standard reference ranges don't apply. The 2023 AAHA endocrine guidelines note the same for some working dogs.

The same review recommends testing only when the history and exam strongly suggest hypothyroidism — not just because a dog is overweight or because T4 appeared on a routine panel. A **normal total T4 effectively rules it out**, since the test catches 89–100% of hypothyroid dogs. A low one needs confirming with **free T4 and/or TSH**. A high TSH supports the diagnosis, but **13–38% of hypothyroid dogs have a normal TSH**, so a normal TSH alone doesn't rule it out.

If your vet suggests starting levothyroxine, it's reasonable to ask which signs and results point to it, and whether a confirmatory test has been done.

## Treatment and Monitoring

Treatment is **levothyroxine**, a synthetic thyroid hormone, given for life. Thyro-Tabs Canine is FDA-approved for dogs (NADA 141-448), with a labeled starting dose of 0.1 mg per 10 lb (0.022 mg/kg) once a day or split into two doses. The 2023 AAHA guidelines suggest starting at **0.02 mg/kg twice daily**, calculated from lean body weight in overweight dogs.

Monitoring follows a simple pattern:

1. **About 4 weeks after starting**, a total T4 is taken **4–6 hours after the pill**, when levels peak.
2. The dose is adjusted about monthly until that result sits in the upper half of the reference range or slightly above.
3. Once stable, T4 is checked **every 6–12 months**.

The AAHA guidelines note that confirming TSH has returned to normal isn't required and adds cost. Some dogs can later move to once-daily dosing after a pre-pill test.

**How quickly it works:** lethargy often improves within a few weeks, and most signs within 4–6 weeks. Skin and coat changes can take months.

Giving the pill consistently — either always with food or always without — matters, because food changes absorption. Missed doses are a common reason treatment seems not to work.

## The Signs to Watch For

- lethargy, reduced activity and weight gain without eating more
- hair loss on both sides of the body that doesn't itch
- hair that doesn't grow back after clipping, a dull or scaly coat
- darkened skin
- repeated skin or ear infections

It typically affects middle-aged dogs. Because these signs overlap with normal aging, obesity and other hormonal diseases such as [Cushing's](/blog/dog-cushings-disease-treatment-cost/), a careful workup is worth the cost. If weight gain is the main concern, our [guide to telling whether a dog is overweight](/blog/how-to-tell-if-dog-is-overweight/) is a good first step.

## A Note on Insurance

Accident-and-illness policies usually cover hypothyroidism diagnosis, medication and rechecks if signs didn't appear before the policy started. Because the yearly cost is modest, insurance matters less here than for other chronic conditions — but a dog with hypothyroidism on its records before enrollment will usually have it excluded.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to treat hypothyroidism in a dog?**
Roughly $500–$1,400 in the first year, including the diagnosis and the rechecks while the dose is set, and about $150–$450 a year after that. The medication itself is cheap: at one large online pet pharmacy, levothyroxine tablets cost about $0.05 (0.1 mg) to $0.12 (0.6 mg) each, which is under $10 a month for many dogs. Prices at a clinic pharmacy may be higher.

**How much is a thyroid test for a dog?**
A basic total T4 screen typically costs around $50 in lab fees, and a fuller thyroid panel with free T4 and other tests up to about $150, usually added to other bloodwork and an exam. For comparison, one university diagnostic lab charges veterinarians $101 for its comprehensive canine thyroid panel; what your clinic charges includes its own fees.

**Can a dog be misdiagnosed with hypothyroidism?**
Yes. The signs are vague, which can lead to overdiagnosis, and many things lower a dog's T4 without thyroid disease, including other illness and drugs such as phenobarbital, high-dose prednisone and some antibiotics. Sighthounds such as Greyhounds normally have lower T4. Vets are advised never to diagnose hypothyroidism from a low T4 alone, and to confirm it with free T4 and/or TSH.

**How long does it take for thyroid medication to work in dogs?**
Lethargy often improves within a few weeks, and most signs improve within 4–6 weeks. Skin and coat changes can take months. The first blood recheck is usually about 4 weeks after starting, with a sample taken 4–6 hours after the pill.

**Is hypothyroidism in dogs lifelong?**
In almost all dogs, yes. Primary hypothyroidism, the common form, is caused by the thyroid gland shrinking or being destroyed by the immune system, so the hormone has to be replaced for life. That's why a confident diagnosis before starting matters so much.

**What are the signs of hypothyroidism in dogs?**
The most common are lethargy, weight gain and reduced activity. Skin and coat changes are also common: hair loss on both sides of the body that doesn't itch, hair that doesn't grow back after clipping, a dull coat, scaling, darkened skin, and repeated skin or ear infections. It typically affects middle-aged dogs.

Librela (bedinvetmab) is a once-a-month injection for arthritis pain in dogs, and it has quickly become one of the most discussed treatments in veterinary medicine — partly because many owners see a big difference, and partly because of its price and the safety reports that followed its launch.

If your vet has suggested it, the two practical questions are what it will cost over a year and how it compares with the daily pills most arthritic dogs take. This guide covers both, along with what the trials showed and what changed on the label.

## What Librela Is

Librela is a **monoclonal antibody** that blocks nerve growth factor (NGF), a protein involved in sending osteoarthritis pain signals. It is given as an injection under the skin once a month, usually at the clinic. The FDA approved it on **May 5, 2023**.

It is not an NSAID, which is why it's often considered for dogs that can't take NSAIDs or don't do well on them. The cat version, frunevetmab, is sold as Solensia.

## What It Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | Typical cost |
|------|--------------|
| Librela injection, small dog | About $75 a month |
| Librela injection, large dog | About $150–$300+ a month |
| Exam or visit fee, if charged per injection | About $50–$100 |
| Baseline bloodwork (chemistry panel) | About $104–$186 |
| X-rays to confirm arthritis, if needed | About $149–$331 |

The dose is based on body weight, so bigger dogs need a larger vial or more than one vial. That is why the price range is so wide: across a year, Librela usually comes to roughly **$900–$3,600**, plus any per-visit fees.

Ask your clinic two questions up front: whether the injection price changes as your dog's weight changes, and whether each monthly injection needs a full exam or just a technician appointment. The second answer can change the yearly total by several hundred dollars.

## Librela vs a Daily NSAID

| | Librela | Daily NSAID (e.g. carprofen) |
|---|---|---|
| How it's given | Injection once a month, usually at the clinic | Pill once or twice a day at home |
| Typical cost | About $75–$300+ a month by weight | Generic carprofen around 50 cents a tablet at some pet pharmacies — often tens of dollars a month |
| Monitoring | Vet may want baseline bloodwork | Bloodwork to check liver and kidney values, often before starting and periodically |
| Main reason to choose it | Dogs that can't take NSAIDs, or owners who struggle with daily pills | Lower cost and a long track record |

For a dog that does well on an inexpensive NSAID, Librela is usually the more expensive choice. The calculation changes for dogs with kidney or liver concerns, a history of stomach upset on NSAIDs, or dogs that refuse pills. Some dogs end up on a combination plan; that's a decision for your vet, who will weigh it against your dog's other conditions.

## What the Trials Showed

The main European field study (**Corral et al., 2021**, *Veterinary Anaesthesia and Analgesia*) randomized 287 dogs with osteoarthritis to monthly Librela or placebo. Owners scored their dog's pain using the Canine Brief Pain Index.

- After the first month, **43.5% of dogs on Librela** met the study's improvement target, compared with **16.9% on placebo**.
- Librela stayed ahead at every later time point over three months, while placebo success stayed below 25%.

In plain terms: roughly four in ten dogs improved clearly within a month, against about one in six on placebo. It helps a substantial share of dogs, but not every dog. Vets typically judge it over the first few injections rather than after a single dose.

## The Safety Reports and the 2025 Label Change

After launch, owners and vets reported adverse events, and on **December 16, 2024** the FDA published a letter to veterinarians. It described reports of ataxia (wobbliness), seizures, other neurologic signs such as weakness and urinary incontinence, and increased thirst and urination. Some cases ended in death, including euthanasia.

In early 2025, the US label was updated:

- A **post-approval experience** section now lists the adverse events reported since launch.
- Vets are advised to give owners a **client information sheet** and **discuss possible side effects before each injection**.
- The contraindications didn't change: not for dogs that are pregnant, breeding or lactating, or sensitive to bedinvetmab. It hasn't been studied in dogs under 12 months.

Zoetis reports post-approval adverse events at a rate of about **1 to 10 per 10,000 doses** distributed worldwide, and says no single category exceeded 10 per 10,000. Because reporting is voluntary, that figure is likely an undercount. A report also doesn't prove the drug caused the problem, especially in older dogs that often have other conditions.

What this means in practice: watch your dog closely in the days after each injection. Tell your vet promptly about any wobbliness, weakness, a seizure, new accidents in the house, or drinking much more than usual, and report it to Zoetis or the FDA.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does Librela cost per month?**
Usually about $75–$300 or more per injection, given once a month, so roughly $900–$3,600 a year. The dose is based on body weight, so a small dog sits at the low end and a large or giant dog at the high end. Some clinics also charge an exam or technician-visit fee for each injection, and many want baseline bloodwork before starting.

**Is Librela more expensive than carprofen?**
Usually, yes. Generic carprofen, a common daily NSAID, sells for roughly 50 cents a tablet at some pet pharmacies, which for many dogs works out to tens of dollars a month plus monitoring bloodwork. Librela typically costs several times that. The price gap matters less for dogs that can't take NSAIDs, or for owners who find daily pills hard to keep up.

**How well does Librela work?**
In the main European field trial (Corral et al., 2021; 287 dogs), 43.5% of dogs on Librela met the owner-scored pain improvement target after the first month, compared with 16.9% on placebo, and Librela stayed ahead at every later check. That means it helps a substantial share of dogs, but not every dog, which is why vets judge it over the first few monthly injections.

**What side effects were added to the Librela label?**
After the FDA's December 2024 letter to veterinarians, the US label was updated in early 2025 with a post-approval experience section. It lists adverse events reported since launch, including ataxia (wobbliness), seizures, other neurologic signs, urinary incontinence, increased thirst and urination, and death, including euthanasia, in some cases. Zoetis reports post-approval adverse events at a rate of about 1 to 10 per 10,000 doses, though voluntary reporting makes that figure imperfect.

**Which dogs shouldn't get Librela?**
The label says not to use it in dogs that are pregnant, breeding or lactating, or that are sensitive to bedinvetmab, and it hasn't been studied in dogs under 12 months. Many vets also want baseline bloodwork first, because Librela can raise blood urea nitrogen (BUN). Ask your vet to go through the client information sheet with you before the first injection, as the updated label advises.

**Does pet insurance cover Librela?**
Many accident-and-illness policies cover prescription medications for arthritis, including injections, as long as the arthritis wasn't diagnosed or showing signs before the policy started. Because arthritis is common in older dogs, cover bought when a dog is young is what usually pays for it. Check whether your policy pays for the exam fee charged with each injection.

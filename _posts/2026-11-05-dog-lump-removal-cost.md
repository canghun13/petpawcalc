Finding a lump on your dog is one of the most common reasons for an unplanned vet visit. Most lumps turn out to be harmless fatty growths. Some don't, and the price of finding out is much lower than the price of waiting.

This guide covers what the tests and removal typically cost, why the inexpensive needle test usually comes first, and why the size of a lump when it's removed has such a big effect on the bill.

## What It Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | What it involves | Typical cost |
|------|------------------|--------------|
| Fine needle aspirate (FNA) | Cells drawn from the lump with a small needle, usually without sedation | About $25–$200, depending on whether the lab fee is included |
| Exam plus aspirate and cytology | The visit and the cell analysis together | Often about $30–$150 at many clinics |
| Simple lipoma removal | Fatty lump removed under anesthesia | About $250–$700 |
| Large or deep lipoma | More surgery time | Up to about $1,000 per growth |
| Infiltrative lipoma | Fatty tumor growing into muscle | About $1,000–$1,800 |
| Mast cell tumor treatment | Surgery, lab grading, sometimes radiation or chemotherapy | About $500–$8,000 (average quote about $5,000) |

Anesthesia, pre-surgery bloodwork and the lab analysis of the removed tissue (histopathology) are often billed separately, so ask for an estimate that includes them.

## Why the Needle Test Comes First

A vet can't tell what a lump is by looking at it or feeling it. A harmless lipoma and a mast cell tumor — one of the most common skin cancers in dogs — can feel much the same.

A **fine needle aspirate** is quick, usually needs no sedation, and is one of the cheapest tests in veterinary medicine. It answers the question that decides everything else:

- **If it's a simple lipoma**, many dogs never need surgery. The lump can be measured and monitored.
- **If it's a mast cell tumor**, the surgeon can plan the right operation from the start, with enough healthy tissue around it, rather than removing it as a "simple lump" and finding out afterwards.

According to the American College of Veterinary Surgeons, a needle aspirate can diagnose mast cell tumors accurately in almost all cases. Mast cell tumors make up about **14–21% of all skin tumors** diagnosed in dogs, and Boxers and Boston Terriers account for roughly half of cases.

## "See Something, Do Something"

Veterinary oncologists promote a simple rule for owners and vets:

> **See something:** a lump the size of a pea (about 1 cm) that has been there for a month.
> **Do something:** have it checked with a needle aspirate or biopsy.

The reasoning is about both outcomes and cost. Smaller tumors are easier to remove, and most skin and under-the-skin tumors can be cured if caught early. A tumor left to grow may need a much bigger operation, sometimes followed by radiation or chemotherapy, and can become too large to remove at all.

The practical habit: when you find a lump, measure it, note the date and location, and book a check if it's pea-sized or bigger and still there a month later.

## Mast Cell Tumors: What Changes the Cost

Most of the wide $500–$8,000 range for mast cell tumors comes down to four things:

- **Size and location.** Surgeons now often use **proportional margins** — healthy tissue around the tumor roughly equal to its width, up to 2 cm. In one study, 95% of tumors were completely removed that way. A small tumor on the side of the body is a much smaller operation than a large one on a leg or face.
- **Grade.** The grade can only be determined from the tissue after removal. **Low-grade** tumors rarely spread and are often cured by surgery alone. **High-grade** tumors are different: without further treatment, most dogs die of the disease within a year, and chemotherapy can extend survival to about two to three years.
- **Staging.** Checking the nearby lymph node, and sometimes the spleen and liver, adds cost but changes the treatment plan.
- **Extra treatment.** Radiation is commonly used after incomplete removal, and chemotherapy for high-grade or spreading tumors.

There's also a non-surgical option for some tumors. **Tigilanol tiglate (Stelfonta)** is injected into the tumor and destroys it, leaving a wound that heals over about 4 to 6 weeks. It's labeled for tumors that haven't spread; in one study, about 75% of low-grade tumors disappeared completely after one treatment, rising to nearly 90% after a second.

## Fatty Lumps: When Removal Is Worth It

Once a needle test confirms a simple lipoma, many vets recommend watching rather than removing it. Removal makes more sense when the lump:

- is growing quickly,
- is in an awkward place, such as an armpit or near a joint, where it can interfere with movement,
- is bothering the dog, or
- turns out to be infiltrative, growing into muscle.

Removing a fatty lump while it's small is usually cheaper and easier than removing it once it's large, so "watch it" works best with regular measurements.

## Ways to Keep the Bill Down

- **Get the aspirate first** instead of removing lumps blindly.
- **Measure and photograph lumps** monthly so you and your vet can see real changes.
- **Combine procedures.** If your dog already needs anesthesia, for example for a [dental cleaning](/blog/dog-cat-dental-cleaning-cost/), ask whether a lump can be removed at the same time.
- **Always send removed tissue to the lab.** Skipping histopathology saves money only if the lump was harmless; if it wasn't, you need the grade to know what comes next.

## A Note on Insurance

Accident-and-illness policies usually cover diagnosis and treatment of lumps and cancer, including aspirates, surgery and lab work, if the lump wasn't noted before coverage began. A lump mentioned in your dog's records before the policy started may be excluded, so it's worth insuring before problems appear. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to remove a lump from a dog?**
It depends on what the lump is. A simple fatty lump (lipoma) usually costs about $250–$700 to remove, an infiltrative lipoma that grows into muscle about $1,000–$1,800, and mast cell tumor treatment ranges from about $500 to $8,000 depending on size, location and whether more treatment is needed. Pre-surgery bloodwork and lab analysis of the removed tissue are often billed separately.

**How much is a fine needle aspirate for a dog?**
Usually about $25–$200, depending on the clinic and whether the lab fee for examining the cells is included. A needle aspirate takes cells from the lump with a small needle, usually without sedation, and is often the cheapest way to find out what a lump is before deciding whether to remove it.

**When should a lump on my dog be checked?**
A widely used rule from veterinary oncologists is 'See something, do something': if a lump is the size of a pea (about 1 cm) and has been there for a month, have it checked with a needle aspirate or biopsy. Smaller lumps are easier and cheaper to remove, and most skin tumors can be cured if they're caught early.

**Can a vet tell what a lump is just by looking at it?**
No. Fatty lumps and mast cell tumors can look and feel similar. A needle aspirate can diagnose mast cell tumors accurately in almost all cases, according to the American College of Veterinary Surgeons, and a lab analysis of the tissue after removal is needed to grade a tumor.

**Should a fatty lump on a dog be removed?**
Not always. Once a needle test confirms a simple lipoma, many vets recommend monitoring it rather than removing it, unless it's growing quickly, in an awkward place such as an armpit, or bothering the dog. Measuring it every month or so is a cheap way to keep track.

**How serious is a mast cell tumor in a dog?**

Diabetes is one of the most common hormonal diseases in dogs, usually diagnosed in middle age when an owner notices a dog drinking and urinating much more than usual, often while losing weight despite a good appetite.

It's very manageable, but it's a long-term commitment, and the costs are different from diabetes in cats. In September 2026, the American Animal Hospital Association (AAHA) published its first diabetes guidelines written specifically for dogs. This guide combines what they say with what each part of treatment typically costs.

## Dogs Aren't Cats: Insulin Is Usually for Life

The 2026 AAHA guidelines split dog and cat diabetes into separate documents because the disease is different in each species. In dogs, it's an **absolute insulin deficiency** caused by permanent loss of the insulin-producing cells in the pancreas, so **dogs need insulin for life**.

That's the biggest difference for your budget. Many cats can go into remission and stop insulin (see [Cat Diabetes Treatment Cost](/blog/cat-diabetes-treatment-cost/)), but for dogs the plan is long-term control. The guidelines say that, with good management, most diabetic dogs tolerate insulin well and have a good long-term outlook.

## What Each Part Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | Typical cost |
|------|--------------|
| Diagnosis (exam, bloodwork, urine test) | About $225–$500 |
| Insulin, smaller dogs | About $40–$80 a month |
| Insulin, larger dogs | About $80–$150 a month |
| Syringes (twice daily, about 60 a month) | About $12–$20 a month |
| Home glucose meter | About $20–$100 one-time |
| Test strips | About $10–$50 a month |
| Continuous glucose sensor (optional) | Roughly $33–$90 per sensor depending on pharmacy |
| Rechecks with bloodwork | About $100–$250 each |
| Prescription diet, if recommended | About $30 for a small bag to $80 for a large one |

Insulin cost depends heavily on the dog's size, because larger dogs need more units. How long a vial lasts also depends on the dose and how long your vet wants an opened vial used.

## What the First Year Adds Up To

Adding up the ranges above:

| | Low | High |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis | $225 | $500 |
| Insulin (12 months) | $480 | $1,800 |
| Syringes | $144 | $240 |
| Glucose meter | $20 | $100 |
| Test strips | $120 | $600 |
| Rechecks (5–8 in the first year) | $500 | $2,000 |
| Diet | $360 | $960 |
| **First-year total** | **$1,849** | **$6,200** |

Once the dose is stable, rechecks drop to a few a year and there's no diagnosis bill, so a **stable year usually costs about $1,300–$4,600**.

## What the 2026 Guidelines Changed

**Once-daily insulin for some dogs.** The guidelines incorporate newer long-acting "basal" insulins, **insulin degludec and glargine U-300**. Their action is flatter and more consistent from day to day, and some dogs can move from two injections a day to one. They also don't have to be timed exactly with meals and are less likely to cause low blood sugar when a meal is missed, which helps dogs with fussy or unpredictable appetites. They can also work in dogs with other conditions such as Cushing's disease.

**Cheaper glucose sensors.** The guidelines note that inexpensive **continuous glucose monitors (CGMs)** are one of the main reasons diabetes care in dogs has changed. Blood or sensor glucose monitoring is the only way to detect low blood sugar, and a sensor shows the whole day rather than one reading taken at a stressful clinic visit.

**The goal is controlling signs.** The guidelines' main aim is controlling clinical signs — thirst, urination, appetite and weight — rather than chasing perfect numbers. A well-controlled dog can still have occasional high readings.

Newer insulins and sensors can cost more per month than older options, but a sensor can replace some in-clinic glucose curves, and once-daily dosing is easier to keep up. Ask your vet which combination makes sense for your dog and your schedule.

## Low Blood Sugar: The Emergency to Know

Too much insulin causes **hypoglycemia** (low blood sugar). The guidelines list the signs as lethargy, sleepiness, strange behavior, an abnormal walk, weakness and seizures, and recommend that owners get **written instructions** for what to do. Ask for yours before you take your dog home.

## Plan for Cataracts

Diabetic dogs develop cataracts often and fast. In a study of **200 diabetic dogs (Beam et al., 1999)**, about **half had cataracts by 170 days** after diagnosis and **75% by 370 days**. Surgery at a veterinary ophthalmologist typically costs **$2,700–$4,900**, and outcomes are best before the cataract matures. Our [Dog Cataract Surgery Cost](/blog/dog-cataract-surgery-cost/) guide covers the details.

It's worth asking for an early eye check and keeping cataract surgery in mind when you plan the budget, whether or not you decide to go ahead with it.

## Diabetic Ketoacidosis

If diabetes goes untreated or becomes badly uncontrolled, the body starts breaking down fat for energy and produces ketones. The guidelines note that most dogs show signs for only a short time before they're seriously ill. Vomiting, refusing food, weakness or collapse in a diabetic dog needs a vet the same day. Several days of hospital treatment for ketoacidosis can cost as much as a year or more of routine management.

## A Note on Insurance

Accident-and-illness policies often cover diabetes, including insulin, monitoring and complications such as cataracts, if the condition wasn't diagnosed or showing signs before coverage started. Because it's a long-term condition, cover bought after diagnosis will normally exclude it. Some policies exclude prescription diets or cap annual payouts, so check the details.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to treat a diabetic dog?**
Typically about $1,850–$6,200 in the first year and $1,300–$4,600 a year once the dog is stable, based on published price ranges. The first year includes diagnosis (about $225–$500) and frequent rechecks while the dose is worked out. Insulin is the biggest ongoing cost, at roughly $40–$80 a month for smaller dogs and $80–$150 for larger ones.

**Can a diabetic dog go into remission like a cat?**
Generally no. The 2026 AAHA Diabetes Management Guidelines for Dogs describe diabetes in dogs as an absolute insulin deficiency caused by permanent loss of the insulin-producing cells, so dogs need lifelong insulin. That's a key difference from cats, which can go into remission. With good management, most diabetic dogs tolerate insulin well and have a good long-term outlook.

**Can dogs have insulin once a day?**
Some can, with newer long-acting insulins. They also don't have to be timed exactly with meals, which helps dogs with unpredictable appetites. Your vet will decide whether one suits your dog.

**Do diabetic dogs go blind?**

Cataract surgery at a veterinary ophthalmologist typically costs about $2,700–$4,900, so it's worth asking for an eye check early and planning for that possibility.

**Do I need a continuous glucose monitor for my dog?**

**What are the signs of low blood sugar in a diabetic dog?**

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is the most common heart disease in cats. The heart muscle thickens, the main pumping chamber gets stiffer, and some cats eventually develop heart failure, blood clots or sudden death — while others live normally for years.

Until 2025 there was no drug approved for cats with HCM at all. **Felycin-CA1** (sirolimus delayed-release tablets) changed that, and it's now available through vets in the US. It's a once-a-week tablet, but it's a long-term cost, and the evidence behind it is still early. This guide covers both.

## What Felycin-CA1 Costs

The dose is **0.3 mg/kg once a week**, using 0.4 mg, 1.2 mg and 2.4 mg tablets. The label dose table for cats 3.5 kg and over, priced at published online pharmacy prices in November 2026:

| Cat's weight | Weekly dose | Approx. cost per week | Per month | Per year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3.5–4.7 kg (7.7–10.4 lb) | 1 × 1.2 mg | About $12.56 | About $54 | About $650 |
| 4.7–6.3 kg (10.4–13.9 lb) | 1 × 0.4 mg + 1 × 1.2 mg | About $17.00 | About $74 | About $880 |
| Over 6.3 kg (13.9 lb) | 1 × 2.4 mg | About $20.94 | About $91 | About $1,090 |

Prices used: 0.4 mg at about $4.44 a tablet (Chewy, 24 tablets for $106.78), 1.2 mg at $150.74 for 12 and 2.4 mg at $251.26 for 12 (Tractor Supply's pet pharmacy). Prices vary by pharmacy and change over time; your vet's clinic may charge differently.

## The Rest of the Bill

| Item | Why it's needed | Typical cost |
|---|---|---|
| Echocardiogram | The only way to confirm HCM and see how thick the heart wall is | About $400–$1,200 |
| NT-proBNP blood test | Optional screening test before an echo | Considerably less than an echo |
| Bloodwork before starting | The label says to screen for liver disease; diabetes rules the drug out | About $80–$300 |
| Liver enzyme checks during treatment | The label says to monitor for rising liver enzymes | About $80–$300 each, plus the exam |

So a first year typically comes to the drug cost plus an echocardiogram and two or three blood tests — roughly **$1,200–$3,200** by adding the ranges above, plus exam fees. Ask your vet how often they plan to recheck bloodwork and repeat the echo.

## What the Evidence Shows — and Doesn't

Felycin-CA1 has **conditional approval**, granted by the FDA on **March 14, 2025**. That pathway is for drugs that meet a serious or unmet need when proving effectiveness requires a particularly difficult study — here, because finding enough cats with subclinical HCM takes advanced testing. The approval lasts one year and can be renewed annually for up to four more years while the company works toward full approval. A larger study, called HALT, is enrolling cats for that.

The conditional approval rests on a **pilot study of 43 cats** with subclinical HCM, published in the FDA's summary of the approval:

- Cats received a placebo, the label dose (0.3 mg/kg weekly) or double that dose for **six months**.
- On the label dose, the thickest part of the left ventricle wall **fell by 0.17 mm** on average; on placebo it **rose by 0.94 mm**. The label-dose group was the only one with a statistically significant difference from placebo.
- On the **double dose**, wall thickness rose by 0.50 mm, and 3 of 12 cats progressed to heart failure or sudden death; whether that was related to the drug is unknown.
- One cat on the label dose **developed diabetes**.
- The wall-thickness measure used to judge the result was chosen after exploratory analysis rather than set in advance.

What the study **didn't** show: whether treated cats live longer, or are less likely to develop heart failure, blood clots or sudden death. It measured heart wall thickness over six months in a small group of cats. That doesn't mean the drug doesn't help — it means the outcomes owners care most about haven't been tested yet.

## Who It's For

Felycin-CA1 is for cats with **subclinical HCM** — confirmed on an echocardiogram, with a thickened heart wall but no heart failure or other signs. According to the label and the FDA:

- **Cats with diabetes shouldn't take it.**
- Cats should be **screened for liver disease** before starting and have **liver enzymes monitored** during treatment.
- At this dose, it isn't expected to suppress the immune system.
- Pregnant or breastfeeding people should avoid handling the tablets.

It's not a treatment for heart failure, and it doesn't replace the medications used once a cat has fluid on the lungs or a high risk of clots.

## How Common HCM Is

In the CatScan study of 780 apparently healthy cats in UK rehoming centres, about **15%** had HCM on an echocardiogram. The drug's maker puts it at about 1 in 7 cats overall and about 1 in 3 cats aged 9 and older. A genetic mutation is linked to HCM in some breeds, including **Maine Coons, Ragdolls and Persians**.

Many cats with HCM show nothing. A heart murmur is often the first clue, but it isn't proof either way — see [Cat Heart Murmur: What It Does and Doesn't Mean](/blog/cat-heart-murmur-what-it-means/).

## Signs That Can't Wait

Whether or not your cat takes Felycin-CA1, the most useful thing you can do at home is watch for signs of progression:

- a **sleeping breathing rate consistently above about 30 breaths a minute** — our [Sleeping Respiratory Rate Counter](/tools/sleeping-respiratory-rate-counter.html) makes this easy to track,
- **open-mouth breathing**, hiding or reduced activity,
- **sudden weakness, dragging or pain in the back legs**, which can be a blood clot.

Open-mouth breathing and sudden back-leg weakness are emergencies.

## Is It Worth It?

That's a decision to make with your vet, ideally a cardiologist. Things worth asking:

- How thick is my cat's heart wall, and is it changing?
- Does my cat have any risk factors for heart failure or clots that change the plan?
- How often will you recheck the echocardiogram and liver enzymes, and what will that cost?
- What would we expect to see if the drug is working?

For some owners, a weekly tablet at $54–$91 a month is a reasonable bet on an early-stage disease with no other approved option. Others may prefer monitoring alone until more evidence is in. Both are reasonable positions while the larger study is running.

## Does Pet Insurance Cover HCM?

Accident-and-illness policies may cover HCM diagnosis and treatment if it wasn't noted before coverage began. A murmur in your cat's records before enrollment can lead to heart conditions being excluded, so insuring young cats early matters — especially breeds linked to HCM. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does Felycin-CA1 cost?**
At published online pharmacy prices, about $54 a month for a cat of 3.5–4.7 kg on one 1.2 mg tablet a week, about $74 a month for a 4.7–6.3 kg cat on a 0.4 mg plus a 1.2 mg tablet, and about $91 a month for a cat over 6.3 kg on one 2.4 mg tablet. That's roughly $650–$1,090 a year for the drug, before the echocardiogram and bloodwork.

**What is Felycin-CA1 used for?**
It's conditionally approved by the FDA to manage thickening of the heart's main pumping chamber (ventricular hypertrophy) in cats with subclinical hypertrophic cardiomyopathy — HCM that isn't yet causing heart failure or other signs. It's the first drug approved for cats with HCM for any use. It's a sirolimus tablet given once a week.

**Does Felycin-CA1 work?**
The evidence so far is limited. In the pilot study behind the conditional approval, 43 cats with subclinical HCM were randomized to placebo or one of two doses for six months. Heart wall thickness fell slightly (by 0.17 mm on average) in cats on the label dose and rose by 0.94 mm in the placebo group. The study didn't measure whether cats lived longer or avoided heart failure, and a larger study is under way.

**What does conditional approval mean?**
It's an FDA pathway for drugs that meet a serious or unmet need when proving effectiveness would require a particularly difficult study. The drug must be shown to be safe, with a reasonable expectation that it works. Felycin-CA1's conditional approval was granted on March 14, 2025, for one year, renewable annually for up to four more years while the company completes the studies needed for full approval.

**Which cats shouldn't take Felycin-CA1?**
Cats with diabetes, according to the label, and cats should be screened for liver disease before starting, with liver enzymes monitored during treatment. In the pilot study, one cat on the label dose developed diabetes. It's for cats whose HCM is subclinical, so it isn't a treatment for cats already in heart failure.

**How is HCM diagnosed in cats, and what does that cost?**
With an echocardiogram (heart ultrasound), which typically costs about $400–$1,200. An NT-proBNP blood test can be used first as a screen. A heart murmur alone doesn't confirm HCM — many murmurs in cats are harmless, and some cats with HCM have no murmur.

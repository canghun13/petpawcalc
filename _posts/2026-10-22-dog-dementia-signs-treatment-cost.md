An older dog that stands staring at a wall, gets stuck behind the sofa or starts pacing at 3 a.m. is easy to write off as "just getting old." Often it is something more specific: **canine cognitive dysfunction (CCD)**, the dog equivalent of dementia.

It is common, under-diagnosed and treatable, though not curable. Recognizing it early is useful for two reasons. Some of its signs overlap with painful or treatable conditions that need ruling out, and the treatments that help work best before the decline is advanced.

## How Common It Is

Studies agree that CCD is common in old dogs and rarely diagnosed:

- In an owner survey of dogs aged 8 to nearly 20, an estimated **14.2% had signs of cognitive dysfunction, but only 1.9% had been diagnosed** by a vet.
- Other research puts it at about **28% of dogs aged 11–12 and 68% of dogs aged 15–16**.
- The **Dog Aging Project** (Yarborough et al., 2022) assessed more than 15,000 dogs with a 13-question owner survey. The **odds of CCD rose by about 52% with each extra year of age**, after accounting for other factors.

The same study found that dogs with a history of eye or ear disorders had about twice the odds of CCD, and **dogs reported as not active had about 6.5 times the odds** of very active dogs. Those are associations, not proof that inactivity causes dementia, but they are a reason to keep older dogs moving and engaged.

## The Signs, and How They Differ From Normal Aging

Normal aging means slowing down: sleeping more, walking shorter distances, taking longer to get up. Cognitive dysfunction looks different, because it changes how a dog understands its surroundings. Vets often group the signs into a few areas:

- **Disorientation.** Getting stuck in corners or behind furniture, waiting at the hinge side of a door, staring at walls or into space, seeming lost in the garden or a familiar room.
- **Changed interactions.** Less interest in greeting family, or the opposite — becoming clingy; new irritability with people or other pets.
- **Sleep-wake changes.** Sleeping more in the day and waking, pacing, panting or vocalizing at night.
- **House soiling.** Accidents indoors in a dog that was reliably house-trained, sometimes right after coming in from outside.
- **Activity changes.** Aimless wandering or pacing, or less purposeful play and exploring.
- **Anxiety.** New fear of being left alone, of noises, or of places the dog used to be fine with.
- **Forgetting learned things.** Not responding to familiar cues, or forgetting routines.

The pattern matters more than any single sign. Write down what you see and when, including dates. That record is one of the most useful things you can bring to the vet.

## Rule These Out First

There is no single test for CCD. It is diagnosed by ruling out other causes, and several of those are treatable. Veterinary references list:

- **Pain**, especially from arthritis. A dog that paces at night may simply be uncomfortable lying down.
- **Vision or hearing loss**, which can look like confusion or ignoring cues.
- **High blood pressure.**
- **Dental disease, kidney decline and bladder problems**, which can cause discomfort and more urination at night.
- **Hormonal (endocrine) diseases.**
- **Brain disease** such as tumors or inflammation, more likely if there are seizures, a head tilt or signs affecting one side of the body.

A basic workup — exam, bloodwork, urinalysis and often a blood pressure check — usually costs about **$160–$490**. Imaging such as an MRI is a much larger expense and is usually only recommended when there are neurological signs that point beyond CCD.

## The Night-Time Problem

Night waking is often the sign that wears owners out first. Once your vet has ruled out pain and medical causes, these are the steps that usually help:

- **Make the day busier.** Short walks with plenty of time to sniff, puzzle feeders and simple training games give the brain work to do and make sleeping at night more likely.
- **Keep a predictable evening routine**: dinner, a last toilet trip as late as practical, then bed in the same place.
- **Use a night light** and keep furniture in the same place, so a dog that wakes can find its way.
- **Give a safe sleeping area** — a gated room or a pen with bedding — so a wandering dog can't get stuck or fall on stairs.
- **Ask your vet about medication for sleep or anxiety** if the steps above aren't enough. There are options, and lack of sleep affects the whole household.

## Treatments and What the Evidence Says

| Treatment | What it is | What to expect |
|-----------|------------|----------------|
| Selegiline (Anipryl) | The only FDA-approved medication for CCD in dogs; once daily in the morning | Onset about 4–12 weeks; not every dog responds |
| Antioxidant-enriched diet | Diet fortified with antioxidants and other nutrients for brain aging | Improved cognition in studies, best combined with enrichment |
| MCT-enriched diet (e.g. Purina Pro Plan NeuroCare) | Medium-chain triglycerides as an alternative brain fuel | Improved cognition in aged dogs fed for 8 months in research |
| Supplements (SAMe; phosphatidylserine/ginkgo products) | Over-the-counter; ask your vet before starting | Some positive studies, generally small |
| Enrichment and routine | Daily mental stimulation, exercise and a consistent schedule | Supports every other treatment |

Two cautions with selegiline. It takes weeks to judge, so don't stop at day 10 because nothing has changed. And it **shouldn't be combined with certain other drugs** — including some antidepressants and other medications that affect serotonin — so your vet needs a complete list of everything your dog takes, including flea and tick products and supplements.

## What It Costs

| Item | Typical cost |
|------|--------------|
| Initial workup (exam, bloodwork, urinalysis, blood pressure) | About $160–$490 |
| Selegiline, small dog (5 mg tablets) | About $40–$50 a month |
| Selegiline, large dog (30 mg tablets) | About $110–$130 a month |
| Therapeutic diet | Usually more than a regular adult food, by brand and dog size |
| Follow-up visits | Exam fee, about $50–$100 each, plus any repeat tests |

Selegiline is dosed by body weight, which is why the monthly cost depends so heavily on the size of the dog. Ask your vet whether an online or human pharmacy can fill the prescription, as prices vary.

For most families the medication isn't the biggest cost. The bigger costs are the ones that come from leaving things alone: untreated pain, a fall on the stairs, damage from night-time wandering, and lost sleep.

## Planning Ahead

CCD is progressive, but many dogs live comfortably with it for a long time, especially when it is recognized early and the household adapts. Keeping a regular record of your dog's good and bad days helps you and your vet notice change.

Cats don't tell you much when they're unwell. A dog in pain limps or whines; a cat in the same pain may simply sleep in a different place, stop jumping onto the bed, or let its coat go a little greasy along the back. Owners often read those changes as "she's just getting older."

That's why judging a cat's quality of life works best when you know **which small changes matter** and you **write them down**. This guide covers the signs vets look for, a pain scale built around cats' faces, and a cat-specific way to use the HHHHHMM quality of life scale.

## Why Cats Are Harder to Read

The Feline Veterinary Medical Association's guidance on pain is blunt: **behavior changes are the primary indicator of pain in cats.** Cats instinctively hide discomfort and illness, so the signs are often subtle.

This matters most for the conditions that drive quality of life in older cats. Arthritis (degenerative joint disease) is described by the same guidance as extremely common and under-diagnosed, with **as many as 92% of cats showing some clinical signs**. Kidney disease, hyperthyroidism and dental disease are common too, and all of them tend to show up first as changes in behavior, appetite or grooming.

## The Signs Vets Ask About

According to the Feline Veterinary Medical Association, these changes can mean a cat is in pain:

| What you might notice | What it can mean |
|---|---|
| Eating less or losing interest in food | Pain, nausea, dental disease, kidney disease |
| Hiding or withdrawing from the family | Pain or feeling unwell |
| Not jumping up, or hesitating on stairs | Joint pain, often arthritis |
| Grooming less; greasy or matted coat on the back | Pain on bending and twisting, especially arthritis |
| Using the floor instead of the litter box | Difficulty climbing in, pain, urinary problems |
| Squinting | Pain, or eye problems |
| Sleeping hunched or tucked up instead of curled | Discomfort |
| Reacting when touched in one spot | Localized pain |

Any one of these is worth mentioning to your vet. Several together usually mean something needs attention.

## The Feline Grimace Scale: Reading Pain in the Face

Researchers at the University of Montreal developed and validated the **Feline Grimace Scale** to score pain from a cat's face. It looks at five features:

- **Ear position** — ears flattened and rotated outward,
- **Orbital tightening** — squinted, partly closed eyes,
- **Muzzle tension** — a tense muzzle,
- **Whisker position** — whiskers pulled together into a bunch,
- **Head position** — head held lowered.

Each feature is scored **0 (absent), 1 (moderate or unsure) or 2 (obvious)**, for a maximum of 10. A total of **4 or more** indicates pain that needs treatment.

The scale was designed for veterinary teams and validated in cats with acute pain at a veterinary hospital; whether owners can use it reliably at home hasn't been established. But the features are easy to learn, and a cat that is squinting, with flattened ears and a lowered head while resting, is worth a closer look — and a photo for your vet.

## Using the HHHHHMM Scale for a Cat

The HHHHHMM scale, created by veterinary oncologist Dr. Alice Villalobos, scores seven areas from 1 to 10. A total of **35 or more out of 70** is generally considered acceptable quality of life; below 35 means it's compromised and worth discussing with your vet. The scale is the same for cats and dogs — what changes is what you look for:

| Category | What to watch in a cat | Low-score warning signs |
|---|---|---|
| **Hurt** | Posture, face, willingness to be handled | Hunched resting, squinting, flinching or hiding when approached |
| **Hunger** | Appetite and weight | Eating much less, needing food warmed or hand-fed, refusing food |
| **Hydration** | Drinking, gums, skin elasticity | Dry gums, skin that stays tented; common with kidney disease |
| **Hygiene** | Grooming and litter box use | Greasy or matted coat along the back, soiling outside the box |
| **Happiness** | Interest in people, windows, routines | Hiding most of the day, not coming out for food or company |
| **Mobility** | Jumping, stairs, reaching the box | Can't jump onto low furniture, needs carrying to the box |
| **More good days than bad** | Your daily log | More bad days than good over the past week or two |

For cats, **Hygiene** and **Mobility** are often the earliest categories to fall, because grooming and jumping both depend on comfortable joints. Our [Cat Quality of Life Calculator](/tools/cat-quality-of-life-calculator.html) adds up the scores and keeps the categories in front of you.

## Low Scores That Can Often Be Raised

A low score is a reason to look for causes, not just a countdown. Common, treatable reasons for low scores in cats include:

- **Arthritis pain.** Reluctance to jump and poor grooming often improve with pain treatment. Options and costs are covered in [Solensia Cost for Cats](/blog/solensia-cost-for-cats/).
- **Dental pain.** Dropping food, chewing on one side or eating less can all come from the mouth — see [Dental Cleaning Cost](/blog/dog-cat-dental-cleaning-cost/).
- **Dehydration from kidney disease.** Fluids given under the skin at home, wet food and more water sources can lift Hydration and Happiness scores — see [Cat Kidney Disease Cost by Stage](/blog/cat-kidney-disease-cost-by-stage/).
- **Poor appetite.** The FDA has approved **Mirataz**, a mirtazapine ointment rubbed on the ear, as an appetite stimulant for cats, and **Elura** (capromorelin), a once-daily oral solution, for weight loss in cats with chronic kidney disease. Ask your vet whether either fits your cat.
- **Hormone problems.** Weight loss with a big appetite often points to [hyperthyroidism](/blog/cat-hyperthyroidism-treatment-cost/) or [diabetes](/blog/cat-diabetes-treatment-cost/), both treatable.
- **The home itself.** A litter box with a low entry, ramps or steps to favorite spots, and food, water and a box on the same floor can restore independence for a stiff or weak cat.

## When a Cat Stops Eating

Treat a cat that stops eating as urgent, whatever its quality of life score. According to Cornell's Feline Health Center, **hepatic lipidosis** (fatty liver disease) is almost always preceded by a cat refusing food, is much more common in overweight cats, and is likely to be fatal without prompt treatment. Our guide to [how to tell if a cat is underweight](/blog/how-to-tell-if-cat-is-underweight/) covers the other urgent signs.

## Keeping a Log

Because cats change slowly and quietly, a written record is more reliable than memory:

- **Score weekly**, or twice a week during illness or treatment changes.
- **Weigh monthly** at home and note the number.
- **Mark each day** good, neutral or bad.
- **Take a photo** of your cat resting in the same spot each week. Changes in posture, coat and face are easier to see side by side.

Bring the log to appointments. It turns "she seems about the same" into something your vet can work with.

## Making the Decision

No score makes the decision for you. Most vets look at the pattern: scores that stay below 35 despite treatment, a cat that has stopped eating, grooming and using the litter box, pain that can't be controlled, and more bad days than good. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**How can I tell if my cat is in pain?**
Look for changes in behavior rather than crying or limping. The Feline Veterinary Medical Association lists reduced appetite, hiding, reluctance to jump or climb stairs, less grooming, changes in litter box habits, squinting, a hunched or tucked-up sleeping posture instead of curling up, and reacting when touched. Any of these is worth a call to your vet.

**What is the Feline Grimace Scale?**
A pain score based on a cat's face, developed and validated at the University of Montreal. It rates five features — ear position, orbital tightening (squinting), muzzle tension, whisker position and head position — from 0 to 2 each. A total of 4 or more out of 10 indicates pain that needs treatment. It was designed for vets, but knowing the features helps owners notice pain sooner.

**How do I use the HHHHHMM scale for a cat?**
Score seven areas — Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility and More good days than bad — from 1 to 10 each, once a week. A total of 35 or more out of 70 is generally considered acceptable quality of life. For cats, grooming, hiding, litter box use and willingness to jump are the most telling things to watch in each category.

**My cat's score is low. Can anything be done?**
Often, yes. Arthritis pain, dental pain, dehydration from kidney disease, poor appetite and hard-to-reach litter boxes are common reasons for low scores, and all can be treated or eased. Appetite stimulants approved for cats include transdermal mirtazapine (Mirataz) and, for weight loss in cats with kidney disease, capromorelin (Elura). Ask your vet what's driving the low score before treating it as final.

**How do I know when it's time to let my cat go?**
There's no single answer. Consistently low scores despite treatment, refusing food, pain that can't be controlled, giving up grooming and the litter box, and more bad days than good are the patterns most vets discuss. Tracking scores weekly gives you and your vet something concrete to look at together.

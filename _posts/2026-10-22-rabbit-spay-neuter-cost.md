Spaying or neutering is the biggest planned vet bill most rabbit owners face, and the quotes can be confusing. The same surgery might be quoted at $200 in one town and over $1,000 in another, and a cat spay at the same clinic might cost less.

There are good reasons for both differences. Knowing them helps you judge whether a quote is fair, and which questions to ask before you book.

## What It Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Procedure | What it involves | Typical cost |
|-----------|------------------|--------------|
| Neuter (male) | Removal of the testicles; shorter, less invasive | About $390–$550; low-cost programs from about $150 |
| Spay (female) | Abdominal surgery to remove the ovaries and usually the uterus | Reported from about $140 to $1,500 |
| Pre-surgery exam and bloodwork | Often recommended, especially for older rabbits | Sometimes included; otherwise extra |
| Take-home medication | Pain relief and, if needed, gut-motility support | Usually under $50 |
| Rabbit wellness exam | Routine visit, often at an exotic-animal practice | About $75–$150 |

Location drives much of the spread in spay prices. Reported quotes run from about $250–$500 in Missouri to about $730 in Los Angeles and $1,400 in White Plains, New York. Some rescues and rabbit organizations run low-cost programs: the House Rabbit Society's clinic in Richmond, California, for example, charges $200–$300 depending on county of residence.

Some clinics quote a flat fee that includes the exam, surgery, pain medication and recheck, while others bill each item separately. Compare totals, not headline prices.

## Why Rabbits Cost More Than Cats

The surgery itself isn't the hard part. Anesthesia is. The largest study of anesthetic deaths in small-animal practice, the UK's Confidential Enquiry into Perioperative Small Animal Fatalities (CEPSAF), found these risks of death within 48 hours of anesthesia or sedation:

| Species | All patients | Healthy patients |
|---------|--------------|------------------|
| Dogs | 0.17% | 0.05% |
| Cats | 0.24% | 0.11% |
| Rabbits | 1.39% | 0.73% |

That is roughly six to eight times the risk for a cat. Those figures come from general practices in the late 2000s, and experienced rabbit vets work hard to bring the risk down. The extra monitoring, warming and aftercare that takes is a large part of why rabbit surgery costs more.

This is also why the cheapest quote isn't automatically the best deal. The House Rabbit Society describes spay and neuter as safe in the hands of an experienced, rabbit-savvy vet, and warns against vets with little rabbit experience. Before booking, it's reasonable to ask:

- **How many rabbit spays or neuters do you do in a typical month?**
- **Will my rabbit be fasted?** Rabbits can't vomit, so they normally shouldn't go without food before surgery the way cats and dogs do. A clinic that tells you to withhold food overnight may not see many rabbits.
- **What pain relief and gut support will my rabbit get, during and after surgery?**
- **How do you keep rabbits warm during and after anesthesia?**
- **When do you expect my rabbit to start eating again, and what happens if they don't?**

## Why Spaying Matters for Female Rabbits

Uterine cancer (uterine adenocarcinoma) is the main medical reason vets recommend spaying does. You'll often see figures like "up to 80%" quoted online. Those come from older reports and colony data, and the exact numbers are hard to trace.

A more recent prospective study gives a clearer picture. **Mäkitaipale et al. (2022)** examined the uteri of 116 pet rabbits in Finland:

- Among rabbits with **no suspected uterine problem**, 44% still had changes under the microscope, and 13% had tumors.
- **Malignant uterine tumors affected 40% of rabbits older than three years.**
- The odds of a malignant uterine tumor were about **19 times higher** in rabbits over three than in younger rabbits.

The practical message is that the risk climbs steeply after about age three, and a doe can look completely healthy while disease develops. Spaying at a young age removes that risk.

Spaying and neutering also help with behavior. Fixed rabbits are usually easier to litter train, less territorial and less likely to spray, and they can be paired with another rabbit without the risk of litters.

## When to Do It

Rabbits reach sexual maturity at about **3.5 to 9 months**, earlier in small breeds and later in giant ones. Most vets aim for around that point and ideally before one year, when risk and cost are lowest. The House Rabbit Society's clinic lists minimum surgery ages of **2.5 months for males and 5 months for females**.

**Males stay fertile after surgery.** A newly neutered buck can still get a doe pregnant for about 30 days, so keep him away from unspayed females for at least that long. Hormone-driven behaviors such as spraying and mounting also fade gradually over several weeks.

**Older rabbits can still be spayed** if they're otherwise healthy. Pre-surgery bloodwork and a careful exam matter more, and the 2022 study recommends removing the uterus as well as the ovaries in older rabbits, because a normal-looking uterus may already contain disease.

## Recovery, and the Complication That Costs Most

Most rabbits are acting normally by the next day, though incisions take one to two weeks to heal. Keep your rabbit in a quiet, small area with soft bedding to limit jumping, check the incision daily, and give only the medications your vet prescribed. Rabbits react badly to some antibiotics that are safe in other species.

**Eating is the thing to watch.** Offer plenty of hay and fresh greens. A rabbit that hasn't eaten within a few hours of coming home, or that stops producing droppings, needs a call to the vet. Pain and anesthesia can slow the gut (GI stasis), which can become serious quickly. Treating GI stasis typically costs **$200–$600 as an outpatient** and over **$1,000 if your rabbit needs to stay in hospital**, which is why good pain relief and gut support are worth paying for up front.

Pet insurance usually doesn't cover spay and neuter, because they are planned, preventive procedures. Some wellness add-ons reimburse part of the cost, and some policies cover complications. Not every insurer covers rabbits, so check before you buy.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to spay or neuter a rabbit?**
A male neuter typically costs around $390–$550, with low-cost programs in some areas starting near $150. Spays cost more because the surgery is longer and opens the abdomen; reported US prices range from about $140 to $1,500, with large coastal cities at the top of that range. Ask whether the quote includes the pre-surgery exam, pain medication and the recheck.

**Why is a rabbit spay more expensive than a cat spay?**
Rabbits are harder to anesthetize safely and need more specialized care around surgery. In a large UK study of anesthetic deaths (CEPSAF), the risk for rabbits was about 1.39%, compared with 0.24% for cats and 0.17% for dogs. Clinics that operate on rabbits often use more monitoring, warming and post-operative gut support, and many rabbit surgeries are done at exotic-animal practices.

**Do female rabbits really need to be spayed?**
Spaying is strongly recommended mainly because uterine cancer is common in unspayed does. In a 2022 prospective study of 116 rabbits (Mäkitaipale et al.), malignant uterine tumors were found in 40% of rabbits older than three years, and the odds were about 19 times higher than in younger rabbits. Spaying also makes litter training and bonding with another rabbit easier.

**What age should a rabbit be spayed or neutered?**
Usually around sexual maturity, which ranges from about 3.5 to 9 months depending on breed size, and ideally before one year, when risk and cost are lowest. The House Rabbit Society's own clinic lists minimum ages of 2.5 months for males and 5 months for females. Your vet will time it to your rabbit's size and development.

**Is it too late to spay an older rabbit?**
Not necessarily. Healthy older rabbits can still be spayed, but the risk of existing uterine disease rises after about three years, so pre-surgery bloodwork and a full exam matter more. The 2022 Mäkitaipale study recommends removing the uterus as well as the ovaries in older rabbits, because a uterus that looks normal can still contain disease.

**Can a neutered male rabbit still get a female pregnant?**
Yes, for a while. Sperm and hormones take time to clear, and the House Rabbit Society notes that a newly neutered male can still impregnate a female for about 30 days after surgery. Keep him separated from unspayed females for at least that long.

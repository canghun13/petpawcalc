If you have looked up how much to exercise a puppy, you have almost certainly met the rule: **five minutes of exercise per month of age, once or twice a day.** A three-month-old gets fifteen minutes; a six-month-old gets thirty.

It is repeated by breeders, trainers, pet shops and a large share of vet clinic websites. It is easy to remember. And there is no study behind the number.

That does not make it harmful — as a rough ceiling for structured walking it is not unreasonable. But it has become a stand-in for an actual body of research, and that research says something more interesting and more useful than a time limit.

## What the Research Actually Found

The study that comes up most often is a Norwegian prospective cohort published by Krontveit and colleagues in the *American Journal of Veterinary Research* in 2012. They followed **501 puppies from 103 litters** across four large breeds prone to hip dysplasia — Newfoundlands, Labrador Retrievers, Leonbergers and Irish Wolfhounds — from birth until official hip screening at 12 or 18 months, and looked at how the puppies were housed and exercised.

Two findings stood out:

- **Stairs before three months were associated with higher hip dysplasia risk.** This was the exercise factor most clearly flagged.
- **Off-lead exercise on soft, moderately uneven ground was associated with *lower* risk.** Puppies allowed to potter about in parkland did better, not worse.

The authors' own conclusion was practical: puppies up to three months old should not have access to stairs, but should be allowed outdoor exercise on soft ground in moderately rough terrain.

Read that second finding again, because it is the part the 5-minute rule quietly contradicts. The protective factor was *more* movement of a particular kind — not less movement.

## Why Over-Restricting Isn't the Safe Choice

The 5-minute rule is usually read as a restriction: a limit you must not exceed. Owners who take it seriously end up keeping young puppies confined and carefully rationed, on the assumption that less is safer.

The Norwegian data does not support that assumption. The risk it identified was not movement in general. It was a specific kind of load — repetitive climbing on hard steps — during a specific window. Meanwhile, free self-directed activity on forgiving ground was associated with better hips.

A useful way to hold the distinction:

| Generally lower risk | Generally higher risk while growing |
|---------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Self-directed play — the puppy chooses pace and when to stop | Forced pace — the owner sets speed and distance |
| Soft, uneven ground: grass, parkland, woodland | Hard, flat surfaces: pavement, concrete |
| Short bursts with rests the puppy takes itself | Sustained continuous effort |
| Varied movement | Repetitive identical impact |
| Sniffing and exploring | Repeated jumping from height |
| Being carried on stairs (large breeds under ~3 months) | Stairs as a daily routine |

None of the right-hand column is catastrophic in small doses. The point is that the *type* of activity carries more of the risk than the number of minutes — which is precisely what a minutes-per-month formula cannot capture.

## Why This Isn't a Calculator

People search for a puppy exercise calculator, and it would be easy to build one: enter the age, multiply by five, print the answer. We deliberately haven't.

A calculator would turn a rule of thumb into something that looks precise, and the precision would be false. It cannot know whether those fifteen minutes are spent sniffing through long grass or sprinting after a ball on concrete — and that difference matters more than the number. It would also give the same answer for a Chihuahua and a Great Dane, whose joints face completely different loads and who finish growing a year apart.

The honest answer to "how much exercise does my puppy need" is a set of principles rather than a figure, and the rest of this page is those principles.

## Breed Size Changes Everything

The Norwegian study looked only at large, dysplasia-prone breeds, and that is where the evidence is strongest. It should not be stretched too far:

- **Toy and small breeds** finish growing much earlier, carry far less weight through their joints, and are not the population this research studied. Their exercise questions are more often about stamina and overheating than about growth plates.
- **Large and giant breeds** are where caution genuinely matters. Their growth plates stay open longer — closing between roughly 6 and 18 months depending on the bone and the breed — and their joints carry more load throughout that period.
- **Mixed breeds** are best judged by expected adult size. If a puppy is heading for 70 lbs, treat it like a large breed regardless of what the rescue paperwork says.

Our [Puppy Weight Chart by Breed Size](/blog/puppy-weight-chart-by-breed-size/) gives the growth timeline for each size group, which is also a reasonable guide to how long the careful period lasts.

## The Factor That Outweighs Exercise

If the goal is healthy joints, exercise is not the biggest lever. **Weight is.**

Overfeeding during growth is one of the most consistently identified risk factors for hip dysplasia and elbow problems in large breeds. A study of Labradors by Sallander and colleagues found free-choice feeding strongly associated with those joint conditions, and long-term feeding research in Labradors has shown that keeping dogs lean substantially reduces hip dysplasia and delays the onset of osteoarthritis.

A slightly lean large-breed puppy on a steady growth curve is doing more for its hips than any exercise rule can. A chubby one being carefully limited to fifteen minutes a day is not being protected by the fifteen minutes. If you are going to be strict about one thing, be strict about the food.

## What a Sensible Day Looks Like

Rather than a number, a set of habits:

- **Let them potter.** Off-lead or long-line time on grass or soft ground, where the puppy sets the pace and lies down when it wants to, is the kind of activity the research associated with *better* hips.
- **Sniff walks over distance walks.** A slow walk where the puppy stops to investigate everything is mentally tiring, physically gentle, and far more satisfying to a young dog than covering ground.
- **Carry large-breed puppies on stairs** until about three months, and avoid making steep stairs a daily habit while they are still growing. A baby gate solves this without constant vigilance.
- **Save intense repetitive fetch and running for adulthood.** Sprinting after a thrown ball, braking and turning over and over, is the kind of forced repetitive impact to hold off on.
- **Watch the puppy, not the clock.** A puppy lying down, lagging behind, or sitting and refusing to continue is telling you it has had enough. Respect it — carrying a tired puppy home is fine.
- **Mental work counts.** Short training sessions, food puzzles and scent games tire a puppy out without any joint load at all.

## When to Talk to Your Vet

Limping, reluctance to walk, stiffness after rest, a "bunny-hopping" gait with both back legs moving together, or a puppy that seems sore after activity all warrant a veterinary check rather than an adjustment to the exercise plan. Hip and elbow problems caught early have more management options.

If you have a large or giant breed and you are planning serious running or dog sports, ask your vet about growth plate closure before you start. Some will confirm it with an X-ray.

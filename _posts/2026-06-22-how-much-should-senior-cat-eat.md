Most cat owners feed their senior cat the same way they fed their adult cat — same food, same portions, same routine. It's understandable. The bag says "adult," the cat eats it, everything seems fine.

But once a cat reaches 10–12 years, the math changes. Senior cats don't just need less food — they need *different* food, timed differently, with more attention paid to protein quality and hydration than most owners realize.

Here's what actually changes, and how to adjust feeding to keep your aging cat in good shape through the senior years.

---

## When Is a Cat Considered Senior?

The Feline Veterinary Medical Association (FelineVMA) classifies cats as senior at 10 years and older. Some vets use 7–8 years as the start of the "mature" phase, where metabolism begins shifting before more significant age-related changes appear.

For practical feeding purposes, think of it in two stages:

- **Mature (7–10 years)**: Metabolism slows, weight gain becomes easier. Calorie needs typically *decrease* slightly.
- **Senior (10+ years)**: Risk of muscle loss increases. Calorie needs may *increase* for some cats, especially those over 12, even while body fat stays stable or rises.

This creates a counterintuitive situation — your senior cat may look heavier but actually be losing the muscle mass that matters most for long-term health.

---

## How Many Calories Does a Senior Cat Need?

A general starting point: most senior cats need roughly **20–25 calories per pound of body weight per day**, compared to about 25–30 for active adult cats.

Here's a simple reference table:

| Cat's Weight | Estimated Daily Calories |
|---|---|
| 8 lbs | 160–200 kcal/day |
| 10 lbs | 200–250 kcal/day |
| 12 lbs | 240–300 kcal/day |
| 14 lbs | 280–350 kcal/day |

These are estimates. The actual number depends on whether your cat is overweight, underweight, active or sedentary, and whether any health conditions are present. Cats with hyperthyroidism or kidney disease, for example, often need significantly adjusted calorie targets — something only your vet can guide accurately.

Use these numbers as a starting point, then adjust based on what you see on the scale.

---

## The Senior Cat Protein Paradox

Here's what catches most owners off guard: senior cats often need *more protein*, not less — even as their overall calorie needs decrease.

As cats age, they become less efficient at digesting and absorbing protein. A 12-year-old cat absorbs roughly 20–30% less protein from the same food than a 3-year-old cat eating the same meal. To compensate, intake needs to go up just to maintain the same muscle mass.

The practical takeaway: don't switch a healthy senior cat to a "senior" formula that's lower in protein. Look for foods that provide at least **30–40% protein on a dry matter basis**. Many "senior" cat foods marketed for older cats actually reduce protein — which is counterproductive unless your vet has specifically recommended protein restriction for kidney disease.

The exception: cats diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (CKD) do need protein-restricted diets, but only under veterinary guidance. For healthy senior cats without CKD, higher-quality protein is almost always the right call.

## How Often Should You Feed a Senior Cat?

Most adult cats do well with 2 meals per day. Senior cats often do better with **3 or 4 smaller meals** spread through the day. Here's why:

Older cats have smaller stomach capacity and may feel full faster but also get hungry again sooner. Frequent smaller meals help maintain energy levels, reduce the likelihood of vomiting (which increases with age in some cats), and support better nutrient absorption.

If you're currently feeding twice daily, try shifting to three meals — same total daily amount, just split into three servings. Many senior cat owners find this also reduces night-time food begging.

**Free-feeding dry food** is generally not recommended for senior cats. It makes portion control impossible, and weight management becomes harder to track. The exception is a thin senior cat who needs to eat more — in that case, access to food throughout the day can help maintain body weight.

## Wet Food vs. Dry Food for Senior Cats

Senior cats benefit more from wet food than younger adults do — primarily because of hydration.

Aging kidneys are less efficient at concentrating urine, so cats need more water intake to protect kidney function. Cats have a naturally low thirst drive and often don't drink enough when eating primarily dry food. Wet food, which is typically 70–80% moisture, supplements water intake passively with every meal.

A 10-pound senior cat should be consuming roughly **7–9 ounces of water per day**. That's difficult to achieve on a dry-food-only diet without consistent, deliberate water drinking.

A practical approach many senior cat owners use:

- **Wet food as the primary diet** (or primary caloric base)
- **Small amount of dry food** as a texture complement or mid-day snack, if the cat enjoys it
- **Water fountain** to encourage drinking — cats are attracted to moving water

If your senior cat has been eating dry food their whole life and resists switching, try adding warm water or low-sodium chicken broth to the dry kibble to increase moisture content without a full food change.

## Signs Your Senior Cat Isn't Eating Enough

Weight monitoring is the most reliable early warning system. Weigh your senior cat monthly. A bathroom scale works — weigh yourself holding your cat, then subtract your own weight. A 10-pound cat that loses 0.5 pounds over two months is showing a meaningful trend that warrants a vet visit.

Watch for:

- **Visible spine, ribs, or hip bones** — indicates muscle and fat loss
- **Dull or thinning coat** — often tied to protein deficiency or reduced grooming
- **Decreased energy or reluctance to jump** — can signal inadequate calories or underlying health issues
- **Food left in the bowl** — a senior cat who was previously a reliable eater leaving food is worth noting
- **Weight loss despite appearing to eat normally** — may indicate hyperthyroidism, diabetes, or intestinal disease

Senior cats who are losing weight but eating normally are a vet priority. Several common senior conditions cause weight loss despite adequate intake — and most are very treatable when caught early.

## Signs Your Senior Cat Is Eating Too Much

Obesity in cats is a serious problem at any age, but especially in seniors. Extra weight puts stress on aging joints, worsens arthritis, and increases the risk of diabetes. The challenge is that senior cats often look "plump" while simultaneously losing muscle — so the scale alone doesn't tell the whole story.

Body condition checks matter more than the number on the scale:

- **Run your hands along your cat's ribs** — you should be able to feel them without pressing hard, but they should not be visible
- **Look from above** — there should be a visible waist narrowing behind the ribs
- **View from the side** — the belly should tuck up slightly toward the hindquarters, not hang down

If you can't feel the ribs without significant pressure, or there's no visible waist, your cat is likely overweight. Even a 1-pound reduction in a 10-pound cat represents 10% of body weight — meaningful for joint health and metabolic function.

**Don't restrict calories rapidly in overweight senior cats.** Gradual reduction of 10–15% over several weeks is safer. Rapid calorie restriction can trigger hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease) in cats — a life-threatening condition that occurs even faster in seniors than in younger cats.

## What to Look for in Senior Cat Food

When choosing food for a senior cat, look at these factors:

**Protein content**: 30–40% on a dry matter basis for healthy seniors. Higher is generally better, provided kidney function is normal.

**Moisture content**: Wet food (70–80% moisture) is preferable for most seniors. If using dry, supplement with water or wet food topper.

**Phosphorus levels**: Important if kidney disease is a concern. Your vet can advise on phosphorus limits based on blood work results.

**AAFCO statement**: Should say "complete and balanced for adult maintenance" or "all life stages." Not all foods labeled "senior" are actually nutritionally superior — read the label rather than relying on the marketing.

**Calorie density**: Check kcal per can or per cup. A 10-pound senior cat needing 200 calories per day eats very different portion sizes from a 5.5 oz can (typically 90–180 kcal) versus a cup of dry kibble (typically 300–400+ kcal).

## A Simple Daily Feeding Framework for Senior Cats

Here's a practical framework that works for most healthy senior cats:

**Morning**: Wet food meal — half of daily wet food ration  
**Midday**: Small wet or dry snack (optional, helpful for thin cats)  
**Evening**: Wet food meal — remaining daily wet food ration  
**Always available**: Fresh water, changed daily. Consider a water fountain.

Track weight monthly. Adjust portions up or down based on what the scale and your hands tell you — not just what the food bag recommends. Feeding guides on packaging are starting points, not prescriptions.

---

## When to Talk to Your Vet About Senior Cat Nutrition

At minimum, senior cats should have a vet check every **6 months** rather than annually. Many age-related conditions — hyperthyroidism, kidney disease, diabetes, dental disease — develop quietly and are easier to manage when caught early through bloodwork.

Bring nutrition questions to these visits. Ask specifically:

- Is my cat's current food appropriate for her health status?
- Should we run a senior blood panel (kidney values, thyroid, blood glucose)?
- Is her weight trend heading in the right direction?
- Any adjustments given her age or anything found on exam?

Senior cat nutrition is not complicated, but it does require more active attention than a young adult cat. Small adjustments — more protein, more moisture, more frequent meals, regular weigh-ins — make a real difference in how well your cat ages.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How many calories does a senior cat need per day?**
Most senior cats need between 200–280 calories per day, depending on weight and activity level. Overweight senior cats may need less; underweight or hyperthyroid cats may need more. Your vet can calculate an accurate target based on your cat's current body condition score.

**Should senior cats eat wet or dry food?**
Wet food is generally better for senior cats. It provides hydration (cats are poor drinkers), is easier to chew, and supports kidney function — a common concern in older cats. Dry food can be used as a supplement but shouldn't be the primary diet for most seniors.

**Why is my senior cat always hungry?**
Increased appetite in senior cats can be a sign of hyperthyroidism, diabetes, or intestinal disease — all of which become more common with age. If your cat's appetite has noticeably increased, a vet visit and bloodwork are warranted.

**How often should I feed my senior cat?**
Two to three meals per day works well for most senior cats. Smaller, more frequent meals are easier on their digestive system and help maintain stable blood sugar — particularly important for diabetic cats.

**When should I switch my cat to senior food?**
Most vets recommend transitioning to a senior formula around age 7–10, depending on the cat's health status. However, not all senior cats need senior-specific food — some do better on high-quality adult food with modified portion sizes. Ask your vet at the annual exam.

Cats are good at hiding illness, so by the time a problem is obvious at home it has often been developing for a while. Regular checkups are how it gets found sooner. This guide uses the visit schedule in a veterinarian-written PetMD guide (Jennifer Coates, DVM, 2025), the life stages in the 2021 AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines, and average US prices from a 2025–2026 study of veterinary practices run for CareCredit by Synchrony.

## The Schedule at a Glance

| Life stage (AAHA/AAFP) | Age | How often (PetMD) |
|---|---|---|
| Kitten | Birth to 1 year | Every 3–4 weeks from 6–8 weeks until 16–20 weeks, then about a year later |
| Young adult | 1–6 years | Once a year |
| Mature adult | 7–10 years | Once a year |
| Senior | 10 years and older | Every 6 months (PetMD: from about 9–10 years) |

VCA's veterinary nutritionists likewise recommend twice-yearly checkups for senior cats.

## Kittens

Kitten visits follow the vaccine series. The 2020 AAHA/AAFP Feline Vaccination Guidelines recommend the core FVRCP vaccine every 3–4 weeks from no earlier than 6 weeks until 16–20 weeks, with a booster considered at 6 months, and FeLV as two doses from as early as 8 weeks — see [Puppy and Kitten Vaccination Schedule](/blog/puppy-kitten-vaccination-schedule/). These visits are also for deworming, FeLV/FIV testing, and planning spay or neuter, which is recommended **by 5 months** ([Spay and Neuter Cost and Timing](/blog/spay-neuter-cost-and-timing/)). They're a chance, too, to get a kitten used to the carrier and the clinic while it's young.

## Adult Cats

A yearly exam covers weight and body condition, heart and lungs, teeth and gums, eyes, ears, skin and coat, and any changes you've noticed at home, plus whichever vaccines are due. After the first-year booster, the guidelines recommend core vaccines every 3 years for most adult cats; FeLV is yearly or every 2–3 years depending on exposure.

Indoor cats are the ones most often skipped because they rarely look ill, but they're not protected from the commonest problems. Periodontal disease starts early — the Merck Veterinary Manual says up to 70% of cats have some by age 2 — and weight gain is common: about 61% of US cats were overweight or obese in the Association for Pet Obesity Prevention's 2022 survey.

## Seniors

From about 9–10 years, PetMD recommends a checkup **every six months**. Senior visits usually add bloodwork, a urinalysis and a blood pressure check, because conditions that become common with age are easier to manage when found early:

- **Hyperthyroidism**, which affects up to about 10% of cats over 10, according to the AAHA/AAFP guidelines ([treatment cost](/blog/cat-hyperthyroidism-treatment-cost/)),
- **Chronic kidney disease** ([cost by stage](/blog/cat-kidney-disease-cost-by-stage/)),
- **Diabetes**, especially in overweight cats ([treatment cost](/blog/cat-diabetes-treatment-cost/)),
- **Weight and muscle loss**, which the 2023 AAHA Senior Care Guidelines note can be marked in older cats — see [How Much Should a Senior Cat Eat?](/blog/how-much-should-senior-cat-eat/).

## What the Visits Cost

| Item | Average cost (Synchrony) |
|---|---|
| Routine exam | $57–$130 |
| FVRCP vaccine | $34 |
| Rabies vaccine | $31 |
| FeLV vaccine | $33 |
| Chemistry panel | $144 |
| X-rays (two to three views) | $158 |
| Dental cleaning | $395 |

PetMD's guide adds ranges of $25–$50 for a fecal exam, $20–$60 for a urinalysis and $20–$40 for an FeLV/FIV test. [How Much Does a Cat Vet Visit Cost?](/blog/how-much-does-a-cat-vet-visit-cost/) works these into a yearly total.

## Making the Trip Easier

Fear is a common reason cats miss checkups, and a calmer cat is easier to examine thoroughly.

- **Leave the carrier out at home** with familiar bedding, so it stops meaning "vet". Our [Cat Carrier Training Planner](/tools/cat-carrier-training-planner.html) counts back from the appointment date.
- **Ask about a pre-visit anti-anxiety medication** if your cat is very fearful.
- **Look for a cat-friendly clinic** with separate waiting areas or quieter appointment times.

## When Not to Wait

See your vet promptly for vomiting that's frequent or new, diarrhea that doesn't settle, eating noticeably more or less, drinking or urinating more, or weight loss. Go to an emergency clinic straight away for **straining in the litter box with little or no urine** — especially in a male cat, where it can mean a life-threatening blockage — **open-mouth or labored breathing**, collapse, or a cat that has stopped eating.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How often do cats need to go to the vet?**
A veterinarian-written PetMD guide recommends visits every three to four weeks for kittens from 6–8 weeks until 16–20 weeks old, about a year after the last kitten visit, then once a year for adult cats and every six months for seniors, from about 9–10 years. A cat that seems unwell should be seen whatever the schedule.

**Do indoor cats need annual vet visits?**
Yes. Indoor cats still get dental disease, gain or lose weight, and develop the conditions of older age such as kidney disease and hyperthyroidism. They may need fewer lifestyle vaccines, but the yearly exam is how slow changes are found, because cats tend to hide signs of illness.

**What are the life stages of a cat?**
The 2021 AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines use kitten (birth to 1 year), young adult (1–6 years), mature adult (7–10 years) and senior (10 years and older), plus a separate end-of-life stage.

**How much does a cat vet visit cost?**
In a 2025–2026 national study of veterinary prices run for CareCredit by Synchrony, a routine cat exam averaged $57–$130, an FVRCP vaccine $34, rabies $31 and FeLV $33. A chemistry panel, which older cats usually need, averaged $144.

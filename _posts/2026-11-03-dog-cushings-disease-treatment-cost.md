Cushing's disease (hyperadrenocorticism) is one of the most common hormonal diseases in older dogs. The body makes too much cortisol, usually because of a small, benign tumor in the pituitary gland, and less often because of a tumor in one of the adrenal glands.

It's rarely an emergency, which makes it easy to underestimate. The signs come on slowly — more drinking, more urinating, a big appetite and a pot belly — and the treatment is a daily capsule. What surprises owners is the monitoring: much of the cost of Cushing's is the blood tests that keep the dose safe.

## What It Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | What it covers | Typical cost |
|------|----------------|--------------|
| Initial workup | Exam, bloodwork, urine test | About $160–$490 |
| ACTH stimulation test | Two blood samples an hour apart, around an injection | About $150–$300 |
| Low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (LDDST) | Samples over about 8 hours at the clinic | Ask in advance; it takes most of a day |
| Abdominal ultrasound, if needed | Looks at the adrenal glands to tell the two forms apart | Commonly several hundred dollars |
| Trilostane (Vetoryl) | Daily capsules, dosed by weight | About $2.49 (5 mg) to $4.61 (60 mg) per capsule at one large pet pharmacy |
| Monitoring rechecks | Exam plus a cortisol test | About $150–$300 each if an ACTH stimulation test is used |

Trilostane works out at roughly **$75–$280 a month**, depending on the capsule strength your dog needs and whether it's given once or twice a day. Many vets now start at a lower dose given twice daily, which can mean more capsules.

## What a Year Costs

Adding up the ranges above:

| | First year | Each year after |
|---|---|---|
| Diagnosis | $310–$1,290 | — |
| Rechecks | 5–6 in the first year: $750–$1,800 | About 4 a year: $600–$1,200 |
| Trilostane | $900–$3,360 | $900–$3,360 |
| **Total** | **About $1,960–$6,450** | **About $1,500–$4,560** |

Rechecks are a big share of the total, and they're the part with the most room to save, as described below.

## Testing: Why Testing a Dog Without Symptoms Wastes Money

There's no single perfect test for Cushing's. The 2023 AAHA guidelines on endocrine diseases in dogs and cats recommend:

- **Testing only when Cushing's is genuinely suspected**, meaning two or more signs or lab findings point to it. Stress and other illnesses can make every test read falsely positive.
- **The LDDST as the preferred screening test.** Cortisol is measured before and 4 and 8 hours after a small dose of dexamethasone.
- **The ACTH stimulation test** as an alternative that's less affected by stress and other illness, especially diabetes. It can miss up to 41% of dogs with the adrenal-tumor form, so a dog that still looks suspicious after a normal result may need an LDDST.
- **A urine cortisol-to-creatinine ratio**, collected at home when the dog is relaxed, mainly to **rule Cushing's out**. A low value almost always means the dog doesn't have it.

Testing healthy-seeming dogs is more common than it should be. In a 2025 survey of more than 2,000 responses from vets in nine European countries, about **60%** said they sometimes screened dogs that had abnormal lab results but no supportive clinical signs, which the authors flagged as a risk of overdiagnosis. If your vet suggests testing, it's reasonable to ask which signs make them suspect it.

## Treatment and Monitoring

**Trilostane** (sold as Vetoryl) is the usual treatment. It's a daily capsule, given with food, that reduces how much cortisol the adrenal glands make. The labeled dose is 2.2–6.7 mg per kg per day, but many vets now start lower and give it twice daily.

Monitoring follows a set rhythm:

1. **10–14 days** after starting or changing the dose — mainly to make sure the dose isn't too high.
2. **About 30 days** — the dose may be increased if signs are still uncontrolled.
3. **About every 90 days** once the dog is stable.

The main risk is **too much suppression**. If your dog becomes lethargic, vomits, has diarrhea or stops eating, stop the trilostane and call your vet.

### Where the savings are

- **Ask about a pre-pill cortisol test.** AAHA's monitoring summary reports that a single cortisol sample taken before the morning dose correlated with owner assessments better than the ACTH stimulation test in a 2016 study, and costs less. It was studied mostly in dogs dosed once daily, so it may not suit every plan.
- **Keep a simple diary** of water intake, appetite and panting. It helps your vet adjust the dose without extra tests.
- **Compare pharmacy prices.** Capsule prices vary between clinics and online pharmacies, and the price per capsule changes with strength.

## Why Treating It Matters

Cushing's grows slowly, so owners sometimes wonder whether treatment is worth it. In a study of 43 dogs with the pituitary form at a primary-care hospital (**Nagata et al., 2017**), **untreated dogs had a median survival of 506 days**, and **52% of trilostane-treated dogs were alive at two years compared with about 9% of untreated dogs**. The study was small and dogs weren't randomly assigned, but the gap was large.

Untreated Cushing's also makes other problems more likely and harder to manage, including urinary infections and diabetes. Dogs with both diabetes and Cushing's often need more insulin until the Cushing's is controlled — see [Dog Diabetes Treatment Cost](/blog/dog-diabetes-treatment-cost/).

## The Signs to Watch For

- drinking and urinating much more than usual, sometimes with accidents indoors
- a big appetite, even in an older dog
- a pot-bellied appearance
- thinning hair, or a coat that doesn't grow back after clipping
- thin skin and panting at rest

Because these appear gradually in middle-aged and older dogs, they're often put down to age. Senior checkups are where most cases are first suspected. Our [Dog Vet Visit Scheduler](/tools/dog-vet-visit-scheduler.html) shows when they're due, and [How Often Should You Take Your Dog to the Vet?](/blog/how-often-vet-visits-dog-cost-by-age/) covers what they cost.

## A Note on Insurance

Accident-and-illness policies often cover Cushing's diagnosis, medication and monitoring, as long as the signs didn't appear before coverage began. Because it's a long-term condition of older dogs, a policy bought when the dog was young is what usually pays. Check whether your policy has annual limits that a year of monitoring could reach.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to treat Cushing's disease in a dog?**
Roughly $1,960–$6,450 in the first year and $1,500–$4,560 a year after that, based on typical prices. The biggest parts are daily trilostane — roughly $75–$280 a month depending on the dog's dose and whether it's given once or twice a day — and the monitoring rechecks, which happen several times in the first year and then about every three months.

**How much is a Cushing's test for dogs?**
An ACTH stimulation test generally costs about $150–$300. The low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (LDDST), which the 2023 AAHA endocrine guidelines prefer for screening, takes about eight hours at the clinic, so ask for its price in advance. Basic bloodwork and a urine test usually come first, and an abdominal ultrasound may be added to look at the adrenal glands.

**Should my dog be tested for Cushing's if it has no symptoms?**
Usually not. The 2023 AAHA guidelines recommend testing only when there are two or more signs or lab findings that point to Cushing's, because stress and other illnesses can cause false-positive results. A 2025 survey of European vets found about 60% screened some dogs without supportive signs, which the authors flagged as a risk of overdiagnosis.

**How long can a dog live with Cushing's disease?**
In a study of 43 dogs with the common pituitary form (Nagata et al., 2017), untreated dogs had a median survival of 506 days, and 52% of trilostane-treated dogs were alive at two years compared with about 9% of untreated dogs. The study was small and not randomized, but it suggests treatment matters for survival as well as for comfort.

**What are the side effects of trilostane?**
The main risk is suppressing the adrenal glands too much. If your dog becomes lethargic, starts vomiting or having diarrhea, or stops eating, stop the medication and contact your vet for a recheck. This is why the first rechecks happen at about 10–14 days and 30 days after starting or changing the dose.

**What are the signs of Cushing's disease in dogs?**
The classic signs are drinking and urinating much more than usual, a big appetite, a pot-bellied look, thinning hair or a coat that doesn't regrow after clipping, thin skin, and panting. They come on gradually in middle-aged and older dogs, which is why they're often mistaken for normal aging.

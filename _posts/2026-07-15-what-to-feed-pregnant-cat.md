Feeding a pregnant cat isn't the same as feeding a pregnant dog, and a lot of advice online treats them as if it were. The key difference is timing: **kittens grow steadily from early in pregnancy**, so a queen's food needs start rising soon after conception rather than waiting for the last few weeks.

This guide covers what to feed, how needs change through pregnancy and nursing, and what to avoid.

## Why Cats Need More From Early On

According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, queens **gain weight steadily throughout pregnancy** because their kittens grow continuously, so their calorie needs rise almost immediately after conception. Pregnant dogs are different: there's little fetal growth in the first two thirds of pregnancy, and their intake usually doesn't need to increase until late in the second trimester.

That weight gain isn't only the kittens. VCA's veterinary nutritionists describe it as energy storage for nursing: a healthy mother cat loses about **40% of the weight she gained at delivery**, and the rest while she's feeding her litter.

## What to Feed

- **A kitten or growth formula.** VCA recommends a high-quality kitten or growth food during pregnancy, ideally one that has passed an **AAFCO feeding trial for gestation and lactation or for all life stages**. Merck describes the right diet as a growth formulation with about 30–40% protein and 10–35% fat on a dry-matter basis.
- **Switch early, and once.** VCA suggests making any food change before breeding, to avoid adding stress later. If she's already pregnant, a gradual change to kitten food early on is reasonable — ask your vet.
- **Stay on it through nursing.** VCA recommends a commercially prepared kitten food from a reputable company while she's nursing too.

Cats also need **taurine**, an amino acid they must get from their food. Complete cat foods include it; **dog food is never a substitute** for a cat, pregnant or not.

## How Much to Feed

| Stage | What changes | Source |
|---|---|---|
| First two trimesters | Needs similar to a young adult cat; she shouldn't lose weight or condition | VCA |
| Last third of pregnancy | Intake typically rises about 25% on average; energy needs can be up to 40% above maintenance | Merck |
| Just before birth | Some cats eat less — only a concern if it lasts | Merck |
| Nursing | 2–3 times normal intake, depending on litter size | Merck |
| Weeks 3–4 after birth | Peak milk production | VCA |
| Around weaning (6–7 weeks) | Her food needs are highest; she'll also share her food with the kittens | VCA |

VCA recommends **meal feeding during pregnancy**, which makes it easier to see how much she's eating and to control weight gain, with **several small meals in the last trimester** as the kittens take up space. ## Feeding a Nursing Cat

Nursing is the most demanding stage. According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, queens need **2–3 times their normal food intake** while nursing, and they tend to lose weight during this period whatever they're fed.

VCA advises **free-choice feeding for the first 3–4 weeks** after birth, with one exception: if she's nursing **only one or two kittens**, free feeding can raise the risk of mastitis, so portions are better controlled. As the kittens start eating her food around weaning, top up her bowl to make up for what they take.

## What to Avoid

| Avoid | Why |
|---|---|
| Dog food | Not formulated for cats; lacks enough taurine |
| Raw fish | Contains thiaminase, which destroys vitamin B1, and carries a risk of bacteria |
| Onions and garlic | Toxic to cats; damage red blood cells |
| Large amounts of liver | Excess vitamin A |
| Supplements on top of a complete diet | Unnecessary, and Merck says they should be discouraged; ask your vet first, including about calcium |

## Signs Something Isn't Right

Call your vet if your pregnant or nursing cat:

- **loses weight or condition** during pregnancy,
- **stops eating** for more than a short spell, especially near the due date or after birth,
- has **hot, hard or painful mammary glands** while nursing,
- seems **weak, wobbly or twitchy** after giving birth.

A cat that stops eating is always worth a call, pregnant or not.

## Worked Example: A Cat Who Normally Eats 250 kcal

For a cat whose normal intake is about 250 kcal a day:

| Stage | What the sources say | Daily calories |
|---|---|---|
| Last third of pregnancy | Intake up about 25% on average (Merck) | about 310 kcal |
| Last third of pregnancy | Energy needs up to 40% above maintenance (Merck) | up to about 350 kcal |
| Pregnancy overall | Intake may rise by about 50% (VCA) | about 375 kcal |
| Nursing | 2–3 times normal intake (Merck) | about 500–750 kcal |

The figures differ partly because they measure different things: an average increase in what cats eat, an upper estimate of energy needs, and a general guide across the pregnancy. Rather than picking one, meal-feed her during pregnancy as VCA recommends and weigh her weekly. Steady gain without getting fat means the amount is right. During nursing, free-choice feeding takes the guesswork out, except with a very small litter.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What should I feed my pregnant cat?**
A complete, high-quality kitten or growth formula. Foods that have passed an AAFCO feeding trial for gestation and lactation, or for all life stages, are preferred. Keep her on the same type of food through nursing. Adult maintenance food isn't designed for the demands of pregnancy and milk production.

**How much more should a pregnant cat eat?**
Her needs rise gradually from early in pregnancy. In the last third, food intake typically rises by about 25% on average, and energy needs can be up to 40% above maintenance, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual. While nursing, she may need 2–3 times her normal intake, depending on how many kittens she's feeding.

**Why do pregnant cats need more food earlier than pregnant dogs?**
Because kittens grow steadily throughout pregnancy. Queens gain weight from soon after conception, so their calorie needs rise early, whereas pregnant dogs show little fetal growth in the first two thirds of pregnancy and usually don't need more food until later. That's why advice written for dogs doesn't translate directly to cats.

**Should I free-feed a nursing cat?**
Usually, yes, for the first three to four weeks after birth, when milk production climbs toward its peak. VCA's veterinary nutritionists make an exception for a mother nursing only one or two kittens, where free-choice feeding can raise the risk of mastitis. Her food needs are highest around weaning, at about 6–7 weeks.

**Does a pregnant cat need supplements?**
Not if she's eating a complete and balanced kitten or growth food. The Merck Veterinary Manual says supplementing an already balanced diet is unnecessary and should be discouraged. Ask your vet before adding anything, including calcium.

The feeding guide on the bag was written for an average adult dog. By the time your dog is a senior, its activity, muscle and metabolism have changed — and so has the right amount of food. The goal isn't "feed less because they're old"; it's feeding to keep a healthy body and muscle condition as those needs shift.

## When Is a Dog Senior?

VCA Hospitals' veterinary nutritionists describe dogs as **mature** at about the halfway point of their expected lifespan and **senior** in the **last 25%**. Because lifespan depends so much on size:

| Size | Typically senior at |
|---|---|
| Small breeds | About 10–11 years |
| Large and giant breeds | About 5–8 years |

Medium breeds fall in between. The 2023 AAHA Senior Care Guidelines stress that species, breed and size differences mean senior care "cannot be one size fits all".

## How Calorie Needs Change

- **They usually fall.** VCA notes a dog's daily energy requirements may drop by about **12–13%** as it ages.
- **But not forever.** Very old dogs **may need more calories and protein** to maintain a normal physique, according to VCA. A thin, frail 14-year-old needs a different plan from a slightly heavy 9-year-old.
- **Lean dogs live longer.** In a lifelong study of 48 Labrador Retrievers, dogs fed 25% less than their pair-mates stayed lean and lived a median of 1.8 years longer (13 versus 11.2 years). Keeping a senior lean — not thin — is one of the most useful things you can do.

## Estimating Daily Calories

Vets start with the **resting energy requirement (RER)**:

**RER = 70 × (body weight in kg)^0.75**

The Merck Veterinary Manual multiplies RER by **1.6 for a neutered adult** and **1.4 for an obesity-prone adult**. Because needs typically fall about 12–13% with age, **1.4 × RER** is a reasonable starting point for a neutered senior at a healthy weight:

| Dog's weight | RER | Starting point for a senior (1.4 × RER) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 lb (4.5 kg) | about 220 kcal | about 300 kcal |
| 20 lb (9.1 kg) | about 370 kcal | about 510 kcal |
| 30 lb (13.6 kg) | about 500 kcal | about 690 kcal |
| 40 lb (18.1 kg) | about 615 kcal | about 860 kcal |
| 50 lb (22.7 kg) | about 730 kcal | about 1,020 kcal |
| 70 lb (31.8 kg) | about 940 kcal | about 1,310 kcal |
| 90 lb (40.8 kg) | about 1,130 kcal | about 1,580 kcal |

Then **adjust by body condition** over the following weeks. For an overweight senior, the AAHA guidelines base calories on the **ideal** weight rather than the current one. Our [Pet Food Calorie Calculator](/tools/pet-food-calorie-calculator.html) does the arithmetic, and the calories per cup on your food label turn calories into portions. VCA advises not relying on a label's feeding chart for more than a couple of weeks, because it can overestimate.

## What to Look For in the Food

- **Protein:** the 2023 AAHA Senior Care Guidelines say senior dogs **may benefit from a mild increase in protein content and quality**. Don't cut protein in a healthy senior; protein is adjusted only for specific diseases, on your vet's advice.
- **Calorie density and fibre:** the AAHA guidelines note senior dogs generally need **less caloric density and more fibre** to keep a good body condition.
- **Moisture:** AAHA cites a study in which adding moisture improved nutrient digestibility in senior dogs.
- **"Senior" on the label isn't a standard.** AAFCO has no senior nutrient profile, so senior foods vary widely. Compare calories, protein and fibre rather than the label.

## How Often to Feed

VCA suggests dividing the daily amount into **two, three or four small meals**. Smaller meals can suit seniors with a reduced appetite or dental problems, and regular mealtimes make it easier to notice if your dog stops eating normally.

## Weight and Muscle: What to Watch

Weigh your dog regularly and learn to check body condition at home — see [How to Tell If Your Dog Is Overweight](/blog/how-to-tell-if-dog-is-overweight/) for the 9-point scale. The AAHA guidelines put the ideal for dogs at **4.5–5 out of 9**, and recommend scoring **muscle** as well as fat, because older dogs can lose muscle while staying the same weight.

See your vet if your senior:

- loses weight or muscle without a change in diet,
- gains weight despite eating the same amount,
- drinks or urinates noticeably more,
- has trouble eating, drops food or chews on one side.

Weight loss in an older dog is often the first sign of an illness rather than a feeding problem. VCA notes vets often recommend **twice-yearly** checkups for senior dogs — see [How Often Should Dogs Go to the Vet?](/blog/how-often-vet-visits-dog-cost-by-age/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much should I feed my senior dog?**
Start from your dog's estimated needs and adjust by body condition. A dog's daily energy needs may drop by about 12–13% with age, according to VCA Hospitals' veterinary nutritionists, so many seniors need a little less than they ate as younger adults — roughly 1.4 × resting energy needs instead of 1.6 for a neutered dog. A 30 lb senior would start at about 690 kcal a day, then adjust up or down over the following weeks.

**Do senior dogs need less food or more food?**
Usually a little less, because energy needs decline with age and activity. But very old dogs may need more calories and protein to keep a normal body shape, according to VCA, so a senior that's losing weight or muscle shouldn't simply be fed less. Watch body and muscle condition rather than age alone.

**Should senior dogs eat less protein?**
Not if they're healthy. The 2023 AAHA Senior Care Guidelines say senior dogs may benefit from a mild increase in protein content and quality to help preserve muscle. Protein is only adjusted for specific diseases, and that's a decision for your vet.

**When is a dog considered senior?**
In the last 25% of its expected lifespan. VCA puts that at about 5–8 years for large and giant breeds and about 10–11 years for small breeds, which is why a Great Dane and a Chihuahua become 'senior' years apart.

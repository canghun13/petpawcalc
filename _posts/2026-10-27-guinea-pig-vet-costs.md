Guinea pigs are often bought as an inexpensive first pet. The cage, hay and pellets are affordable. The vet bills are where people get surprised — not because routine care is expensive, but because guinea pigs need a vet who treats exotic pets, and their most common health problems tend to need anesthesia or surgery.

Here is what the typical costs are, why most households are paying for two, and the everyday habits that prevent the most expensive problems.

## What Guinea Pig Vet Care Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | Typical cost |
|------|--------------|
| Wellness exam (exotic-pet practice) | About $50–$110 |
| Sick-pet consultation | Often more than a wellness exam; one practice lists about $118 |
| Bloodwork | About $100–$200 |
| X-rays | About $100–$200 |
| Urine test | About $20–$50 |
| Course of medication | About $20–$60 |
| Neuter | About $150–$530 |
| Spay | About $150–$630 |
| Abscess drainage | About $200–$400 |
| Dental treatment under anesthesia | About $400–$600+ |
| Bladder stone surgery | About $300–$800 |
| Emergency exam | About $100–$150+ |
| Emergency stabilization | About $300–$600, before hospital care |

After-hours visits often add 50–100% to normal prices.

## You're Usually Budgeting for Two

Guinea pigs are social animals, and our [New Guinea Pig Checklist](/checklists/new-guinea-pig-checklist.html) recommends keeping them with a companion of their own species. That means every routine cost and every emergency fund is effectively doubled for most households.

A realistic plan **per guinea pig**:

- **Routine care:** about $50–$150 in a healthy year.
- **Emergency fund:** about $300–$500.
- **Monthly set-aside:** $20–$30.

For a pair, that's roughly **$100–$300 a year in routine care and a $600–$1,000 emergency fund**. With a lifespan of about 5 to 7 years, the vet budget over a pair's life is often well into four figures.

## The Three Problems That Cost the Most

### 1. Teeth

All of a guinea pig's teeth grow continuously. Normal chewing — mostly on hay — wears them down evenly. When that fails, the cheek teeth overgrow and form sharp edges or bridges that trap the tongue, and the guinea pig stops eating.

Treatment usually means trimming or extracting teeth under anesthesia, typically **$400–$600 or more**, and the problem often recurs. Prevention is cheap: **unlimited grass hay**, such as timothy or orchard grass, every day.

### 2. Bladder stones

Bladder stones are common in guinea pigs. Signs include straining to urinate, squeaking while urinating, and blood in the urine. Diagnosis needs X-rays, and many stones need surgery, typically **$300–$800** for the operation alone. Treat straining or bloody urine as a same-day problem.

### 3. Ovarian cysts in females

Ovarian cysts are very common in unspayed female guinea pigs. Post-mortem studies have reported them in roughly **76% to 90%** of adult females. They're most common in middle-aged and older sows, and can cause hair loss on the sides, a swollen belly and discomfort. Treatment is usually a spay, which costs more and carries more risk in an older or unwell guinea pig than a planned surgery in a young, healthy one.

## Why Surgery Prices Vary So Much

Published spay and neuter prices range from about $150 to over $600. The higher prices usually include pre-surgery bloodwork, IV fluids and pain relief. Those items matter, because small mammals are riskier to anesthetize than cats and dogs. The UK's large CEPSAF study of anesthetic deaths found that most small pet species had higher death rates than cats and dogs, with rabbits at about 1.39% compared with 0.24% for cats.

So, as with rabbits, the cheapest quote isn't automatically the best deal. Ask how many guinea pigs the practice operates on, whether pain relief and fluids are included, and when they expect your guinea pig to start eating again. Our [Rabbit Spay and Neuter Cost](/blog/rabbit-spay-neuter-cost/) guide covers the same questions in more detail.

## Cheap Habits That Prevent Expensive Problems

- **Vitamin C every day.** Guinea pigs can't make their own vitamin C. The Merck Veterinary Manual puts the requirement at **at least 10 mg per kg of body weight a day**, and about 30 mg per kg for pregnant females. Feed vitamin C-rich vegetables such as bell peppers daily, and use guinea-pig-specific pellets — their vitamin C breaks down with heat, light and storage, so don't buy months ahead. Deficiency can cause dental problems, swollen joints and bleeding.
- **Unlimited hay.** It wears the teeth down and keeps the gut moving.
- **Weigh weekly.** A kitchen scale is the best early warning. Steady weight loss often comes before any obvious sign of illness.
- **Find an exotics vet before you need one.** Many general practices don't treat guinea pigs, and searching during an emergency costs time.

## When It's an Emergency

Guinea pigs hide illness, so by the time a problem is obvious it's often advanced. Get a same-day appointment if your guinea pig:

- has **stopped eating** or isn't producing droppings,
- is **breathing with effort**, or has crusty eyes and nose with lethargy,
- is **straining, squeaking or bleeding** when urinating,
- is **hunched, puffed up and unwilling to move**.

## A Note on Insurance

Not every pet insurer covers guinea pigs, and those that do often use separate "exotic pet" policies. As with cats and dogs, conditions that showed signs before coverage started are excluded. Compare the premium against the emergency fund figures above to see whether it's worth it for your household.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does a guinea pig vet visit cost?**
A routine wellness exam typically costs about $50–$110 at a practice that sees exotic pets, and a consultation for a sick guinea pig often costs more. Tests are extra: bloodwork usually runs about $100–$200, X-rays about $100–$200 and a urine test about $20–$50. An emergency visit usually starts at $100–$150 for the exam and can reach $300–$600 for initial stabilization.

**How much should I budget for guinea pig vet care each year?**
Plan on about $50–$150 per guinea pig for routine care in a healthy year, plus an emergency fund of roughly $300–$500 per guinea pig. Because guinea pigs are social and are usually kept in pairs, double those figures for most households. Setting aside $20–$30 a month per guinea pig covers most unexpected bills.

**How much does it cost to spay or neuter a guinea pig?**
Prices vary widely: published ranges start around $150–$300, while one exotic practice lists about $529 for a neuter and $633 for a spay, including pre-surgery bloodwork, anesthesia, fluids and pain relief. Spays cost more because the surgery is abdominal. Males are most often neutered so they can live with females.

**Why are guinea pig dental problems so expensive?**
Because guinea pigs' teeth grow continuously, and when they stop wearing evenly the overgrown teeth usually have to be trimmed or removed under anesthesia, often more than once. Dental treatment under anesthesia typically costs about $400–$600, and more for severe cases. Unlimited grass hay, which wears the teeth down naturally, is the main prevention.

**How much vitamin C does a guinea pig need?**
At least 10 mg per kilogram of body weight a day, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual, and about 30 mg per kilogram for pregnant females. Guinea pigs can't make their own vitamin C, so it has to come from fresh vitamin C-rich vegetables such as bell peppers and from guinea-pig-specific pellets, whose vitamin C degrades with storage. Deficiency can cause dental problems, swollen joints and bleeding.

**When does a guinea pig need an emergency vet?**
When it stops eating or stops producing droppings, has difficulty breathing, is straining or crying when urinating, has blood in its urine, or is hunched and unwilling to move. Guinea pigs hide illness well, so by the time signs are obvious the problem is often advanced, and a guinea pig that isn't eating should be seen the same day.

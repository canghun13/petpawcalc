Most conversations about spaying focus on unwanted litters, and lately on whether to wait, because of research linking early spaying to joint problems in some breeds. One risk tends to get left out of both: **pyometra**, a serious infection of the uterus that affects roughly **1 in 4 unspayed female dogs by age 10**, and over half in some large breeds.

It is usually treated with emergency surgery, which commonly costs several times a planned spay. If you are keeping a female dog intact, for breeding or on a vet's advice to delay spaying, this is the risk to know about.

## What Pyometra Is

After every heat cycle, a female dog's body spends weeks under the influence of progesterone, which thickens the uterine lining and partly shuts down local defences. Repeated cycles leave the uterus more vulnerable each time. If bacteria get in, the uterus can fill with pus.

Untreated, pyometra can lead to sepsis, kidney damage and a ruptured uterus. It is an emergency, not a condition to watch at home.

## How Common It Is

The best data comes from Swedish pet insurance records, where far fewer dogs are spayed than in the US:

- The overall risk was **about 24% by 10 years of age** in unspayed females.
- The **average age at diagnosis was about 7 years**, but cases range from a few months to 16 years.
- **Breed matters a great deal.** In a 2012 Swedish study, the highest proportions affected were in large and giant breeds: **Bernese Mountain Dogs (66%), Great Danes (62%), Leonbergers (61%), Rottweilers (58%) and Irish Wolfhounds (58%)**. Earlier Swedish data found lower-than-average risk in breeds including Dachshunds, Miniature Dachshunds and German Shepherds.

In cats it is much rarer: Swedish insurance data on cats found about **2.2% of unspayed cats** affected by 13 years of age.

## When It Happens: The Weeks After a Heat

Pyometra isn't random in timing. Most dogs develop it **within about three months of the start of their last heat, on average five to six weeks after**. That is the window to watch.

Signs include:

- lethargy and loss of appetite
- vomiting
- drinking and urinating noticeably more than usual
- vaginal discharge, often pus-like or bloody
- a swollen belly or fever

There are two forms. In **open** pyometra the cervix is open, so discharge is visible. That makes it easier to notice, and it is the more common form. In **closed** pyometra there is **no discharge** and the uterus can swell dangerously, so a dog that is simply quiet, drinking a lot and off her food after a heat may be in the more dangerous situation, not the milder one.

**Any unspayed female who becomes unwell in the weeks to months after a heat needs a vet the same day.**

## What It Costs Compared With a Spay

Typical US prices:

| Procedure | Typical cost |
|-----------|--------------|
| Routine spay, private clinic | $200–$700 (more for large dogs) |
| Routine spay, low-cost clinic | Often far less; varies by program |
| Pyometra surgery, general practice | About $500–$2,500 |
| Diagnostics before surgery (bloodwork, X-rays or ultrasound) | About $150–$1,100 |
| Hospitalization before and after surgery | About $160–$500 |
| Emergency, after-hours or complicated cases | Often well over $3,000 |

Pyometra surgery is technically a spay, removing the uterus and ovaries, but done on a sick dog with a swollen, fragile, infected uterus, often at night or on a weekend, with IV fluids, antibiotics and monitoring around it. That is where the extra cost comes from.

Most dogs recover well when treated promptly. One veterinary review reports a **92% survival rate after surgery**, which also means some dogs don't survive, usually those brought in late.

## How Pyometra Fits the "Should I Wait to Spay?" Question

Research from UC Davis has found that for some breeds, especially larger ones, spaying or neutering early is associated with more joint disorders, and some owners now delay or skip spaying as a result. Our [Neuter Timing by Breed & Size](/tools/neuter-timing-by-breed-size.html) tool summarises those findings breed by breed.

That research is about joint disorders and some cancers. It doesn't weigh pyometra, and the overlap is striking: **several of the breeds where delaying is most often discussed are the same large and giant breeds with the highest pyometra rates.** The UC Davis work also reported that Great Danes and Irish Wolfhounds showed no increased joint-disorder risk at any neutering age, and those are two of the breeds with the highest pyometra proportions.

None of this means everyone should spay early. It means the decision has another column:

- **If you delay spaying**, plan the spay rather than leaving it open-ended. Know the post-heat warning window and the signs, and budget for the possibility of emergency surgery or insurance.
- **If you keep a female intact long-term**, for breeding or any other reason, pyometra is likely enough that it belongs in the plan from the start.
- **Ask your vet** to weigh joint risk, cancer risk and pyometra risk for your specific breed together, not one at a time.

## Can It Be Treated Without Surgery?

Sometimes. In stable dogs, particularly breeding animals that aren't severely ill, **medical treatment** is a recognised option. But pyometra comes back in roughly **20–70% of dogs within two years** after medical treatment. For most pet dogs, surgery is the recommended treatment.

## Does Pet Insurance Cover Pyometra?

Accident-and-illness policies often do, because pyometra usually comes on suddenly rather than being a pre-existing condition. As with anything, it won't be covered if signs showed up before coverage started or during a waiting period. Check your own policy's terms. If you are keeping a female dog intact, this is one of the larger bills insurance is likely to be asked to pay.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How common is pyometra in unspayed dogs?**
Common. Swedish insurance data found the overall risk of pyometra was about 24% by 10 years of age in unspayed female dogs, and in some large breeds the proportion affected was over half. The average age at diagnosis is around 7 years, but it can occur at almost any age after a dog's first heat.

**How much does pyometra surgery cost compared with a spay?**
A routine spay at a private clinic typically costs $200–$700. Pyometra surgery is commonly quoted at about $500–$2,500 at general practices, before diagnostics and hospitalization, and emergency, after-hours or complicated cases can run well over $3,000. Low-cost surgical clinics in some areas charge less for both.

**When does pyometra happen after a heat cycle?**
Most dogs develop pyometra within about three months of the start of their last heat, on average five to six weeks after. Any unspayed female who becomes unwell in the weeks to months after a heat, especially drinking more, vomiting or seeming lethargic, should be checked for it.

**What are the signs of pyometra in a dog?**
Lethargy, loss of appetite, vomiting, drinking and urinating more than usual, and often a vaginal discharge. When the cervix is closed there is no discharge, which makes the infection harder to spot and more dangerous, because the uterus can become very swollen. Any of these signs in an unspayed female, particularly after a heat, needs a vet the same day.

**Can pyometra be treated without surgery?**
In some stable dogs, yes. Medical treatment is a recognised option, usually reserved for valuable breeding animals that are not severely ill. But pyometra comes back after medical treatment in roughly 20–70% of dogs within two years, so for most pet dogs surgery to remove the infected uterus is the recommended treatment.

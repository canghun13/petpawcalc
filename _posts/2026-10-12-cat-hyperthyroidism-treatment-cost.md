Hyperthyroidism is one of the most common reasons an older cat's vet bills suddenly change. The 2016 American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) guidelines put it at **up to 10% of cats older than 10**. The classic picture is a senior cat that is eating more than ever and still losing weight.

When the diagnosis comes, owners are usually offered several treatments with very different price tags. The instinct is to pick the cheapest one each month, and that is often the more expensive choice over the cat's remaining years. There is also one good medical reason to start with the cheaper option anyway, and it has nothing to do with money.

## The Four Treatments and What They Cost

Typical US price ranges:

| Treatment | What it does | Typical cost |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| Radioiodine (I-131) | One injection destroys the overactive thyroid tissue; cures most cats | $1,200–$2,000 one-time, plus pre-treatment tests |
| Methimazole (pill or transdermal gel) | Controls thyroid hormone daily; does not cure | About $40–$80 a month, plus recheck bloodwork |
| Prescription iodine-restricted diet | Starves the thyroid of iodine; does not cure | About $80–$120 a month |
| Surgery (thyroidectomy) | Removes the affected gland; can cure | About $1,500–$3,000 |

Radioiodine and surgery are the two treatments the AAFP guidelines describe as potentially permanent cures. Medication and diet control the disease only for as long as they are given.

## The Arithmetic That Flips the Choice

Methimazole looks cheap at $40–$80 a month. But it is for life, and it needs monitoring. The AAFP guidelines recommend rechecking about 2–4 weeks after starting or changing the dose, then every 4–6 months once the cat is stable. At roughly $80–$150 per recheck:

| Methimazole, per year | Low | High |
|-----------------------|-----|------|
| Medication (12 months) | $480 | $960 |
| Rechecks (2–3 a year once stable) | $160 | $450 |
| **Yearly total** | **$640** | **$1,410** |

The first year costs more, because of the early rechecks while the dose is being set.

Radioiodine is a one-time **$1,200–$2,000**, plus the tests done beforehand. Set the two side by side and methimazole catches up with radioiodine somewhere between **about one and three years**, depending on where your costs fall in those ranges.

That is the flip. For a hyperthyroid cat expected to live several more years — and many are diagnosed at 12 or 13 and live well into their late teens — the expensive one-time cure usually costs less in total than the cheap monthly pill. It also ends the daily pilling, which some cats fight hard.

The flip doesn't hold for every cat. If a cat is very elderly, has other serious illness, or is unlikely to live more than a year or two, methimazole is often the more sensible choice both medically and financially.

## Why Vets Start With Pills Anyway

Even when radioiodine is the plan, many vets start with a few weeks of methimazole first. This isn't a sales tactic, and it isn't about cost.

An overactive thyroid increases blood flow through the kidneys, which can **mask chronic kidney disease**, another very common condition in older cats ([what CKD costs by stage](/blog/cat-kidney-disease-cost-by-stage/)). Once the thyroid is brought under control, that extra blood flow drops, and kidney disease that was there all along can show up. The AAFP guidelines describe this as treatment **"unmasking" pre-existing kidney disease**, and note that a methimazole trial before radioiodine or surgery may predict the risk of significant kidney problems.

The difference is that methimazole can be stopped or adjusted. Radioiodine and surgery can't be undone. A short trial lets your vet see how the kidneys cope with normal thyroid levels before committing to a permanent fix. So a month of pills before radioiodine is money well spent, not money wasted.

## The Diet Option: Cheap to Start, Hard to Keep

The prescription iodine-restricted diet is the simplest treatment on paper: no pills, no hospital stay. The catch is how strict it has to be. The AAFP guidelines describe it as **absolutely the only food the cat can eat for the rest of its life**, with only low-iodine treats and water allowed.

In practice that rules out:

- homes with other cats whose food the patient can reach
- cats that go outdoors and hunt
- cats that won't accept the food long-term

At about $80–$120 a month, the diet is also not cheaper than methimazole. It is the right fit for some households, usually single-cat indoor homes with a cat that likes the food, and an expensive frustration for others.

## What Radioiodine Involves

If radioiodine is the route, there are practical costs beyond the price.

- **It is only offered at specialist facilities**, so there may be travel involved.
- **The cat stays in a hospital isolation unit for at least three days**, sometimes up to about eight, until its radiation level is low enough to go home. Visits aren't allowed.
- **Pre-treatment tests** — typically bloodwork, a urine test and blood pressure, and sometimes imaging — are usually billed separately.
- **Success is high.** The AAFP guidelines report that a single dose works in over 95% of cats in most studies, and experts generally regard radioiodine as the treatment of choice for most hyperthyroid cats.

## Catching It Early Keeps It Cheaper

Hyperthyroidism is usually found one of two ways: an owner notices weight loss despite a big appetite, or a routine **T4 blood test** in a senior wellness panel picks it up before symptoms are obvious. The T4 test itself is inexpensive, typically $45–$80 on its own and often included in senior bloodwork.

Earlier diagnosis matters for cost because untreated hyperthyroidism strains the heart and raises blood pressure, and those complications add their own treatment bills. Our [Cat Vet Visit Scheduler](/tools/cat-vet-visit-scheduler.html) shows when senior checkups are due, and [How Much Does a Cat Vet Visit Cost?](/blog/how-much-does-a-cat-vet-visit-cost/) covers what those visits cost.

## A Note on Insurance

Accident-and-illness pet insurance can cover hyperthyroidism treatment, but only if the condition wasn't diagnosed, or showing signs, before coverage started. Because it is a disease of older cats, cover bought for a young cat is what usually ends up paying for it. Insuring a 12-year-old cat that is already losing weight is unlikely to help with this particular bill.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does it cost to treat hyperthyroidism in a cat?**
It depends on the treatment. Radioiodine (I-131), which cures most cats with a single dose, usually costs about $1,200–$2,000 plus pre-treatment tests. Daily methimazole costs roughly $40–$80 a month, and with the recheck bloodwork it needs, that comes to roughly $640–$1,410 a year for as long as the cat lives. A prescription iodine-restricted diet runs about $80–$120 a month, and surgery about $1,500–$3,000.

**Is radioiodine cheaper than methimazole in the long run?**
Usually, if the cat is expected to live more than two or three years after diagnosis. Methimazole plus monitoring costs roughly $640–$1,410 a year, so it overtakes a one-time $1,200–$2,000 radioiodine bill in somewhere between about one and three years. For a very elderly cat or one with other serious illness, staying on medication can be the more sensible choice.

**Why do vets start with methimazole even if radioiodine is planned?**
Because treating hyperthyroidism can unmask kidney disease. An overactive thyroid increases blood flow through the kidneys, which can hide chronic kidney disease. The 2016 AAFP guidelines note that a trial of methimazole before radioiodine or surgery may predict the risk of significant kidney problems, and because methimazole can be stopped, it lets your vet see how the kidneys cope before choosing a permanent treatment.

**Does the hyperthyroid diet really work?**
It can control the disease, but only if it is absolutely the only food the cat ever eats. The 2016 AAFP guidelines describe the iodine-restricted diet as the only food the cat can eat for the rest of its life, with only low-iodine treats and water allowed. That is hard in homes with other cats, cats that hunt, or cats that refuse the food, which is why it suits some households and not others.

**How successful is radioiodine treatment for cats?**
Very. The 2016 AAFP guidelines report that a single dose succeeds in over 95% of cats in most studies, and experts generally regard it as the treatment of choice for most hyperthyroid cats. Cats stay in a hospital isolation unit for at least three days afterward, sometimes up to about eight, until their radiation level is low enough to go home.

**How common is hyperthyroidism in cats?**
It is the most common hormonal disorder in middle-aged and older cats in the US. The 2016 AAFP guidelines put its prevalence at up to 10% of cats older than 10. Weight loss despite a good or increased appetite is the classic sign, and a simple T4 blood test, often included in senior bloodwork, picks it up.

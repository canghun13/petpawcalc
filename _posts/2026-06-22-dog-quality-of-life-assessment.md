When a dog is old or seriously ill, it's hard to judge how it's really doing. Impressions shift with your own mood, and as a dog slowly declines, it's easy to adjust to each new normal without noticing how much has changed. A structured scale asks the same questions each time, so the trend becomes visible.

## The HHHHHMM Scale

The most widely used scale was created by **Alice Villalobos, DVM**, a veterinary oncologist, for pets in palliative and hospice care (published in 2004 and in her 2006 textbook on geriatric oncology). It scores seven areas from **0 to 10**:

| Area | What it asks |
|---|---|
| **Hurt** | Is pain well controlled, including breathing? |
| **Hunger** | Is the dog eating enough? Does hand-feeding help? |
| **Hydration** | Is the dog dehydrated? Would fluids under the skin help? |
| **Hygiene** | Can the dog be kept clean, especially after toileting? Are pressure sores managed? |
| **Happiness** | Does the dog show interest and respond to family, toys and surroundings? Does it seem depressed, anxious or afraid? |
| **Mobility** | Can the dog get up without help? Does it need a sling or other help? Does it want to walk? |
| **More good days than bad** | Do bad days outnumber good ones? |

Villalobos's threshold is a total **above 35 out of 70** as acceptable for good palliative care. Our [Dog Quality of Life Calculator](/tools/dog-quality-of-life-calculator.html) adds up the scores and keeps a record.

## What to Look For in Each Area

The scale gives the questions; these are the things worth watching in a dog. The score bands are a guide for consistency, not part of the original scale.

**Hurt.** Pain in dogs often looks like reluctance to get up, lie down or use stairs; restlessness or panting at rest; flinching when touched; or a change in how the dog walks or sits. The AAHA pain guidelines note that chronic pain usually shows as **gradual behavior changes** that owners tend to miss. Labored breathing at rest also counts here.

**Hunger.** Is your dog eating enough to hold its weight, or does it need coaxing or hand-feeding? A bad day during treatment is different from a steady decline.

**Hydration.** Is your dog drinking normally? Dry or tacky gums and skin at the back of the neck that's slow to fall back when gently lifted suggest dehydration. Your vet can show you how to give fluids under the skin if that would help.

**Hygiene.** Dogs that can't move well can develop pressure sores over bony points, and incontinence makes cleanliness harder. The question is whether your dog can be kept clean and comfortable with the care you can give.

**Happiness.** Does your dog still greet you, show interest in food, toys or walks, and respond to familiar voices? Withdrawal, staring or confusion count against this.

**Mobility.** Your dog doesn't need to run. Can it get up, reposition itself, get outside to toilet and reach food and water, with help if needed?

**More good days than bad.** Mark each day good, neutral or bad on a calendar. Over two weeks, what's the honest balance?

A simple, consistent reading of each area:

| Score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 8–10 | Normal or close to it |
| 5–7 | Affected, but managed with care or treatment |
| 0–4 | Severely affected despite care |

## Track the Trend, Not One Score

A single score is a snapshot. Score once a week — more often during a crisis or after a change in treatment — and write down the date and each area's score. A dog that moves from 44 to 39 to 33 over a month is declining; one that moves between 37 and 43 is fairly stable. Bring the log to vet visits: dated scores make the conversation much more specific than "some weeks are better".

## What a Low Score Does and Doesn't Mean

A total of 35 or below isn't an automatic decision. It means your dog's comfort needs attention now. Some causes may be treatable:

- pain that isn't fully controlled,
- dehydration that fluids could improve,
- poor appetite that a medication or a different food could help,
- mobility that a harness, sling, ramps or non-slip floors could support,
- confusion and night waking, which can sometimes be helped — see [Dog Dementia: Signs and Treatment Cost](/blog/dog-dementia-signs-treatment-cost/).

If scores stay low despite good treatment, your dog has more bad days than good, and it has stopped doing the things that made its life its own, the scale is telling you something important. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the HHHHHMM scale for dogs?**
A quality of life scale created by veterinary oncologist Alice Villalobos for pets in palliative and hospice care. It scores seven areas — Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility and More good days than bad — from 0 to 10 each, for a maximum of 70.

**What score indicates poor quality of life in dogs?**
In Villalobos's scale, a total above 35 is considered acceptable for good palliative care. A total at or below 35, especially if it stays there despite treatment, is a signal to talk with your vet about whether anything can be improved and about end-of-life options.

**How do I know when it's time to put my dog down?**
No score makes the decision for you. Vets usually look at the pattern: scores that stay low despite treatment, pain that can't be controlled, a dog that has stopped eating or can't get up, and more bad days than good. Tracking scores over weeks gives you and your vet something concrete to discuss.

**Can a dog's quality of life score improve?**
Often, yes. Some causes of a low score can be treated — pain that isn't fully controlled, dehydration that subcutaneous fluids can help, poor appetite, or mobility that a sling or better footing can support. It's worth asking your vet what is still treatable before reading a low score as final.

**Is a quality of life assessment only for old dogs?**
No. It's useful for any dog with a serious or long-term illness or injury. The 2022 AAHA Pain Management Guidelines note that chronic pain can develop even in young dogs, and that owners often miss it because the changes are gradual.

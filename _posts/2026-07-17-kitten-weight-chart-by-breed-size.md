"My kitten weighs 3 pounds — is that normal?" It depends on age, and a little on breed. In the first two months kittens follow a fairly predictable curve; after that the most useful checks are a simple rule of thumb, a steady upward trend and the kitten's body condition.

## Kitten Weight Chart: The First Eight Weeks

A newborn kitten typically weighs **90–110 g (about 3–4 oz)** and gains about **10–15 g a day**, doubling its birth weight by around two weeks, according to Cats Protection. The ASPCA's kitten progression chart gives these weekly ranges:

| Week | Age in days | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–7 | 70–190 g (2.4–6.7 oz) |
| 2 | 8–14 | 205–295 g (7.2–10.4 oz) |
| 3 | 15–21 | 310–400 g (10.9–14.1 oz) |
| 4 | 22–28 | 415–505 g (14.6–17.8 oz) |
| 5 | 29–35 | 520–610 g (18.3–21.5 oz) |
| 6 | 36–42 | 625–715 g (22.0–25.2 oz) |
| 7 | 43–49 | 730–820 g (25.7–28.9 oz) |
| 8 | 50–56 | 835–925 g (29.4–32.6 oz) |

The chart works on a gain of roughly **half an ounce (about 14 g) a day** — around 100 g a week. Grams are the more practical unit at this age, when a few ounces either side is a meaningful difference; a kitchen scale is accurate enough.

The same chart lists the milestones that go with these weights: eyes opening around the second week, the first baby teeth from about week 3, gruel at 3–4 weeks and weaning onto solid food by about 6–7 weeks.

## After Eight Weeks: About a Pound a Month

From two months on, there's no standard weekly chart, but two guides cover most kittens:

- **The pound-a-month rule.** The ASPCA's guide to aging kittens says healthy kittens weigh **about 1 pound for every month of age** — roughly 2 lbs at 2 months, 3 lbs at 3 months and 4 lbs at 4 months.
- **The growth curve flattens.** VCA Hospitals' veterinary nutritionists say kittens approach **about 80% of adult size at around 30 weeks** (roughly 7 months), reach **adult body size at about 40 weeks** and are **mature at 10–12 months**.

| Age | Rough guide | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 8 weeks | About 2 lbs (835–925 g) | ASPCA, Alley Cat Allies |
| 3 months | About 3 lbs | ASPCA rule of thumb |
| 4 months | About 4 lbs | ASPCA rule of thumb |
| About 30 weeks (7 months) | About 80% of adult size | VCA |
| About 40 weeks (9 months) | Adult body size | VCA |
| 10–12 months | Mature; most domestic shorthairs weigh about 8–12 lbs as adults | VCA, PetMD |

The pound-a-month rule stops working as a kitten nears adult size, and it was never meant for large breeds. For a more precise check, VCA recommends the **WALTHAM kitten growth charts**, which plot a kitten's weight against age percentile curves, much like a child's growth chart.

## Estimating a Kitten's Age From Weight

The rule also runs in reverse: a 2 lb kitten is probably about 2 months old. Weight is the least reliable aging cue, though, because an underfed or sick kitten weighs less than its age. Alley Cat Allies uses other signs alongside it — eyes and ears opening over the first two weeks, wobbly first steps and baby teeth at about 3 weeks, running and playing at 4–5 weeks. Our [Kitten Age Estimator](/tools/kitten-age-estimator.html) combines these signs, and a vet can narrow the age down in one visit.

## Large Breeds Grow on a Different Timeline

Most charts describe a typical domestic shorthair. Large breeds keep growing well past their first birthday, so a big-breed kitten can look small next to a generic chart. The Association for Pet Obesity Prevention's breed reference, which draws on the breed registries, gives:

| Breed | Typical adult female | Typical adult male | Time to full maturity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ragdoll | 10–15 lbs | 15–20 lbs | Up to 4 years (TICA) |
| Norwegian Forest Cat | 8–12 lbs | 12–18 lbs | Up to 5 years (TICA) |
| Siberian | 8–12 lbs | 12–18 lbs | Not stated |
| Maine Coon | 10–20 lbs (general reference) | Can reach or exceed 20 lbs | Not stated |
| Turkish Van | 7–12 lbs | 10–20 lbs | 3–5 years (TICA) |

These are typical weights, not health targets. [How Much Should a Cat Weigh?](/blog/how-much-should-a-cat-weigh/) has the full breed list.

## When Slow Gain Needs a Vet

A young kitten should be gaining **every day**. See a vet promptly if a kitten:

- stops gaining or loses weight, especially under 8 weeks,
- has diarrhea, a pot belly or isn't eating normally,
- is lethargic or noticeably smaller than its littermates.

Young kittens have little reserve, so this isn't a wait-and-see situation. Intestinal parasites are common in kittens, and your vet can check a stool sample at the first visit.

## Body Condition: The Check That Matters More Than the Number

Once a kitten is a few months old, the same hands-on checks used for adult cats work well:

- **Ribs:** felt easily under a slight covering of fat, not seen.
- **Waist:** a visible waist behind the ribs from above.
- **Belly:** only a small fat pad from the side.

A kitten that passes these checks is very likely at a healthy weight for its frame, even if its number sits outside a generic chart.

## Feeding a Growing Kitten

VCA recommends **portion feeding** rather than leaving food out, because free-feeding raises the risk of excess body fat, and suggests dividing the daily amount into **three to four or more small meals**. Use a food labelled for growth or for all life stages, and weigh your kitten regularly. After spaying or neutering, calorie needs fall — the Merck Veterinary Manual's starting multiplier is 1.2 × resting energy needs for a neutered adult cat, compared with 2.5 for a kitten — so watch body condition as your kitten finishes growing.

## What the Chart Implies Per Day

The ASPCA ranges step up by about 105 g (3.7 oz) each week from week 2 to week 8. That works out to roughly **15 g (half an ounce) a day**. The ASPCA's rule for after 8 weeks, about a pound a month, is roughly the same daily rate: 454 g over 30 days is also about 15 g a day.

The two sources were written separately, but they describe one steady pace of growth for an average kitten through the first four months. A kitten that is gaining far less than about 100 g a week in that period is falling behind the chart, even if its weight is still inside one of the weekly ranges.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much should a kitten weigh at 8 weeks?**
About 2 lbs. The ASPCA's kitten progression chart gives 835–925 g (about 29–33 oz) for week 8, and Alley Cat Allies uses 2 lbs as the 8-week benchmark. Kitten Lady, a kitten-rescue educator, notes that kittens of 8 weeks and 2 lbs can be spayed or neutered and adopted.

**Is there a quick rule for kitten weight by age?**
Yes. The ASPCA's guide to aging kittens says healthy kittens weigh about 1 pound for every month of age, so a 3-month-old is around 3 lbs. It's a rough guide for young kittens — growth slows as a kitten approaches adult size, and large breeds grow for much longer.

**When do kittens reach their adult size?**
According to VCA Hospitals' veterinary nutritionists, kittens approach about 80% of adult size at around 30 weeks, reach adult body size at about 40 weeks, and are mature at 10–12 months. Some large breeds keep developing for years — up to four for Ragdolls and five for Norwegian Forest Cats.

**What if my kitten isn't gaining weight?**
Young kittens should gain weight every day — the ASPCA's chart works on roughly half an ounce (about 14 g) a day. A kitten that stops gaining or loses weight should see a vet promptly, because young kittens have little reserve. Intestinal parasites, not getting enough food and illness are among the causes a vet will check.

**Can a kitten be overweight?**
Yes. VCA notes that eating more calories than needed for growth leads to excess body fat and can cause obesity by adulthood, and that free-feeding raises the risk. Measured portions, split into three or four small meals, make intake easier to control.

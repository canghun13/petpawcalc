Your puppy was doing well, and now there are puddles again. Usually nothing has gone badly wrong: the puppy's routine, body or surroundings have changed faster than its habits. But a sudden change can also be a medical problem, so the first step is working out which you're dealing with.

## When It Tends to Happen

A veterinarian-written PetPlace guide (Rhiannon Koehler, DVM, 2025) notes that regression may be seen at **4–6 months of age** or **during adolescence**, which usually comes before a dog's first birthday. Several things change around then:

- **Teething.** The American Kennel Club's timeline has baby teeth falling out and adult teeth coming in from about **12–16 weeks**, with all 42 adult teeth in by about **6 months**.
- **The schedule.** It's easy to keep the break schedule set for a younger puppy, or to stretch the gaps too quickly. Our [Puppy Potty Training Calculator](/tools/puppy-potty-training-calculator.html) gives an updated schedule for your puppy's age.
- **Adolescence.** Distraction, independence and, in intact dogs, hormonal changes — including urine marking in males.
- **Changes at home**: a move, a new pet, a new work schedule, or fear of going outside after a scare.

## Rule Out a Medical Cause First

In a peer-reviewed review of house soiling for *Today's Veterinary Practice*, veterinary behaviorist **Kelly Ballantyne, DVM, DACVB**, advises that medical causes should come first for puppies that resist house training and for any soiling that starts suddenly. These include conditions that make a dog need to go more often or more urgently, that make it painful to go, or that affect mobility or control.

| More likely behavioral | Check with a vet |
|---|---|
| Accidents follow a missed break, a long gap or a clear change at home | Sudden start with no obvious trigger |
| Normal-sized puddles | Frequent small puddles, or accidents right after coming in |
| Otherwise well | Straining, crying while peeing, blood in the urine, drinking more, lethargy, appetite change |

For dogs under 5, Ballantyne's suggested minimum workup is a physical exam, a urinalysis and imaging of the lower urinary tract; for accidents involving stool, a parasite check. A urinalysis costs about $20–$60 in PetMD's price guide.

## Behavioral Causes Vets Consider

Ballantyne lists the main behavioral causes as:

- **Incomplete house training** — the most common, especially if the puppy has learned to prefer an indoor surface,
- **Urine marking** — small amounts on upright surfaces, most common in intact males,
- **Excitement or submissive urination** during greetings, most common in puppies and young females,
- **Fear or anxiety**, including separation anxiety, noise fears and fear of going outdoors.

Each needs a different fix, so it's worth identifying which fits.

## The Reset Plan

Ballantyne's house-training steps:

1. **Take your puppy out on a schedule**, supervised — every 2–3 hours, on waking, before confinement, and within 10–15 minutes of meals and play.
2. **Prevent unsupervised accidents** with a crate or pen, or by tethering your puppy to you or to a fixed spot while you can't watch closely.
3. **Reward immediately** — give a treat the moment your puppy finishes in the right place, not when you get back inside.
4. **Clean accidents with an enzymatic or odor-eliminating cleaner**, because leftover smell encourages repeat accidents in the same spot.
5. **Interrupt, don't punish.** If you catch an accident, a clap or whistle followed by an immediate trip outside is enough. Ballantyne advises against verbal or physical punishment, which can cause fear, anxiety and aggression and doesn't teach the right place to go.
6. **Relax gradually.** She considers house training complete only after **no accidents for at least 8 weeks**; most owners can get there within one to two months of starting.

## What Regression Doesn't Mean

It doesn't mean your puppy is stubborn, that you've failed, or that you need a different method. It means the structure needs to come back for a while. If accidents continue despite a consistent reset, or if any medical signs appear, see your vet and consider a veterinary behaviorist or a certified trainer.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it normal for a potty trained puppy to start having accidents again?**
It's common. A veterinarian-written PetPlace guide notes regression may be seen at 4–6 months of age or during adolescence, which usually comes before a dog's first birthday. It doesn't mean training failed, but sudden accidents should also be checked by a vet, because urinary problems can look like a training setback.

**When are accidents medical rather than behavioral?**
Veterinary behaviorist Kelly Ballantyne, DVM, DACVB, advises putting medical causes first for puppies that resist house training and for accidents that start suddenly. Signs that point that way include straining, frequent small puddles, blood in the urine, drinking more, or accidents soon after coming in from outside. A urinalysis is the usual first test.

**How long does it take to get back on track?**
Ballantyne writes that most owners can house train a puppy within one to two months with the right approach, and that house training isn't really complete until there have been no accidents for at least 8 weeks. Supervision can be relaxed gradually after that.

**Should I punish my puppy for accidents?**
No. Ballantyne advises against verbal or physical punishment, which can cause fear, anxiety and aggression and doesn't teach the puppy where to go. If you catch an accident happening, interrupt it calmly — a clap or whistle — and take the puppy straight outside, then reward it for finishing there.

**Can teething cause potty training regression?**
It may play a part. According to the American Kennel Club, baby teeth fall out and adult teeth come in from about 12–16 weeks, with all adult teeth in by about 6 months, so teething overlaps with the age when many puppies first slip. It's also worth checking whether the break schedule has kept up as your puppy has grown.

Intervertebral disc disease (IVDD) is one of the most common causes of sudden back pain and paralysis in dogs. A disc between the vertebrae ruptures or bulges, presses on the spinal cord, and a dog that was fine in the morning may be wobbly, in pain, or unable to use its back legs by evening.

It's also one of the biggest single vet bills a dog owner can face. The good news, from recent research, is that surgery isn't the only route back to walking for many dogs. The key is knowing which group your dog is in — and that comes down to one test.

## What It Costs

Typical US price ranges:

| Item | Typical cost |
|------|--------------|
| Neurology consultation | About $200–$300 |
| MRI with spinal fluid tap | About $3,000–$3,500 |
| Surgery (hemilaminectomy) alone | Often quoted at about $2,000–$8,000 |
| Full surgical episode at a specialist (consult, MRI, surgery, 3–4 nights in hospital, physical therapy, follow-up) | About $10,000–$15,000 |
| Conservative care (exam, pain relief, rechecks, strict rest) | Far less — no imaging, anesthesia or hospital stay |

The consultation and MRI figures come from the University of Wisconsin's neurology service (2021 pricing, so likely higher now). The full-episode range is from published specialist estimates, including Southeast Veterinary Neurology; ThePricer also reports one specialist hospital's all-inclusive 2026 package at $10,000–$12,000. Surgery-only figures leave out the imaging and hospital stay, which is why they look much lower.

An MRI (or CT) is normally needed before surgery, because the surgeon has to see exactly which disc to remove and on which side. That's why the imaging is a large share of the total.

## The Test That Decides: Deep Pain

Vets grade IVDD by how much function the dog has lost. The simplest version:

| Severity | What you see |
|---|---|
| Pain only | Back pain, hunched posture, reluctance to move, but walking normally |
| Ambulatory weakness | Wobbly, knuckling or weak in the back legs, but still walking |
| Non-ambulatory, deep pain present | Can't walk, but still feels a firm toe pinch |
| Deep pain absent | Can't walk and can't feel a firm toe pinch — the most severe |

**Deep pain sensation** is whether the dog consciously feels a firm pinch of the toe (by turning, crying or reacting — not just pulling the leg away, which is a reflex). It's the single biggest factor in the decision.

## Recovery Odds: Surgery vs Conservative Care

A **systematic review and meta-analysis (Langerhuus and Miles, 2017, *The Veterinary Journal*)** pooled 20 studies of dogs that couldn't walk after a thoracolumbar (mid-to-lower back) disc extrusion — 1,688 treated with surgery and 122 treated conservatively:

| Group | Recovered walking — surgery | Recovered walking — conservative |
|---|---|---|
| Can't walk, deep pain present | 93% | 62–79% |
| Deep pain absent | 61% | 10% |

Surgery was also faster for dogs with deep pain: mean time to walking was about 10–15 days after surgery versus about 2–3 months with conservative care.

A newer **prospective study (Khan, Jeffery and Freeman, 2024, *Journal of Veterinary Internal Medicine*)** followed 72 small, non-brachycephalic dogs that couldn't walk and were managed **without surgery**:

- **96%** of dogs with deep pain present (49 of 51) walked again within 12 weeks, at a median of **11 days**.
- **48%** of dogs with deep pain absent (10 of 21) walked again, at a median of 25 days.
- Recovery didn't depend on how much the spinal cord compression shrank.

What this means in practice:

- **Back pain only, or still walking:** conservative care is usually tried first.
- **Can't walk, but deep pain present:** both routes give good odds. Surgery tends to be faster; conservative care has done better in recent research than older studies suggested. This is the group where cost is most reasonably part of the decision — discuss both options openly with your vet.
- **Deep pain absent:** surgery offers a clearly better chance of walking again, and timing matters — surgery is recommended within **24–48 hours** of losing sensation. This is an emergency.

## What Conservative Care Involves

Conservative care isn't "do nothing". It usually means:

- **strict confinement for about 4–6 weeks** — a crate or small pen, with short leash trips outside only to toilet,
- **pain relief and anti-inflammatory medication** as your vet prescribes,
- **bladder care** for dogs that can't urinate on their own — some need their bladder expressed,
- **rechecks** to make sure the dog isn't getting worse, and rehabilitation once your vet allows it.

The rest is the hard part. A dog that feels better after a few days will want to run and jump, and the disc needs time to seal. If a dog on crate rest gets weaker, loses the ability to walk, or stops being able to urinate, it needs to be rechecked the same day — that can move it into the group where surgery matters most.

## Signs That Need a Vet Today

- sudden **weakness, wobbling or knuckling** in the back legs,
- **inability to walk**,
- **inability to urinate**, or dribbling urine,
- crying out, a hunched back, or refusing to move after jumping or running.

A dog that suddenly can't use its back legs should be seen as an emergency, because if deep pain is lost, the window for surgery is short.

## Which Dogs Are at Risk

According to Clinician's Brief, **chondrodystrophic breeds** — dogs with short legs and long backs — account for about **73%** of cases, and **Dachshunds** have the highest prevalence, around **25%**. Beagles, Cocker Spaniels, Shih Tzus, Pekingese, Lhasa Apsos, Corgis and poodles are also predisposed, and the Animal Medical Center in New York also lists French Bulldogs, Basset Hounds and German Shepherds among breeds at risk. Older large-breed dogs can develop a slower-onset form.

## After Surgery: Recurrence

Surgery fixes the disc that ruptured, not the others. In a review summarised by Clinician's Brief, about **6%** of dogs had another problem after surgical decompression; **Dachshunds recurred more often (10% vs 3%)**, and recurrence may approach 20%. Recurrence may be more common after conservative treatment.

## A Note on Insurance

IVDD is a classic reason owners are glad they insured early. Accident-and-illness policies often cover diagnosis, surgery and rehab if back problems weren't noted before coverage began — but a single episode of back pain in your dog's records can lead to an exclusion, and some policies have longer waiting periods for orthopedic conditions. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**How much does IVDD surgery cost for a dog?**
At a specialist referral hospital, the full episode — consultation, MRI, surgery, three or four nights in hospital and follow-up — usually costs about $10,000–$15,000, according to published specialist estimates. Surgery alone is often quoted at about $2,000–$8,000, but that leaves out the imaging and hospital stay. One university neurology service quoted $3,000–$3,500 for an MRI with a spinal fluid tap.

**Can a dog with IVDD recover without surgery?**
Many can, especially if they can still feel their toes. In a 2024 prospective study of 72 small dogs that couldn't walk, 96% of those that still had deep pain sensation recovered the ability to walk within 12 weeks with conservative care, at a median of 11 days. Of the dogs that had lost deep pain sensation, 48% recovered. Conservative care means strict crate rest for about 4–6 weeks plus pain relief.

**What is deep pain sensation, and why does it matter?**
It's whether a dog can still consciously feel a firm pinch of a toe. Dogs that have lost it have the most severe spinal cord injury. In a meta-analysis of 1,810 dogs, 61% of dogs without deep pain recovered walking after surgery compared with 10% treated conservatively, so this is the group for whom surgery makes the biggest difference — ideally within 24–48 hours.

**Is IVDD surgery worth it?**
It depends mainly on severity. For dogs that can't walk but still feel their toes, surgery and conservative care both give high recovery rates, though surgery tends to be faster. For dogs that have lost deep pain sensation, surgery gives a much better chance of walking again. For dogs with back pain but no weakness, conservative care is usually tried first. Your vet's neurological exam is what decides which group your dog is in.

**Which dogs get IVDD?**
It's most common in chondrodystrophic breeds — dogs with short legs and long backs — which account for about 73% of cases. Dachshunds have the highest prevalence, around 25%. Beagles, Cocker Spaniels, Shih Tzus, Pekingese, Lhasa Apsos, Corgis, French Bulldogs and poodles are also predisposed.

**Can IVDD come back after surgery?**
Yes. About 6% of dogs had another disc problem after surgical decompression in a review summarised by Clinician's Brief, with Dachshunds more likely to recur than other breeds (10% vs 3%), and recurrence may approach 20%. It may be more common after conservative treatment.

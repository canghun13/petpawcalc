⚕️ This tool counts and logs breathing — it cannot diagnose heart disease or rule it out. Laboured breathing, pale or bluish gums, collapse, or a cat breathing with its mouth open is an emergency whatever the number: go to an emergency vet. Your own vet's instructions for your pet always take priority over the general thresholds used here.

Why Cardiologists Ask Owners to Count

When heart disease progresses to heart failure, fluid starts to build up in or around the lungs. One of the earliest things that changes is how fast a pet breathes at rest, often before coughing, tiredness or obvious distress. A sleeping pet has none of the usual reasons to breathe fast, so a rise in the sleeping rate is a signal worth acting on.

That is why veterinary cardiologists routinely send owners of dogs and cats with heart disease home with one instruction above all others: count the breaths while they sleep, and call if the number stays up. It costs nothing, takes thirty seconds, and it is the one heart check you can do every day. The 30-breath threshold this tool uses is from VCA Hospitals' owner guide to home breathing rates; a veterinary cardiologist's review in Clinician's Brief (Scansen) gives the study data behind it.

The Numbers

  • About 15–30 breaths a minute is normal for a sleeping or calmly resting dog or cat, including pets with heart disease that isn't causing signs or is well controlled, according to VCA. Lower can be normal in a pet that is otherwise well.
  • Study data: healthy dogs averaged about 14 breaths a minute asleep, with an upper limit of about 25; in cats the upper limit of normal sleeping rate is about 30–35 (Scansen, Clinician's Brief).
  • Consistently above about 30 while asleep is considered increased (VCA). Recount, and contact your vet if it stays up. Your vet may set a different threshold for your pet.
  • Your pet's own baseline matters as much as the threshold. Scansen notes a rise of 25% or more from a pet's baseline may signal a problem, confirmed over 2–3 readings. A dog that usually sleeps at 16 breaths a minute and is now steadily at 27 has changed, even though 27 is under 30.

How to Get an Accurate Count

  • Count during real sleep — Scansen suggests the pet should have been resting or asleep for at least 15 minutes.
  • Watch, don't touch. Look at the chest or belly from a little distance. A hand on the chest often wakes the pet or changes the rhythm.
  • One rise plus one fall is one breath. Counting each movement separately doubles the number, the single most common counting mistake.
  • Skip active dreaming. Don't count during paddling or twitching; wait until it settles.
  • Cats must not be purring (VCA). Dogs shouldn't be panting.

VCA suggests counting once a day for about a week while you're learning. After that, follow your vet's schedule: often once or twice a week for heart disease without signs, and at least once a day for a pet with heart failure on medication.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a normal sleeping breathing rate for a dog or cat?

VCA Hospitals gives 15–30 breaths a minute for a sleeping or calmly resting dog or cat, including pets whose heart disease isn't causing signs or is well controlled; lower can be normal. A rate consistently above 30 while asleep is considered increased. Study data put healthy dogs' average sleeping rate at about 14.

Why count breaths while the pet is asleep?

Sleep removes most other reasons for fast breathing, such as excitement, exercise, panting and purring, and a cardiologist's review in Clinician's Brief notes the sleeping rate appears less variable than the awake resting rate. In pets with heart disease, a rise can be an early sign of fluid building up.

My pet's breathing rate is over 30. What should I do?

Recount while your pet is properly asleep; Clinician's Brief suggests confirming an increase over 2–3 readings. If it stays consistently above about 30, contact your vet, and follow any threshold your vet has set. Fast breathing with laboured breathing, pale or bluish gums or collapse is an emergency.

How do I count my cat's breathing if she is purring?

Wait until she stops. VCA says cats should not be purring when you count. Watch the chest or belly and count one breath each time it rises and falls once, for 30 seconds, then double it.

How often should I check my pet's sleeping respiratory rate?

VCA suggests once a day for about a week while you learn your pet's baseline, then as your vet directs: often once or twice a week for heart disease without signs, and at least daily for heart failure on medication. A rise of 25% or more from baseline may matter even under 30.

What Is the Paw Score (HHHHHMM Scale) for Cats?

The HHHHHMM Scale was developed by Dr. Alice Villalobos, a veterinary oncologist, as a practical framework for evaluating quality of life in animals facing serious illness, chronic pain, or end-of-life conditions. The acronym stands for Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility, and More good days than bad.

Each of the seven areas is scored from 0 to 10, for a total out of 70. Villalobos considers a total above 35 acceptable for good palliative care. At or below 35, it's time to talk to your vet about what can be improved — pain, dehydration and poor appetite can often be treated.

Cats are good at hiding pain and illness, which is why the categories above lean on concrete, observable behaviors — grooming habits, litter box use, willingness to jump — rather than more obvious cues like vocalizing or limping, which cats often suppress until a condition is advanced.

Look at your cat's face as well as its behavior. The Feline Grimace Scale, developed at the University of Montreal, scores pain from five features: ears flattened and rotated outward, squinted eyes, a tense muzzle, whiskers pulled together, and a lowered head. It was designed for veterinary teams, but noticing those features in a resting cat is a good reason to score Hurt lower and call your vet. When to Use a Quality of Life Assessment for a Cat

For cats, these are the times a regular score helps most:

Senior cats (10 and older in the AAHA/AAFP life stages): Repeating the score regularly helps you notice gradual changes. Aging cats change slowly — grooming less, jumping less, sleeping in different spots — and a structured checklist catches drift that day-to-day observation misses.

Cats with chronic kidney disease, cancer, or hyperthyroidism: Ongoing monitoring of wellbeing helps guide treatment decisions alongside bloodwork and vet exams.

Cats with chronic pain conditions: Cats hide stiffness well and rarely limp the way dogs do. Reduced grooming and reluctance to jump are often the first visible signs, and both are captured directly in the categories above.

When treatment changes: A cat can't tell you whether a new pain medication, home fluids or an appetite stimulant is helping. Score the week before and a few weeks after, and watch grooming and jumping in particular — they're often the first things to improve.

The Hardest Question: More Good Days Than Bad

"More good days than bad" is hard to judge in a cat, because a bad day often looks like a quiet one: more time out of sight, less grooming, food left in the bowl. Without a record, those days blur together.

A "good day" for a cat doesn't require play or exploring. It might mean settling in a favorite sunny spot, purring when petted, grooming normally, or greeting you at the door. A "bad day" is one where those moments are absent — replaced by hiding, refusing food, or obvious discomfort that can't be relieved.

Mark each day as good, neutral or bad on a calendar, and take a photo of your cat resting in the same place once a week. Changes in posture and coat are much easier to spot side by side than from memory.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a paw score for a cat?

"Paw score" isn't a formal veterinary term; on this page it means the total on the HHHHHMM Quality of Life Scale created by veterinary oncologist Alice Villalobos for pets in palliative and hospice care. Seven areas — Hurt, Hunger, Hydration, Hygiene, Happiness, Mobility and More good days than bad — are each scored 0 to 10, for a maximum of 70.

When should I use a quality of life calculator for my cat?

For any cat with a serious or long-term illness, chronic pain, or a terminal diagnosis, and for seniors whose wellbeing you're tracking. Repeat it regularly and bring the scores to your vet; the trend tells you more than one total.

What score indicates a good quality of life for a cat?

Villalobos considers a total above 35 out of 70 acceptable for good palliative care. A high total can still hide one very low area, so the result flags any area scoring 3 or less. The score is a starting point for a conversation with your vet, not a verdict.

Does the HHHHHMM scale work for cats?

Yes. Villalobos created it for pets in palliative and hospice care, and the same seven areas apply to cats. In cats, look closely at grooming, hiding, jumping and litter box use, since cats hide discomfort well.

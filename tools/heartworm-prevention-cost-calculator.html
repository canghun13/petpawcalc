Prevention Cost vs. Treatment Cost

Treating heartworm usually costs about $700–$2,000 for an uncomplicated case and can pass $3,000, because the drug that kills adult worms is dosed by weight and the American Heartworm Society protocol runs for months, with confirmatory tests, X-rays, doxycycline, steroids and three melarsomine injections. See Heartworm Treatment Cost for the item-by-item breakdown.

Missed months are the real risk. In a study of US clinic purchase records (Lavan et al., 2018, Parasites & Vectors), owners bought about 7 months of heartworm medication per dog per year on average, and only about a third of dogs had 11–12 months. The calculator's "7 months" option shows that pattern's cost — and its gap.

Safety Notes That Matter More Than the Price

  • Test before you start. A dog needs a negative heartworm test before beginning any preventive product. Giving a preventive to a dog that already has an active infection can trigger a severe, potentially fatal reaction as the medication rapidly kills circulating microfilariae in the bloodstream.
  • Test annually, even while on prevention. No preventive product is 100% effective, and a missed dose, a vomited chewable you did not notice, or a rare breakthrough infection can happen even with consistent use. The American Heartworm Society recommends annual testing regardless of how reliably you dose.
  • Year-round dosing is the actual recommendation, not seasonal. The American Heartworm Society recommends year-round prevention. Mosquito seasons don't stop cleanly at a calendar line, and a gap in coverage can let an infection through.

What About Cats?

This calculator is built around dogs, since dosing, product options, and treatment protocols differ meaningfully by species — but heartworm matters just as much for cats, arguably more. There is no FDA-approved heartworm treatment for cats at all. If a cat becomes infected, veterinary care focuses on managing symptoms and supporting the cat through the infection rather than eliminating the worms, since the treatment used in dogs (melarsomine) is not safe for cats. That makes monthly preventive medication the only real defense for cats in any heartworm-risk area, indoor cats included — mosquitoes get inside, and heartworm disease in cats can look like asthma or simply go undiagnosed until it is serious.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much is heartworm prevention for dogs?

At Chewy prices checked in November 2026, a year of Heartgard Plus cost about $119 for a dog up to 25 lb, about $151 for 26–50 lb and about $146 for 51–100 lb. A yearly ProHeart 12 injection is roughly $78–$150 up to 25 lb and $135–$190 for 51–75 lb on published clinic price lists. Add the annual heartworm test, typically $35–$75.

What does heartworm prevention cost per month?

About $10–$13 a month for Heartgard Plus at Chewy prices in November 2026, depending on the weight band. Simparica Trio, which also covers fleas and ticks, was about $31–$39 a month. ProHeart 12 is a single yearly injection, so its cost is usually compared with 12 months of monthly chews.

Is heartworm prevention worth it?

Treating heartworm usually costs about $700–$2,000 for an uncomplicated case and can pass $3,000, and the treatment takes months of restricted activity. A year of prevention for most dogs costs a fraction of that. For cats there's no approved drug to treat heartworm infection, so prevention is the only option.

How much does a heartworm test cost?

Typically $35–$75 at a veterinary clinic. The American Heartworm Society recommends testing every year, even for dogs on prevention, and before starting a preventive. Our heartworm test guide explains why the timing of the test matters more than its price.

Does the comparison include ProHeart 12?

Yes. ProHeart 12 (moxidectin) is a once-a-year injection given at the vet. Published clinic price lists put it at roughly $78–$150 for dogs up to 25 lb, $105–$160 for 26–50 lb, $135–$190 for 51–75 lb and $154–$246 for 76–100 lb. Its label limits it to dogs at least 12 months old.

Does this calculator work out a dose?

No. It compares what a year of prevention costs against what treating an infection would cost. Heartworm preventives are weight-dosed prescription products, and both the product and the dose are your veterinarian's decision.

👂 Why Do the Ear Infections Keep Coming Back?

If your pet has had several ear infections, the ear is rarely the actual problem. Veterinary dermatology treats otitis as four stacked layers, and the drops only reach one of them. This works out which layer is likely being missed.

Do not put anything into your pet's ear without a vet confirming the eardrum is intact, including leftover drops from a previous infection and over-the-counter cleaners. If the eardrum is perforated, some products can damage the middle ear and hearing.

The Four Layers (PSPP)

Veterinary dermatology classifies otitis externa into four categories, usually abbreviated PSPP. Understanding which layer the treatment is addressing explains almost everything about why some ears clear and stay clear while others become a permanent feature of your life.

LayerWhat it isExamples
PrimaryCan cause otitis in a completely healthy earAllergic skin disease (atopic or food), ear mites, foreign bodies, hormonal disease, polyps or growths
SecondaryOnly causes trouble in an already-abnormal earYeast (Malassezia) and bacterial overgrowth — this is what the drops treat
PredisposingRaises risk but never causes otitis aloneFloppy or hairy ears, narrow canals, swimming, over-cleaning
PerpetuatingConsequences of inflammation that block resolutionThickened canal lining, narrowing, mineralisation, middle ear involvement

Ear drops treat the secondary layer. That is genuinely useful — the infection is real and needs clearing — but if the primary cause is untouched, the canal becomes inflamed again and the organisms come back with it. That single mismatch is the mechanism behind most recurring ears.

Allergy Is the Usual Primary Cause

In dogs, the numbers are lopsided. A 2025 review in Today's Veterinary Practice (Lundberg and Montin Mills) puts atopic dermatitis as the primary cause in 43% to 75% of dogs with otitis externa, and 31% to 50% of dogs with chronic or recurrent otitis.

Food allergy matters too. In a study cited by veterinary dermatologist Melissa Hall in dvm360 (Picco et al., 2008), 55% of 65 food-allergic dogs had otitis externa, and in 34% the ear problems came before any other sign. So "it can't be allergies, his skin is fine" is not the reassurance it sounds like.

The distinction between food and environmental allergy matters for what you do next, and seasonality is the most useful clue an owner has. Environmental allergy classically flares with pollen seasons; food reaction runs year-round. When One Ear Changes the Answer

Allergy affects the whole animal, so allergic otitis usually involves both ears. The Today's Veterinary Practice review lists causes of one-sided otitis such as polyps — particularly in cats, where allergic otitis is less common — and a vet looks down the canal for foreign objects and polyps (Merck). A dog that suddenly starts shaking its head and pawing at one ear after a walk through long grass may have a grass seed in it, which needs a vet rather than drops.

Why Cytology Every Time, Not Just the First Time

"The most valuable diagnostic tool is cytology," according to the Today's Veterinary Practice review — a swab examined under the microscope shows whether yeast, cocci or rods are growing, and the treatment differs. You can't tell them apart by smell or appearance, and what grows can change between episodes, so drops that worked last time may not be right this time. If you've been given medication without a swab, asking for cytology is reasonable.

Cats Are a Different Picture

Allergic otitis is less common in cats than in dogs (Today's Veterinary Practice), which shifts the likely causes. The Merck Veterinary Manual lists parasites, foreign objects and allergies as causes in cats, and polyps are a cause of one-sided otitis particularly in cats. Ear mites spread between pets in the household. These need a vet to look down the canal, which in a painful or uncooperative cat may mean sedation.

The Damage That Does Not Reverse

Chronic inflammation thickens the canal lining and narrows it, and can eventually mineralise it. The Today's Veterinary Practice review notes that early changes such as thickening can be reversed with treatment, but mineralisation cannot. Middle ear involvement can hide in recurrent cases: in one MRI study cited in the review, 20.8% of 121 dogs with chronic or recurrent otitis had otitis media with no obvious signs of it. That's the argument for pushing on the underlying cause early.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my dog keep getting ear infections?

Usually because the infection is the consequence, not the cause. Vets use the PSPP framework — primary causes, secondary infection, predisposing and perpetuating factors — and drops treat only the secondary infection. A 2025 review in Today's Veterinary Practice puts atopic dermatitis as the primary cause in 43% to 75% of dogs with otitis externa.

Can food allergies cause ear infections in dogs?

Yes. In a study cited in dvm360 (Picco et al., 2008), 55% of 65 food-allergic dogs had otitis externa, and in 34% the ear problems came before any other sign. If ears keep recurring, an elimination diet trial is worth discussing with your vet.

Why is only one ear infected?

Allergy affects the whole animal, so allergic otitis usually involves both ears. A one-sided problem makes causes such as a foreign body or a polyp worth ruling out — polyps particularly in cats, where allergic otitis is less common. A vet needs to look down the canal.

Can I treat my dog's ear infection at home?

Not safely. If the eardrum is damaged, some medications can harm the middle ear, so the eardrum needs checking before treatment, and the Merck Veterinary Manual warns against home remedies such as hydrogen peroxide or vinegar. Leftover drops may also be wrong for what's growing this time.

What is ear cytology and why does it matter?

A swab of the ear examined under a microscope to see whether yeast, cocci or rods are growing. A 2025 veterinary review calls it the most valuable diagnostic tool in otitis, because treatment differs by organism and what grows can change between episodes.

Can chronic ear infections cause permanent damage?

Yes. Repeated inflammation thickens and narrows the canal; early changes can be reversed with treatment, but mineralisation cannot. In one MRI study, about 1 in 5 dogs with chronic or recurrent otitis had middle ear involvement with no obvious signs.

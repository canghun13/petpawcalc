Your Dog's Estimated Cycle Stages

⚕️ Dates are estimates from published averages; individual dogs vary, and VCA notes cycles can be irregular for up to two years after the first heat. A calendar can't time ovulation — for breeding, ask your vet about progesterone testing.

How Often Dogs Come Into Heat

VCA Hospitals says most dogs cycle twice a year, small breeds may cycle three times a year, and giant breeds may cycle once a year. Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center gives an average interval of 5–11 months and notes that some breeds, such as the Basenji and Tibetan Mastiff, typically cycle only once a year.

The first heat comes at about six months on average, according to VCA, though large and giant breeds may not cycle until 18 months to two years. Cornell puts the range at 6–24 months. Early cycles can be irregular, so if you have dates from two past heats, entering her own interval gives a better estimate than the averages.

The Stages of the Dog Heat Cycle

Proestrus: the vulva swells and a bloody discharge appears. She attracts males but usually won't accept them yet. Cornell says this stage lasts, on average, six to eleven days.

Estrus: she becomes receptive to mating. Cornell says it usually lasts five to nine days but may vary between one and twenty. VCA notes the discharge thins to watery and pink, ovulation usually happens around that point, and pregnancy is possible at any point during estrus because sperm can survive about a week.

Diestrus: progesterone rises whether or not she is pregnant, peaks 2–3 weeks after ovulation and then falls gradually (Cornell). Many unspayed females show some signs of false pregnancy in this period.

Anestrus: the resting phase until the next proestrus. Cornell notes the uterus needs about four months to recover after a heat or a litter.

Overall, Cornell says the bloody discharge usually lasts between 14 and 21 days.

Signs Your Dog Is in Heat

  • Vulvar swelling, which can be subtle
  • Bloody discharge that later thins to watery and pink
  • Urinating more often, or small marking urinations
  • Interest from male dogs, drawn by pheromones in her urine

If You're Not Planning to Breed

VCA calls spaying before the first heat the best way to prevent pregnancy, and most vets recommend it before six or seven months of age, though some large breeds may benefit from waiting until after their first cycle — ask your vet. Spaying prevents pyometra, a serious uterine infection that Swedish insurance data found in about 24% of unspayed females by age 10, and in many cases reduces the risk of mammary cancer. If she is already in heat, Cornell recommends waiting about two months after the cycle before spaying.

Until then, keep her away from intact males for the whole heat, not just the days you think are fertile.

Timing a Breeding

A calendar can tell you roughly when a heat is due, but not when she ovulates. Progesterone blood tests at your vet's office track the hormone changes around ovulation; when ovulation is timed this way, Cornell puts the due date at 63 days, give or take 1–2.

Frequently Asked Questions

How often do dogs go into heat?

VCA Hospitals says most dogs cycle twice a year, small breeds may cycle three times a year and giant breeds once a year. Cornell gives an average interval of 5–11 months, with some breeds such as the Basenji and Tibetan Mastiff typically cycling once a year.

How long does a dog's heat last?

Cornell says the bloody discharge usually lasts 14–21 days. Within that, proestrus averages six to eleven days and estrus usually five to nine days, though estrus can range from one to twenty.

When is a dog most fertile?

During estrus. VCA notes ovulation usually happens around the time the discharge turns watery, and pregnancy is possible on any day of estrus because sperm can survive about a week. Progesterone testing by your vet is the way to time ovulation for breeding.

When does a dog have her first heat?

At about six months on average, according to VCA, though large and giant breeds may not cycle until 18 months to two years. Cornell gives a range of 6–24 months, with smaller breeds tending to start earlier.

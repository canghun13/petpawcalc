🐈 Cat Arthritis Screening Tool

Answer 6 quick yes/no questions from a validated veterinary screening checklist to check for signs of arthritis in cats. Takes about a minute.

Answer each question based on your cat over the past few weeks, and compare them with how they moved a year or two ago — not with other cats. Each question asks whether an activity still happens normally, so a "No" is the answer that gets flagged. This is a screening tool, not a diagnosis.

⬆️
Jumping Up
Does your cat jump up normally?
Watch for hesitation before the jump, choosing a lower surface than usual, or breaking one jump into two hops via a chair. Reaching the counter "eventually" is not the same as reaching it normally.
⬇️
Jumping Down
Does your cat jump down normally?
Jumping down loads the front limbs hard, so elbow pain often shows here first. Lowering themselves carefully, pausing at the edge, or landing with a thud instead of silently all count as not normal.
🪜
Climbing Up Stairs
Does your cat climb up stairs or steps normally?
A healthy cat usually bounds up stairs two at a time. Taking them one step at a time, pausing partway, or avoiding the upstairs floor entirely are all meaningful changes.
🔽
Climbing Down Stairs
Does your cat climb down stairs or steps normally?
Going down is harder than going up for a painful cat, because the front limbs absorb the weight on every step. Descending slowly, sideways, or reluctantly is worth flagging.
🏃
Running
Does your cat run normally?
Think about the last time your cat sprinted across the house or tore off after something. A stiff, short-strided trot in place of a real run, or simply never running any more, is a change.
🧶
Chasing Moving Objects
Does your cat chase moving objects (toys, prey, and so on) normally?
Reduced play is very commonly filed under "they are just getting older." A cat who still watches the toy intently but no longer pounces is often telling you the pounce itself has become uncomfortable.

⚕️ This is a screening tool, not a diagnostic tool, and it does not replace veterinary examination. Degenerative joint disease can only be confirmed by a veterinarian, and several other conditions common in older cats can produce similar changes in movement and activity. Never give your cat any pain medication that was not prescribed for that cat — many human and canine painkillers are toxic to cats.

How This Screening Was Built

The six questions above are the Feline Musculoskeletal Pain Screening Checklist, published by Enomoto, Lascelles and Gruen in the Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery in 2020. The researchers, working at North Carolina State University's pain research program, started from longer questionnaires already used by veterinarians and cut them down question by question, dropping the items that contributed least, until only six remained.

The underlying data came from more than 300 cats with and without confirmed joint pain. According to the researchers, shortening the questionnaire this far did not meaningfully reduce its screening accuracy compared with the longer parent version. The rule they proposed is deliberately simple: if any one of the six activities is answered "no," that should prompt further evaluation.

It was designed as a conversation starter between owner and veterinarian, not as a diagnosis. That is exactly how this page uses it.

Why Cats Are Screened Differently From Dogs

If you have used our Dog Arthritis Screening Tool, you may notice the questions here are not simply the cat version of the same list. That is intentional — the two species genuinely need different questions.

Radiographic studies illustrate the gap starkly: joint changes show up on X-rays in the majority of older cats, yet across several studies only a small minority — under 17% in the figures most often cited — showed any lameness their owner or vet could see. A screening built around limping would simply miss most of them.

Signs That Are Not on the Checklist (But Still Matter)

The six questions were chosen because they screen efficiently, not because they are the only signs that exist. Several others are worth mentioning to your vet even if all six items came back normal:

  • Grooming changes. A matted or greasy coat over the lower back and tail base often means twisting to groom there has become painful. Some cats go the other way and over-groom a single sore joint until the fur thins.
  • Litter box accidents. A high-sided box that used to be nothing can become a real obstacle. Missing the box, or going just outside it, is sometimes a mobility problem rather than a behavior problem.
  • Change in resting spots. A cat who has abandoned the top of the wardrobe for a chair, or who now sleeps on the floor, may not have changed preferences — they may have lost access.
  • Being less tolerant of handling. Reacting when picked up or brushed, hiding more, or becoming grumpy with other pets in the house can all be pain-driven.

This Tool vs. the Cat Quality of Life Calculator

PetPawCalc has two tools that both ask about how your cat is doing, and they are for different moments. This screening is for finding a problem you do not yet know about — a cat with no diagnosis, where you have a vague sense that something has changed but cannot name it.

The Cat Quality of Life Calculator (Paw Score) is for tracking a cat you already know is unwell — it scores hurt, hunger, hydration, hygiene, happiness and mobility over time, and is generally used alongside a veterinarian during ongoing care. If your screening here comes back positive and your vet confirms a diagnosis, the Quality of Life Calculator becomes the more useful of the two from that point on.

Weight Is Part of the Picture

Every extra pound on a cat is proportionally enormous — a 15 lb cat that should weigh 10 lb is carrying 50% more load through every joint on every jump. Weight management is a first-line recommendation in veterinary pain management guidelines for exactly that reason, and unlike most interventions it costs nothing and has no side effects. If you are not sure where your cat sits, our Cat Weight Calculator gives you a healthy range by breed, and the Pet Food Calorie Calculator has a weight-loss mode that works out target portions. Any weight-loss plan for a cat should be checked with your vet first — cats lose weight far more slowly than dogs must, because rapid loss risks hepatic lipidosis.

When to Skip the Screening and Call the Vet

This screening is built for gradual, chronic change measured in months. It is not the right tool for anything sudden. A cat who becomes acutely lame, cannot use a leg at all, drags a back leg, cries out when touched, or suddenly cannot stand needs to be seen the same day — sudden hind-limb weakness in particular can be an arterial clot, which is a genuine emergency. Likewise, a cat who stops eating for more than a day or two needs prompt attention regardless of what this screening says.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the signs of arthritis in cats?

The signs of arthritis in cats are almost never limping. Instead, cats keep doing the same activities but do them worse: hesitating before a jump, jumping onto a chair instead of the counter, taking stairs one at a time, running less, and losing interest in chasing toys. The validated screening above checks exactly those six activities, because they are the changes owners can actually observe at home.

How to tell if my cat has arthritis

How to tell if my cat has arthritis comes down to comparing your cat with their own past self rather than looking for obvious pain. Ask whether they still jump up, jump down, use stairs, run, and chase toys the way they did a year ago. If any of those is no longer normal, that is a positive screen worth raising with your veterinarian, who can examine your cat and decide whether X-rays or a treatment trial make sense.

Why is my cat not jumping up anymore?

A cat not jumping up anymore, or a cat who stopped jumping on the bed and counters they used to reach easily, is one of the single most common owner-reported signs of feline joint pain. Cats rarely refuse a jump outright at first — they hesitate longer, gather themselves, pick a lower landing spot, or break one big jump into two smaller ones. Because it happens gradually, it is very easy to write off as your cat simply getting older or calmer.

Is there a cat arthritis test at home?

There is no diagnostic cat arthritis test at home — degenerative joint disease is confirmed by a veterinarian through an orthopedic exam and often X-rays. What you can do at home is a validated screening like the one above. It does not diagnose arthritis, but it gives you a structured, research-backed way to decide whether the changes you are noticing are worth a vet visit, and it gives your vet something concrete to work from.

How common is arthritis in cats?

Very common, and very under-diagnosed. Researchers at NC State have estimated that around 45% of all cats and roughly 90% of cats over the age of 10 are affected by degenerative joint disease to some degree. Radiographic studies have found joint changes in the majority of older cats while only a small minority — in several studies under 17% — showed any lameness at all, which is exactly why owner observation at home matters so much.

Do cats limp when they have arthritis?

Usually not. Feline arthritis is very often bilateral — affecting the same joint on both sides — so there is no good leg to shift weight onto and no visible limp to spot. That is the core reason a dog-style check for limping misses most arthritic cats, and why this screening asks about jumping, stairs, running and chasing instead. A cat who is genuinely limping on one leg should be seen promptly, since that pattern points more toward injury than chronic joint disease.

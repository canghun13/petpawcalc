Contamination Audit — Do This Before Day One

Trials rarely fail because the diet was wrong. They fail because something else got into the pet. Tick everything you have actually dealt with — anything left unticked is a hole in the trial.

⚕️ This planner organises a protocol your veterinarian prescribes — it does not replace them. The trial diet itself must be chosen by your vet, since suitable diets are hydrolyzed or genuinely novel for your individual pet and most are prescription-only. Do not stop or change prescribed medication to run a trial, and never withhold food from a cat that refuses the new diet.

Why the Trial Has to Be This Strict

A food allergy is an immune reaction to a specific protein. The only way to prove one exists is to remove every protein the immune system already knows, wait long enough for the reaction to settle, and then see whether putting the old food back brings the signs straight back. There is no shortcut around that, which is why the protocol is so unforgiving: a single flavored chew in week six can restart the clock without you ever knowing it happened.

The duration is not arbitrary either. A 2015 critically appraised topic by Olivry, Mueller and Prélaud in BMC Veterinary Research examined how long elimination diets need to run, and the 8-week figure that dermatology practice settled on comes out of that line of work. Shorter trials miss cases — a pet whose signs would have resolved in week seven looks like a treatment failure if you stop at week four.

Food Allergy Is Less Common Than the Internet Suggests

It is worth going in with realistic expectations. Most chronically itchy dogs turn out to have environmental atopic dermatitis, fleas, or a secondary skin infection rather than a food allergy — food is one item on a longer list, not the default answer. A few patterns point more towards food than environment:

  • Year-round, non-seasonal signs. Environmental allergies classically flare with pollen seasons. Itching that never lets up regardless of month is more suspicious for food.
  • Skin and gut signs together. Itching plus chronically loose stool, frequent gas, or intermittent vomiting shifts the odds towards diet.
  • Onset very young or later in life. Adverse food reactions often begin before 1 year or after 7, while environmental atopy typically starts between roughly 1 and 3.
  • Recurring ear infections. Ears that keep flaring, especially with gut signs alongside, are a recognised pattern.

None of these is proof. They are reasons your vet may put a diet trial higher up the list — and none of them removes the need to rule out fleas first, which is why the audit above starts there.

Tracking Whether It's Actually Working

The benchmark dermatologists use is roughly a 50% reduction in itching behaviour — licking, scratching, chewing — by the end of the trial. That is much harder to judge from memory than it sounds, because improvement over eight weeks is gradual and you are with your pet every day. Scoring the itch on a fixed scale at the start and then weekly gives you a comparison that memory cannot: our Pet Itch Score Tracker uses the owner-assessed pruritus scale validated for exactly this purpose, and the planner's recheck dates are the points at which to record a score.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does an elimination diet trial take for a dog?

Most veterinary dermatologists run an elimination diet trial for at least 8 weeks in dogs, and often extend it to 12. Research summarised in Today's Veterinary Practice indicates that extending the trial to 8 weeks raises sensitivity for diagnosing food allergy above 90%. Trials for purely digestive signs are often shorter — commonly 3 to 4 weeks — because gut signs tend to resolve faster than skin signs. The planner above adds a 7-day transition period on top of the trial itself, so plan for roughly 9 to 13 weeks door to door.

How long does a food trial take for a cat?

Cats are usually given a longer trial than dogs — commonly 12 weeks for skin signs, versus 8 for dogs. Cats also present a practical problem dogs do not: they may simply refuse the new food, and a cat that stops eating for more than a day or two is at real risk of hepatic lipidosis. Never starve a cat into accepting a trial diet. If your cat refuses the food, stop and call your vet rather than waiting it out.

Why do most elimination diet trials fail?

Contamination, not the diet. The trial only works if the prescribed food and water are genuinely the only things entering your pet's mouth for the whole period, and the items owners forget are almost always flavored: chewable heartworm and flea preventives, flavored or compounded medications, dental chews, toothpaste, pill pockets, training treats, table scraps, and food stolen from another pet's bowl in a multi-pet household. The Tufts veterinary nutrition service has written specifically about this category of mistake. The audit in the planner above walks through each of these before you start.

Are blood or saliva tests for dog food allergies accurate?

Veterinary nutrition and dermatology specialists are consistently critical of blood and saliva tests marketed for diagnosing food allergy, describing them as unreliable for this purpose. The elimination diet trial followed by a challenge remains the reference standard for diagnosing an adverse food reaction. That is inconvenient — a test would be far easier than 8 to 12 weeks of strict feeding — but a test result that sends you eliminating the wrong ingredient costs more time than doing the trial properly once.

What is the challenge phase, and do I have to do it?

The challenge is where you reintroduce the original food after a successful trial and watch for symptoms to return. Without it, you only know your pet improved on a new diet — not why. Improvement could have come from the season changing, a flea treatment, or medication given alongside. A flare after rechallenge is what actually confirms a food reaction. Signs typically return within 1 to 3 days but can take up to two weeks. Not every pet should be rechallenged, and that decision belongs to your veterinarian.

Can I use a grain-free or limited-ingredient store food for the trial?

Generally not, and this is one of the most common and costly misunderstandings. Trial diets are either hydrolyzed — proteins broken into fragments too small to trigger a reaction — or built on a protein and carbohydrate your pet has genuinely never eaten. Over-the-counter limited-ingredient foods frequently contain trace proteins not listed prominently on the front of the bag, and "grain-free" is unrelated to protein allergy since the usual culprits are meat proteins rather than grains. Your veterinarian should choose the trial diet.

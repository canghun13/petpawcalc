🩸 Titer Test vs. Revaccination Cost Calculator

Compare the real cost of titer testing against standard revaccination for your dog or cat's core vaccines over time.

⚕️ This calculator compares cost only, not medical necessity. Rabies vaccination is legally required in most U.S. jurisdictions regardless of titer results — this tool covers non-legally-mandated core vaccines. Veterinary organizations are divided on how much weight to give titer results for determining actual protection; talk to your vet about whether titer testing fits your specific pet before switching from a standard vaccination schedule.

Where the Numbers Come From

  • Revaccination interval: every 3 years for core vaccines in healthy adults after the first-year booster, in both the 2022 AAHA canine and 2020 AAHA/AAFP feline guidelines.
  • Vaccine price: if you leave it blank, the national averages from a 2025–26 Synchrony study for CareCredit — $42 for DHPP and $34 for FVRCP. Exam fees are extra either way.
  • Titer price: ask your clinic, since it adds its own blood-draw and handling fees. For reference, the University of Wisconsin's CAVIDS titer lab charges $60 for a canine distemper/parvovirus panel and $31 for a single virus such as feline panleukopenia.
  • Retest interval: CAVIDS suggests about every three years for quantitative titers in most adult dogs and cats, and yearly for in-clinic yes/no screening tests.

Why Titer Testing Isn't Automatically Cheaper

  • A titer test plus the blood draw often costs as much as or more than one booster, so savings only come from boosters you genuinely skip.
  • If no antibody is detected, CAVIDS says the pet is very likely to benefit from a vaccine dose — so you pay for both.
  • Yearly in-clinic tests add up faster than a three-yearly lab titer.

What a Titer Can and Cannot Tell You

A titer measures antibody level in blood. That is genuinely informative for some diseases and much less so for others, and the distinction is the whole reason this is a conversation with your vet rather than a simple substitution.

  • Core viral diseases — canine distemper and parvovirus, feline panleukopenia — are what titer labs such as CAVIDS test for; it also tests for canine adenovirus (infectious hepatitis).
  • Leptospirosis, Bordetella, Lyme and influenza aren't candidates: CAVIDS doesn't offer titers for them because protective thresholds are unknown and antibody from these vaccines often reduces disease severity without preventing infection.
  • Rabies is not a choice. Rabies vaccination intervals are set by law rather than by immunology, and a rabies titer does not satisfy a legal requirement in the United States. Titer results are used for international travel documentation in some destinations, which is a separate purpose entirely.

CAVIDS explains that a titer at or above the protective threshold means the dog is considered protected; detectable antibody below it may lessen disease without preventing infection, and those animals may benefit from a vaccine dose. Your vet interprets the result.

Situations Where Titering Actually Changes the Decision

For a healthy adult pet on a normal schedule, titering usually costs more than it saves and changes nothing. It becomes genuinely useful in narrower circumstances:

  • A previous vaccine reaction. Where a pet has reacted badly, knowing whether they are still protected can justify not revaccinating.
  • An autoimmune condition or a pet on immunosuppressive medication, where your vet may want evidence either way before deciding.
  • Unknown history. A rescue with no records — titering can avoid vaccinating an animal that is already protected.
  • Boarding or facility requirements. Ask the facility whether it accepts titers before paying for one on that basis.

The honest summary is that titering is a tool for specific clinical questions rather than a general cost-saving strategy — and the cost comparison above is the arithmetic behind that. Which vaccines your pet needs, and at what interval, belongs to your veterinarian and depends on age, health, local disease prevalence and lifestyle.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a titer test?

A blood test that measures antibody to a specific virus. The University of Wisconsin's CAVIDS lab explains that a dog at or above the protective threshold is considered protected, while a pet with no detectable antibody is very likely to benefit from a vaccine dose.

Is a titer test cheaper than revaccination?

Not necessarily. A titer plus the blood draw often costs as much as a booster — the national average is $42 for DHPP and $34 for FVRCP — so it only saves money if results stay positive and you skip boosters. A negative result means paying for the test and the vaccine.

Can I use a titer test instead of the rabies vaccine?

Generally no. Rabies vaccination is set by law, and a titer doesn't replace a legally required rabies vaccine. Rabies antibody tests are used for some international travel, such as Japan's requirement of at least 0.5 IU/ml.

How often should I titer test my dog or cat?

CAVIDS suggests about every three years for quantitative lab titers in most adult dogs and cats, and yearly for in-clinic yes/no screening tests. Puppies can be tested within their first 6 months and again at a year.

Which vaccines can a titer replace?

Only some core vaccines. Titer labs such as CAVIDS test for canine distemper, parvovirus and adenovirus and feline panleukopenia. They don't offer titers for leptospirosis, Bordetella, Lyme or influenza, because protective thresholds aren't known. Rabies follows the law.

Motion Sickness, Anxiety, or Both?

VCA Hospitals lists the signs as whining and pacing, excessive drooling, lip smacking or licking, lethargy, vomiting and defecation. The two problems feed each other:

  • Motion sickness is more common in young dogs, because the balance structures of the inner ear aren't fully developed. Puppies often outgrow it by about 1 year old.
  • Anxiety is common in adult dogs that aren't used to riding in cars, and in dogs that ride only once or twice a year — usually to the vet — and learn to connect the car with what comes next. A frightening first ride can leave a puppy with lasting travel anxiety.
  • Repeated stressful trips can condition a dog to expect nausea and fear in the car, which can make vomiting and diarrhea worse.

VCA's Desensitization Steps

  1. Sit in the parked car with the engine running for a few minutes.
  2. The next day, back out of the driveway and return. Praise and reward with food.
  3. Take a trip around the block.
  4. Gradually build up to 20–30 minutes of comfortable riding, through several short trips before a long one.

Expect it to take days or weeks. If your dog shows visible anxiety, stop and try again another day — and never scold.

Making Trips Easier

  • Withhold food for up to 8 hours before travel, if that isn't too stressful, to reduce nausea and bathroom needs; offer water when you can.
  • Use a carrier or safety harness, and get your dog used to it before the trip.
  • Keep the car cool and quiet, with soft music.
  • Add a shirt or blanket that smells of you, and special toys used only for travel.
  • Mix in short drives that end somewhere enjoyable, so the car doesn't only mean the vet.

Medication: Ask Your Vet

VCA separates two kinds. Anti-nausea drugs — maropitant (Cerenia), whose single dose lasts 24 hours, dimenhydrinate and meclizine — treat motion sickness, not anxiety. Anti-anxiety drugs such as trazodone or alprazolam are separate prescriptions; some are given the night before and again before departure, and some need to start days to weeks ahead. Pheromone products and over-the-counter calming aids also exist. Discuss any of these with your vet first.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my dog has motion sickness or car anxiety?

They often overlap. VCA lists whining and pacing, drooling, lip licking, lethargy, vomiting and defecation as signs. Distress before the car moves points toward anxiety; nausea that starts once you're moving points toward motion sickness. Dogs that ride only to the vet often learn to dread the car itself.

Do puppies grow out of car sickness?

Often. VCA says motion sickness is more common in young dogs because the balance structures in the inner ear aren't fully developed, and puppies often outgrow it by about 1 year old. A frightening first ride, though, can leave lasting travel anxiety.

How do I desensitize my dog to the car?

VCA's steps: sit in the parked car with the engine running for a few minutes; the next day, back out of the driveway and return, with praise and food; then go around the block; then gradually build up to 20–30 minutes of comfortable riding. It can take days or weeks. If your dog looks anxious, stop and try again another day.

Should I feed my dog before a car ride?

VCA suggests withholding food for up to 8 hours before travel, if that isn't too stressful for your dog, to reduce nausea and the need for bathroom stops. Offer water when you can.

What medications help with car sickness or anxiety?

Ask your vet. Anti-nausea drugs such as maropitant (Cerenia) treat motion sickness but not anxiety; VCA notes a single maropitant dose lasts 24 hours. Anti-anxiety medications such as trazodone or alprazolam are separate prescriptions, and some need to start days or weeks before travel.

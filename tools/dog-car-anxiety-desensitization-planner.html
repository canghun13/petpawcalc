First: Anxiety or True Motion Sickness?

This matters because the two need different approaches. Many dogs have a mix of both, which is common enough to plan for.

Anxiety-driven True motion sickness
Drooling, panting, or refusing to get in before the car even moves Symptoms start once the car is moving — worse with acceleration, turns, stops
Pacing, whining, trembling tied to the car door or driveway itself Lip-licking, drooling, vomiting — a physical (vestibular/inner ear) response
Responds well to desensitization training alone Desensitization helps, but often needs a vet visit too
Common at any age Especially common in puppies — the inner ear isn't fully mature yet, and many puppies improve with age

An estimated 15% or so of adult dogs have true motion sickness. If that sounds like your dog — especially if they're already an adult — mention it to your vet alongside working through the plan below, since anti-nausea medication can make training sessions far more effective.

Smooth Signs vs. Warning Signs

Move to the next stage Go back a stage
Relaxed body language — loose posture, normal breathing, willing to take treats Heavy panting, drooling, trembling, or refusing treats
Walks toward the car or carrier on their own by the end of a session Bracing, freezing, or trying to escape
No vomiting or excessive lip-licking during or after a session Vomiting, even once — reduce the next session's intensity

Practical Tips That Actually Help

Positioning matters. Facing forward, able to see the direction of travel, tends to reduce motion sickness more than side-facing or rear-facing setups — the mismatch between what the inner ear feels and what the eyes see is the core mechanism behind motion sickness. Always in a secured crate or crash-tested harness.

Drive smoothly. Gentle acceleration, early braking, and avoiding sharp turns all reduce the physical trigger for nausea, especially during the early training stages.

Timing of food matters. A small, light meal 3-4 hours before travel is a common recommendation — a full meal right before or a completely empty stomach can both make some dogs more prone to nausea.

Ventilation and window covers help with overstimulation. Fresh air can ease nausea, while a window shade can reduce visual overstimulation for anxiety-driven dogs who react to passing scenery, people, or other dogs.

If the Car Only Means "We're Going to the Vet"

If car rides almost always end at the vet, groomer, or somewhere else stressful, a dog can learn that the car itself predicts something unpleasant — even if the ride was fine. The fix is to deliberately mix in short drives to purely fun destinations: a park, a friend's yard, or even just around the block and home again. This rebuilds the car as a neutral or positive experience rather than a reliable warning sign.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my dog has motion sickness or car anxiety?

Timing is the clearest clue. If drooling, panting, or reluctance to get in shows up before the car even starts moving, anxiety is likely the main driver. If symptoms like lip-licking, drooling, or vomiting begin once the car is actually in motion - especially with acceleration, turns, or stops - true motion sickness is more likely. Many dogs have some of both, which is common enough that it's worth addressing both angles.

Do puppies grow out of car sickness?

Often, yes. A puppy's inner ear (the vestibular system that controls balance) isn't fully developed, which makes true motion sickness considerably more common in puppies than adult dogs. Many puppies improve substantially as this system matures, on top of any benefit from desensitization training - though for dogs whose car trouble is anxiety-driven rather than physical, maturity alone won't resolve it.

Can desensitization training fix true motion sickness?

Not entirely on its own. Desensitization is very effective for anxiety-driven car trouble, but true motion sickness has a physical cause (the vestibular system) that training alone doesn't address. If you suspect true motion sickness - especially in an adult dog - talk to your vet, since anti-nausea medication can make training sessions far more successful by removing the physical discomfort that's undermining them.

Where should my dog sit in the car to reduce motion sickness?

Facing forward, able to see the direction of travel, tends to help more than side-facing or rear-facing positions. Motion sickness stems from a mismatch between what the inner ear senses and what the eyes see, and being able to see the road ahead reduces that mismatch. This should always be in a secured crate or a crash-tested harness, not loose in the car.

Should I feed my dog before a car ride?

A small, light meal 3-4 hours before travel is commonly recommended rather than a full meal right before departure or traveling on a completely empty stomach - both extremes can make nausea more likely for some dogs. Individual tolerance varies, so it's worth adjusting timing based on how your own dog responds.

Why does my dog only get anxious when we're going to the vet?

If car rides are almost always followed by something stressful like a vet visit, a dog can learn to associate the car itself with that outcome, even if the ride itself was fine. Mixing in short drives to purely fun destinations - a park, a friend's yard, just around the block and back - breaks that association and rebuilds the car as a neutral or positive experience rather than a predictor of something unpleasant.

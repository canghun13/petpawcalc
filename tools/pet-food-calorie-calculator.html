🍖 Pet Food Calorie Calculator

How much should you be feeding your pet? Enter their details below to get a daily calorie target based on their weight, age, and lifestyle — or switch to weight-loss mode if your dog or cat needs to slim down.

⚕️ This tool is for informational purposes only. Always consult your veterinarian before making significant changes to your pet's diet.

How This Calculator Works

It follows the 2021 AAHA Nutrition and Weight Management Guidelines. First it works out the resting energy requirement (RER): 70 × (body weight in kg)0.75. Then it multiplies by AAHA's life-stage factor to estimate daily needs:

AAHA notes that sedentary or indoor pets may need less than these factors suggest, so treat the result as a starting point and adjust by weight and body condition.

Senior Pets

AAHA's table has no separate senior factor. For dogs, VCA's veterinary nutritionists note that daily energy needs may fall by about 12–13% with age, so the calculator takes 12% off the adult figure — but very old dogs can need more again. In cats, AAHA's senior care guidelines note energy needs start rising again from about 10 years, so the calculator uses the adult factor for senior cats.

From Calories to Serving Size

Divide the daily calories by the kcal per cup or per can on your food's label. A dog needing 700 kcal a day on a food with 350 kcal per cup needs 2 cups a day, split into meals. Count treats as part of the total.

Weight Loss

For weight loss, AAHA bases calories on the pet's ideal weight, not its current weight: 1.0 × RER for dogs and 0.8 × RER for cats, adjusted as you go. A safe pace is about 1–2% of body weight a week for dogs and 0.5–2% for cats, with rechecks about every two weeks. Never crash-diet a cat — a cat that stops eating can develop hepatic lipidosis.

Sources: 2021 AAHA Nutrition and Weight Management Guidelines (Box 1 life-stage factors); AAHA weight management guidance on loss rates; 2023 AAHA Senior Care Guidelines; VCA Hospitals (feeding senior dogs); Cornell Feline Health Center. Full list on our sources page.

Why Isn't My Pet Losing Weight?

If you've been feeding the calculated amount for a few weeks with no change on the scale, work through these before assuming the plan isn't working:

  • Uncounted treats or table scraps — they add up quickly; count them in the daily total.
  • Someone else is also feeding the pet — common in multi-person households; agree on one feeding chart everyone follows.
  • The weight is out of date — reweigh at the start and about every two weeks, on the same scale (AAHA).
  • An underlying medical cause — if your pet isn't losing weight on a reduced intake, ask your vet to check for a medical reason.

Frequently Asked Questions

How are a pet's daily calories calculated?

The 2021 AAHA Nutrition and Weight Management Guidelines start with the resting energy requirement — 70 × body weight in kg to the power 0.75 — and multiply by a life-stage factor: for example 1.4–1.6 for a neutered adult dog and 1.2–1.4 for a neutered adult cat.

How many calories does a senior dog need?

Often a little less than a younger adult: VCA's veterinary nutritionists note that energy needs may fall by about 12–13% with age. But very old dogs can need more calories and protein to keep weight and muscle, so adjust by body condition.

How many calories does my cat need per day?

For a neutered adult cat, AAHA's factor is 1.2–1.4 × RER — about 260–300 kcal a day for a 10 lb (4.5 kg) cat — or 1.0 × RER for an inactive cat. Indoor cats may need less. Weigh regularly and adjust.

Should I follow the feeding guidelines on the pet food bag?

Use them as a starting point, then adjust by weight and body condition. AAHA notes that sedentary or indoor pets may need less energy than standard estimates suggest, and treats count toward the total.

How does the weight-loss mode work?

It follows AAHA: calories are based on the target (ideal) weight, at 1.0 × RER for dogs and 0.8 × RER for cats. It also estimates how long the loss would take at AAHA's suggested pace.

How fast should my pet lose weight?

AAHA suggests about 1–2% of body weight a week for dogs and 0.5–2% for cats, with rechecks about every two weeks. Cats must never be crash-dieted, because a cat that stops eating can develop hepatic lipidosis.

Does it check the nutrient balance of my pet's food?

No. It estimates calories only. Choose a complete and balanced food for your pet's life stage and ask your vet about anything else.

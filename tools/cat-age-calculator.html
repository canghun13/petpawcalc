How Cat Aging Works

A cat's first year equals roughly 15 human years. Their second year adds about 9 more. After that, each cat year is approximately 4 human years. This calculator follows the guidelines developed by the American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP).

Cat Life Stages at a Glance

The life stages used here follow the 2021 AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines, which define four age-based stages plus an end-of-life stage that can arrive at any age. Kittens (birth to 1 year) need high-calorie growth nutrition and a full vaccine series. Young adults (1–6 years) are usually in peak condition — this is when feeding habits and annual checkups do the most for long-term health.

Mature adults (7–10 years) benefit from routine bloodwork to catch kidney disease and hyperthyroidism early — both are common from middle age and far more manageable when found soon. Seniors are cats over 10, and most vets suggest more frequent checkups from here, often twice a year. Many cats live comfortably into their late teens with that kind of monitoring.

If you have seen charts that call a 1–2-year-old a "junior" or split older cats into "senior" (11–14) and "geriatric" (15+), those come from the earlier six-stage scheme. The 2021 update merged them into fewer stages because it made care plans simpler to follow — the underlying biology didn't change, so neither did the human-year conversion above.

Why Age-Appropriate Care Matters

Feeding a senior cat kitten food (or vice versa) can cause real problems over time. Calorie density, protein levels, and nutrient ratios are formulated for specific life stages. Knowing your cat's equivalent human age helps you have more productive conversations with your vet about what's normal — and what to watch for — at each stage.

Why Cats Age Differently Than Dogs

Unlike dogs, cat aging doesn't vary much by breed or size — a Maine Coon and a domestic shorthair age at roughly the same rate, unlike the large-vs-small breed gap seen in dogs. This makes the human-year conversion more consistent across the species, though individual health, indoor/outdoor lifestyle, and genetics still affect how gracefully a cat ages.

Indoor cats tend to live longer than outdoor cats — often 12–18 years versus 2–5 years for outdoor-only cats — due to reduced exposure to trauma, disease, and predators. This calculator's life stage guidance assumes a typical indoor lifestyle; outdoor cats may show physical signs of aging earlier than their calendar age suggests.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you convert cat years to human years?

Cats age rapidly in their first two years. A 1-year-old cat is roughly equivalent to a 15-year-old human, and a 2-year-old cat is about 24 in human years. After that, each cat year adds approximately 4 human years. So a 5-year-old cat is roughly 36 in human years.

At what age is a cat considered a senior?

Under the 2021 AAHA/AAFP Feline Life Stage Guidelines, a cat is a senior once it is over 10 years old. The guidelines use four age-based stages — kitten (birth to 1 year), young adult (1–6), mature adult (7–10) and senior (over 10) — plus an end-of-life stage that can come at any age. Older charts that put the senior threshold at 11 and add a separate geriatric stage at 15 come from the earlier six-stage scheme. Senior cats generally benefit from more frequent checkups, often twice a year rather than annually.

Do indoor and outdoor cats age differently?

Outdoor cats are exposed to more environmental hazards, which can affect health and lifespan. However, the aging formula itself doesn't differ by lifestyle — the human-year equivalent is based on biological age, not environmental exposure.

How old is my cat in human years?

Rather than the old "multiply by seven" shortcut, the converter above uses the standard veterinary approach: a cat's first year is roughly equivalent to 15 human years, the second year adds about 9 more, and each year after that adds roughly 4. So a 3-year-old cat is about 28 in human terms and a 10-year-old is about 56.

Does it work for kittens?

Yes, in half-year steps — enter 0.5 for a six-month-old. A cat's first year is worth roughly 15 human years and the tool spreads that evenly across the year, so six months comes out at about 8. Kittens actually develop fastest in the earliest months, so treat the figure at that age as a rough guide; the life-stage label is more useful than the exact number.

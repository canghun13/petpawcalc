Normal vs. Call the Vet: Incision Checklist

Check the incision once or twice a day during the recovery window. Most of what you'll see is completely normal — here's how to tell the difference from something that needs a call.

Normal (keep monitoring) Call your vet
Slight redness or pink color along the incision line Pus, cloudy, or foul-smelling discharge
Mild swelling or bruising that improves day by day Swelling that keeps growing instead of shrinking
Small scab or dried discharge right at the incision edge The incision edges separating or gaping open
Reduced appetite or low energy for the first 24-48 hours Not eating or drinking beyond 48 hours, or visible pain/crying
A little grogginess the evening of surgery Vomiting, diarrhea, or a swollen belly beyond the surgery site

Why You Can't Take the Cone Off Early

This is the single most common recovery mistake. The visible skin often looks closed by around day 7, but the tissue layers underneath are still knitting together for a week or more beyond that. A few minutes of licking at day 7 — when everything looks fine — can reopen the incision or introduce bacteria that wasn't a risk the day before.

Licking is also not the soothing behavior it looks like — it actively delays healing and is one of the most common causes of a reopened incision or infection requiring a second vet visit. Keep the cone on for the full window your vet recommends, even on days the incision looks perfect.

Cone vs. Recovery Suit

Cone (E-collar) Recovery suit
Protection Most reliable — physically blocks reaching the incision Good for most pets, but a determined licker can sometimes still reach through fabric
Airflow to incision Full airflow — nothing touches the site Less airflow, which can be a problem in humid climates or if the incision needs to stay dry
Comfort/stress Bumps into doorways and furniture; some pets adjust within a day or two Usually less stressful and easier to eat/drink/navigate in
Best for Persistent lickers, or any time the incision needs guaranteed protection Calmer pets, or as a supplement when the cone comes off briefly for meals (with supervision)

Ask your vet before switching to a suit instead of a cone — for some incision locations and some pets, the cone is the only option that reliably works.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a dog or cat need to wear the cone after spay/neuter?

Most vets recommend keeping the cone (E-collar) on for the full recovery window rather than removing it once the incision looks closed — typically 10-14 days for dogs and 7-14 days for cats depending on the procedure. A spay (abdominal surgery) generally needs the cone longer than a neuter.

Why can't I take the cone off once the incision looks healed?

The visible skin can close by around day 7 while the tissue underneath is still healing for another week or more. A few minutes of licking at that stage can reopen the incision or introduce bacteria, even though it looked fine moments earlier. The cone is protecting healing you can't see, not just the surface.

Why does my male cat need a special litter after neutering?

Cat neuters usually leave a small, open scrotal incision without external sutures, so standard clumping or dusty litter can stick to the wound or irritate it. Vets commonly recommend shredded paper or non-clumping pellets for about 5-14 days after surgery, until the incision has closed.

Is spay recovery longer than neuter recovery?

Yes, for both dogs and cats. Spaying is abdominal surgery (removing the ovaries and typically the uterus) while neutering only requires accessing the externally located testicles, so spay recovery generally involves a longer activity restriction and sometimes a longer cone period.

What's normal vs. what needs a vet call after spay/neuter surgery?

Mild swelling, slight redness, or a small amount of bruising in the first few days is normal and usually improves daily. Call your vet if you see pus or discharge, a foul odor, the incision separating or gaping open, swelling that keeps growing rather than shrinking, or your pet seems lethargic, won't eat, or is in obvious pain.

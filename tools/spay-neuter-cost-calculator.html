Why Spay Costs More Than Neuter

Spaying (ovariohysterectomy) is abdominal surgery — the vet accesses and removes the ovaries, fallopian tubes, and uterus through an incision into the abdominal cavity. It requires more surgical time, more anesthesia, and a longer recovery. Neutering (castration) is simpler because the testicles are externally accessible, making the procedure faster with less invasive access. This is why a spay is usually the more involved procedure.

For dogs, size matters too: larger dogs need more anesthetic and longer surgery, and clinics often price by weight. That's why the Synchrony averages — $455 to spay and $487 to neuter a small or medium dog, $353 and $262 for a cat — are best read as a starting point for larger dogs. In dogs the spay and neuter averages are close; in cats the neuter is clearly cheaper.

Private Clinic vs. Low-Cost Clinic

Low-cost and shelter-affiliated clinics often charge much less than private practices, typically by working at high volume and with nonprofit support. Ask what's included — some don't offer extras such as IV fluids or overnight care — and expect longer waits for an appointment.

Local humane societies, county programs and some national charities offer vouchers or fixed-price certificates; some are limited by income or residency.

How to Actually Find a Low-Cost Clinic

"Look for a low-cost clinic" is advice people hear constantly without being told where to look. Three places cover most of the country:

  • SpayUSA, run by North Shore Animal League America, keeps a national directory of reduced-cost programs. The ASPCA's low-cost spay/neuter page points owners to it rather than running its own nationwide search.
  • Your county animal services office. Many counties run voucher programs that are not advertised beyond their own website — residency and sometimes income criteria apply, and availability is often limited to a set number per month.
  • Your local humane society or SPCA. These run the highest-volume clinics in most areas, and their pricing is usually published.

Two practical notes: book early, because nonprofit clinics can be booked up weeks ahead, and check the paperwork they need — many ask for proof of rabies vaccination — before the day.

Ask What the Quoted Price Includes

This single question explains most of the gap between the number people are quoted and the number on the invoice. Clinics differ in what falls inside the base fee, and the same headline price can mean quite different things.

ItemAsk whether it's included
Pre-surgical exam, anesthesia and the surgeryThe core of every quote
Pain medication on the day and to take homeOften split — ask about both
E-collar (cone) or recovery suitVaries by clinic
MicrochipSometimes bundled; PetMD's price guide puts placement at $20–$75
Pre-anesthetic bloodworkOften extra; a chemistry panel averages $164 (dog) or $144 (cat) in Synchrony's study
IV catheter and fluidsVaries, especially at low-cost clinics

Bloodwork is the one worth understanding rather than just declining. Some clinics require it for older pets, so ask whether it applies to yours and budget for it from the start.

Ask for an itemised estimate in writing before you agree. It takes a minute on the phone and it is the only reliable way to compare two clinics, because a $250 quote that excludes bloodwork, the cone and take-home medication is not cheaper than a $350 quote that includes all three.

What Can Add to the Cost

Pre-surgical bloodwork is often recommended, especially for older pets, to check that anesthesia is safe. Retained testicles (cryptorchidism) need a more involved surgery, because the vet may have to search the abdomen for a testicle that hasn't descended. A pet in heat or pregnant at the time of surgery typically incurs additional fees due to increased surgical complexity and bleeding risk.

Many vets combine spay/neuter with other procedures at the same visit — most commonly microchipping, since the pet is already under anesthesia and it eliminates a separate injection later.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to spay or neuter a dog?

In a 2025–2026 national study of veterinary prices run for CareCredit by Synchrony, spaying a small or medium dog over 6 months old averaged $455 and neutering $487. Larger dogs usually cost more, as do dogs in heat and retained testicles. Shelters and humane societies often offer reduced-fee surgery; prices are set locally.

How much does it cost to spay or neuter a cat?

Synchrony's 2025–26 averages for cats over 6 months were $353 for a spay and $262 for a neuter. Low-cost clinics, shelters and trap-neuter-return programs for community cats usually charge much less; the ASPCA points owners to the SpayUSA directory to find them.

Is spay/neuter surgery covered by pet insurance?

Standard accident-and-illness pet insurance plans typically do not cover spay/neuter surgery, since it's considered elective preventive care rather than an accident or illness. Some insurers offer it as part of an optional wellness add-on for an extra monthly fee.

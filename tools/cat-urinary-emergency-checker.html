A cat straining in the litter box with little or nothing coming out is a life-threatening emergency — and it is routinely mistaken for constipation. Four questions will tell you how fast you need to move.

The Mistake That Kills Cats

A cat crouched in the litter box, straining, tail up, clearly working at something and producing nothing looks exactly like a constipated cat. Owners see it, think "he's blocked up," and decide to see how things look in the morning.

The AVMA addresses this directly: although it may seem like the cat is trying to pass stool, straining in the litter box is more often a sign that the urethra is blocked. And the consequences of the two are not remotely comparable. Constipation is uncomfortable and can usually wait for a normal appointment. A complete urethral obstruction backs urine up toward the kidneys, drives blood potassium to levels that affect the heart, and becomes life-threatening within roughly 24 to 48 hours.

The question that separates them: a constipated cat is still passing urine normally. A blocked cat is passing little or none. If you cannot tell, that uncertainty is itself a reason to be seen — not a reason to wait.

One practical tip that resolves the ambiguity fast: if you have a covered box or share boxes between cats, temporarily switch to a single open tray with a thin layer of litter, so you can see immediately whether anything is being produced and by whom.

Why Male Cats Are the Ones That Block

A male cat's urethra is longer and much narrower than a female's. The urethral plugs — soft material made of minerals, cells and mucus-like protein — and the small stones that a female would pass without much trouble can lodge and obstruct a male completely. The AVMA notes that urethral obstruction is most often found in male cats between one and ten years old, and neutering does not remove the risk.

This does not mean female cats can be ignored. Females get lower urinary tract disease at similar rates and are in real pain when they do; they are simply far less likely to become fully obstructed. A female cat with urinary signs still needs to be seen — just usually on a same-day rather than a same-hour timescale, unless she is showing systemic signs.

What FLUTD Actually Covers

Feline lower urinary tract disease is an umbrella term rather than a diagnosis. The signs are shared across the whole group — straining, frequent or prolonged attempts, crying out, excessive licking of the genital area, urinating outside the box, and blood in the urine — but the causes differ substantially:

  • Feline idiopathic cystitis (FIC). The most common cause in younger cats. There is no specific test for it and it is diagnosed by ruling other things out. Stress plays a substantial role.
  • Urethral obstruction. The emergency. Most often male cats, caused by plugs or stones.
  • Bladder stones and crystals. May need dietary dissolution or surgery.
  • Bacterial infection. Less common in young cats than owners expect, more common in older cats and those with kidney disease or diabetes.

Because these need different treatment and look the same from your sofa, the workup matters. Expect an exam, a urinalysis, and often bloodwork and imaging — our vet visit cost guide breaks down what a urinary workup typically runs.

After the Crisis: Reducing the Odds of a Repeat

Cats that have had one episode are at meaningful risk of another, and the evidence-backed response is environmental rather than pharmaceutical. A body of work from Ohio State on multimodal environmental modification (MEMO) found that restructuring a cat's environment reduced urinary signs — and, notably, reduced fearfulness and other stress-related problems alongside them. Frequently Asked Questions

My cat is straining in the litter box — is that constipation?

Assume it is not until a vet says otherwise. The AVMA notes that although straining in the litter box can look like a cat trying to pass stool, it is more often a sign that the urethra is blocked. This is the single most dangerous misreading in feline medicine: constipation is uncomfortable, while a blocked urethra kills within a day or two. The two look almost identical from across the room, so the question that separates them is what comes out — a constipated cat still passes urine normally, a blocked cat passes little or none.

How long can a cat go without urinating before it becomes dangerous?

Not long. Once the urethra is completely obstructed, urine backs up toward the kidneys and toxins accumulate in the bloodstream, and veterinary sources describe this becoming life-threatening within roughly 24 to 48 hours. Potassium rises to levels that affect the heart. There is no version of this that resolves on its own, and waiting overnight to see whether things improve is how cats are lost to a condition that is very treatable when caught early.

Why are male cats at higher risk of urinary blockage?

Anatomy. A male cat's urethra is longer and considerably narrower than a female's, so the mineral and protein plugs or small stones that a female would pass can lodge and obstruct completely. The AVMA notes urethral obstruction is most often found in male cats between one and ten years old, and neutering does not remove that risk. Female cats absolutely still get lower urinary tract disease and still need to be seen — they are simply far less likely to become fully blocked.

What are the signs of FLUTD in cats?

The recognised signs are straining to urinate, frequent or prolonged attempts, crying out while urinating, excessive licking of the genital area, urinating outside the litter box, and blood in the urine. Feline lower urinary tract disease is an umbrella term covering several causes, of which urethral obstruction is the emergency. A cat with an obstruction shows the same signs but passes little or no urine and becomes progressively more distressed, so urine output is the detail that matters most.

Can a cat's urinary blockage clear on its own?

No. A complete obstruction will not resolve without veterinary intervention, and home remedies, extra water, or waiting do not relieve it. Treatment usually involves sedation, placing a urinary catheter to relieve the obstruction, and fluid therapy to correct the metabolic consequences, often with a few days of hospitalisation. That is expensive — commonly quoted in the range of $1,500 to $3,500 — but it is a treatable condition when it is caught in time, which is the entire reason this page exists.

Is blood in my cat's urine an emergency?

Blood in the urine always warrants veterinary attention, and how fast depends on what accompanies it. Blood with normal urine volume in a cat that is otherwise bright is usually a same-day or next-day appointment rather than a middle-of-the-night drive. Blood together with straining and little or no urine output, or with vomiting, lethargy or hiding, is an emergency now. When you are not sure which situation you are in, phone an emergency clinic and describe what you are seeing — that call is free and they will tell you.

Warm First. Always.

This is the one rule worth learning before anything else, because the instinct it overrides is so strong. A tiny, crying, obviously hungry kitten makes you want to feed it immediately — and if that kitten is cold, feeding it can kill it.

A hypothermic neonate cannot digest. Circulation slows, gut motility essentially stops, and formula sits in the stomach undigested, which leads to regurgitation and aspiration. Neonates also cannot fix this themselves: kittens do not develop a shivering reflex until around two weeks of age and cannot regulate their own temperature until about four weeks. Left cold, they simply keep getting colder.

Warming has to be gradual — guidance from neonatal rescue organisations puts it at roughly one to three hours, and rewarming too fast causes its own problems. Practical points that come up repeatedly in shelter-medicine and rescue guidance:

  • Never place a neonate directly on a heat source. A heating pad on its lowest setting under two layers of towel, a warmed rice sock, or a covered warm water bottle. Always leave an unheated area they can move away to.
  • Heat lamps are not recommended for neonates — they burn and dehydrate.
  • Your own body is only a partial measure. Human skin sits well below the 100–102°F a kitten should be, so skin-to-skin helps but does not finish the job.
  • Hypoglycaemia runs alongside hypothermia. While warming, a drop of sugar water or corn syrup rubbed on the gums is commonly advised — that is absorbed through the gums, and is not the same as feeding.
  • Get to a vet even once they perk up. A neonate that went properly cold needs examining regardless of how well it seems to be responding.

Only once the animal is genuinely warm — active, rooting, with a suckle reflex — does the feeding plan below apply.

The Arithmetic Behind the Numbers

Two figures drive everything, and they pull against each other:

FigureValue used hereWhat it decides
Daily formula requirement~8 ml per oz of body weight per day (~28 ml per 100 g)Total volume across 24 hours
Comfortable stomach capacity~4 ml per 100 g of body weightCeiling on any single feed

Here is the conflict that static charts skip. Divide 28 by 4 and you get seven — meaning a neonate needs at least seven feeds a day just to fit its daily requirement inside its stomach. Most milk replacers sold in the US supply noticeably less than 1 kcal per ml, so the volume needed to meet energy requirements is larger still. Push that volume into fewer feeds and you exceed comfortable capacity, which is a recognised route to regurgitation, aspiration and diarrhoea.

So when the numbers do not fit, the answer is another feeding, not a bigger one. The calculator shows you the per-feed volume next to the capacity figure so you can see the margin, and tells you what the feed size would be if you split the day further.

To be straight about it: published feeding schedules themselves sometimes sit slightly above the comfortable-capacity figure, and the reference charts that quote both numbers say as much in their footnotes. That is a genuine tension in the source material rather than something a calculator can resolve for you — so this one shows both numbers instead of quietly picking a side. Regurgitation or loose stool is the signal to split the feeds.

Weight Is the Only Honest Feedback

A neonate that is crying may be hungry, cold, in pain, or dying. Behaviour will not tell you which. Weight will.

Weigh at the same time every day on a gram-accurate kitchen or postal scale, and write it down alongside how much was actually taken at each feed. A healthy neonatal kitten gains roughly 10–15 g a day. A day with no gain is a warning; any loss is a vet visit. Things That Are Not Formula

Cow's milk, goat's milk, evaporated milk and human infant formula are all wrong for a neonate and reliably cause diarrhoea — which, in an animal made mostly of water, becomes dangerous dehydration fast. Kitten milk replacer and puppy milk replacer are also not interchangeable. If it is the middle of the night and you have nothing, warming and a vet call come first; improvised milk is not a safe stopgap, and most areas have a rescue that will hand over a can at short notice.

After Every Feed: Stimulation

Neonates cannot urinate or defecate on their own — the mother triggers it by licking. Until roughly three to four weeks of age you have to do the same job with a warm, damp cloth or cotton pad on the genital area after each feed, until they go. Skipping it causes a genuine and avoidable medical emergency, and it is the step first-time fosters most often do not know about.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much formula does an orphaned kitten need?

The widely used rule is about 8 ml of formula per ounce of body weight per day, which works out to roughly 28 ml per 100 g per day. That total is then divided across the day's feeds rather than given at once. So a 150 g kitten needs about 42 ml across 24 hours — around 6 ml per feed if you are feeding seven times a day. Weigh the kitten daily on a kitchen or postal scale and recalculate, because the amount changes as fast as they grow.

Why can't I feed a cold kitten?

Because a hypothermic neonate cannot digest. When body temperature drops, circulation slows and gut movement essentially stops, so formula sits undigested in the stomach. Feeding at that point risks regurgitation and aspiration, and can kill an animal that warming alone might have saved. Neonates also cannot shiver to generate heat — kittens do not develop that reflex until around two weeks — so they cannot recover on their own. Warm first, gradually, over one to three hours, then feed.

How often should I bottle feed a newborn kitten?

Newborns under a week generally need feeding every 2 to 3 hours around the clock, which is 8 to 10 feeds a day including overnight. That stretches to roughly every 3 hours in weeks one to two, every 3 to 4 hours in weeks two to three, and every 4 to 5 hours by weeks three to four, at which point gruel can start being introduced. The overnight feeds are not optional for the youngest kittens — this is the part of fostering people most underestimate.

Can I give a kitten cow's milk or human baby formula?

No. Cow's milk, goat's milk, evaporated milk and human infant formula all have the wrong protein and lactose balance for a kitten and commonly cause diarrhoea, which in an animal this small can become fatal dehydration within hours. Use a commercial kitten milk replacer, and do not substitute puppy milk replacer for a kitten. If you genuinely cannot get formula tonight, contact a vet or a rescue rather than improvising — most areas have someone who will hand over a can at short notice.

What if the calculated amount is more than the kitten's stomach can hold?

Add a feeding rather than increasing the volume per feed. Comfortable neonatal stomach capacity is roughly 4 ml per 100 g of body weight, and most milk replacers sold in the US supply well under 1 kcal per ml, so the volume needed to meet energy requirements can exceed what the stomach comfortably holds. Overfilling risks regurgitation, aspiration and diarrhoea. The calculator above shows the per-feed volume next to the capacity figure, and where they conflict it tells you what the feed size would be if you split the day into more feeds.

How do I know if the kitten is getting enough?

Weight, not behaviour. A healthy neonatal kitten gains roughly 10 to 15 g a day, and should not go a full day without gaining. Weigh at the same time each day on a gram scale and keep a written log of weights and volumes taken. A kitten that stalls for a day, or loses weight at all, needs a vet rather than another adjustment at home — at this size there is very little margin, and weight loss is usually the first measurable sign that something is wrong.

🦴 Dog Arthritis Screening Tool

Answer 6 quick yes/no questions based on a validated veterinary screening checklist to check for early signs of arthritis in dogs. Takes about a minute.

Answer each question honestly based on your dog over the past few weeks, not just today. This is a screening tool, not a diagnosis — it is designed to help you decide whether a vet conversation is worth having, not to replace one.

🦵
Limping or Stiffness After Exercise
Does your dog limp, or seem stiff, in the period after exercise or a walk?
Post-exercise stiffness that eases with rest, then returns after the next walk, is one of the most commonly reported early signs of osteoarthritis in dogs.
😣
Signs of Pain
Setting limping aside — do you think your dog is showing any other signs of pain?
This is a general, gut-instinct question on the original checklist. Trust your read on your own dog, even if you cannot point to one specific behavior.
🪜
Reluctant to Climb Stairs or Jump
Is your dog reluctant to climb stairs, jump onto furniture, or jump into the car?
Reduced willingness to jump or use stairs is often one of the very first changes owners notice — sometimes before any obvious limping appears.
🛌
Difficulty Rising From Rest
Does your dog have difficulty getting up from lying down or sitting?
Owners often describe this as needing a "run-up," several attempts to stand, or being noticeably slower to rise after naps than after a night's sleep.
🔄
Change in Behavior
Have you noticed any change in your dog's behavior lately, compared with how they normally act?
Reduced play, more irritability, less enthusiasm for walks, or increased sleeping can all reflect chronic discomfort rather than simple aging.
🚶
Tires Easily or Lags on Walks
Does your dog tire more easily than they used to, or lag behind during walks?
Falling behind on a walk your dog used to keep pace with, or needing more rest breaks, can signal joint pain rather than simply losing interest.

How This Screening Was Built

This tool is based on a 2022 study published in the Journal of Small Animal Practice (Wright et al.), which tested a 9-question, owner-completed osteoarthritis screening checklist on 500 dogs. Every dog whose owner answered "yes" to at least one question was examined by a veterinarian, and osteoarthritis was confirmed in 38% of them (188 of 500) — a notably higher rate than earlier prevalence estimates, suggesting a real number of cases were going unrecognized by owners.

Across all 9 original questions, a "yes to any" rule caught about 99% of confirmed osteoarthritis cases, but also flagged a large share of healthy dogs (specificity around 37%) — useful for not missing cases, but not very efficient. The researchers found that narrowing the checklist to the 6 strongest-performing questions (dropping three based on prior injury, prior pain medication, and recent weight gain, which turned out to be weaker predictors) gave a better-balanced result: roughly 88% sensitivity and 71% specificity. Those are the exact 6 questions used above.

In plain terms: answering "yes" to even one of the six questions above was associated with correctly flagging about 88 of every 100 dogs who truly had osteoarthritis in this study, while about 71 of every 100 healthy dogs correctly screened negative. No screening tool is perfect — some dogs with early or mild osteoarthritis will screen negative, and some healthy dogs (especially seniors with unrelated aches) will screen positive. That is exactly why this is framed as a conversation-starter for your vet, not a diagnosis.

Why Weight Matters for Joint Pain

Weight management is not a minor add-on to arthritis care — it is a tier-1, first-line recommendation in the 2022 AAHA Pain Management Guidelines for Dogs and Cats, alongside exercise, and ahead of most medications and supplements on the guideline's priority list. Extra weight puts direct mechanical load on already-painful joints, and fat tissue itself produces inflammatory compounds that can worsen joint pain independent of the extra load.

The effect size is not small either: studies in overweight dogs with hip or elbow osteoarthritis have found measurable improvement in OA symptoms with as little as 6% to 18% body weight loss. If your dog screened positive above and is carrying extra weight, that is often the single highest-leverage change available before medication is even discussed with your vet. When to Seek Care Sooner Rather Than Later

This screening is built around gradual, chronic signs — the kind that build up over weeks or months. It is not meant for sudden changes. If your dog suddenly will not put any weight on a leg, is crying out, has a visibly swollen joint, or the change happened within a day or two (rather than gradually), that points toward an acute injury rather than chronic osteoarthritis, and it is worth contacting your vet promptly rather than completing a screening and waiting for a routine appointment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the signs of arthritis in dogs?

The signs of arthritis in dogs most commonly reported by owners are limping or stiffness after exercise, reluctance to climb stairs or jump, difficulty rising from rest, tiring easily or lagging on walks, and a general change in behavior such as increased irritability or withdrawal. These are exactly the six items this screening checks, based on a validated 500-dog study (Wright et al., 2022, Journal of Small Animal Practice).

How to tell if my dog has arthritis

How to tell if my dog has arthritis starts with watching for a pattern rather than a single moment — a dog who is occasionally stiff after a long hike is different from one who is stiff after every short walk and slow to rise afterward. The six-question screening above gives you a structured way to check for that pattern. A positive result is worth bringing to your veterinarian, since X-rays and a physical exam are generally needed to actually confirm osteoarthritis.

Is my dog in pain?

Is my dog in pain? Dogs hide pain far more effectively than most owners expect, so subtle behavior changes are often the earliest indicator, not obvious signs like yelping. Reduced enthusiasm for walks, more time spent resting, reluctance to be touched in certain spots, or general irritability can all reflect pain in a dog with no obvious limp. If this sounds familiar, use the screening above and consider a vet visit.

Is there a dog arthritis test at home?

There is not a diagnostic dog arthritis test at home — osteoarthritis is confirmed by a veterinarian through a physical exam and usually X-rays. What you can do at home is a validated screening like the one above: it does not diagnose osteoarthritis, but it gives you a structured, research-backed way to decide whether what you are seeing is worth a vet visit.

Why does my senior dog seem stiff after a walk?

A senior dog stiff after walk that eases within an hour, then returns after the next walk, is one of the clearest early patterns for canine osteoarthritis — it is exactly Q1 on the screening above. Occasional stiffness after unusually long or hard exercise is common and less concerning; stiffness after every walk, or stiffness that is getting more frequent, is the pattern worth screening for.

Why is my dog struggling to get up?

A dog struggling to get up from lying down or sitting is Q4 on the screening above, and it is one of the more specific single signs of osteoarthritis on this checklist. It commonly shows up as needing several attempts to stand, a stiff "walk it off" phase right after rising, or a growing reluctance to lie down on hard floors in the first place.

🚪 Is It Really Separation Anxiety?

Boredom, confinement distress, isolation distress, incomplete house training and several medical problems all look like separation anxiety from the wreckage. They need different plans. This sorts that out first, then gives a realistic starting point if it is the real thing.

🐾 PetPawCalc
⚕️ This is a screening aid, not a diagnosis. Separation anxiety is diagnosed by a veterinarian or behavior professional, ideally with video of your dog alone. Medical problems can cause similar signs, so see your vet first.

What Separation Anxiety Looks Like

The ASPCA lists house soiling, barking or howling, chewing or digging at doors and windows, escape attempts, pacing and eating feces — behaviours that happen when the dog is alone and not when the owner is there. Some dogs get agitated at departure cues such as picking up keys or putting on a coat, and distress often starts within minutes of being left. Common triggers include losing or being separated from a person, adoption from a shelter, a move, or a sudden change in how long the dog is left alone.

Dogs don't do this out of spite. The ASPCA is explicit that anxious behaviours aren't disobedience, and punishment usually makes the distress worse.

What to Rule Out First

  • Medical causes of indoor accidents: urinary incontinence from infections, hormone changes after spaying, bladder stones, diabetes, kidney disease, Cushing's disease, neurological problems, and some medications (ASPCA).
  • Other behaviours: incomplete house training, urine marking, submissive or excitement urination, juvenile destruction, boredom, and barking at sights and sounds that happens whether or not you're home (ASPCA).
  • Other anxieties: veterinary behaviorist Melissa Bain also lists generalized anxiety, noise phobia and confinement anxiety among the problems to consider.

Film It First

Bain writes that it's imperative to record a video of the dog alone. It shows when distress starts, whether the dog settles, and whether the trouble is about being alone or about being shut in. Some dogs destroy nothing and are still clearly distressed on camera — panting, pacing, trembling.

Crates

Crates help some dogs feel secure and stress others. If your dog pants heavily, drools, tries frantically to escape or barks constantly in a crate, the ASPCA suggests a room behind a baby gate instead.

Treatment

  • Mild cases: a food-stuffed puzzle toy, such as a frozen KONG, that lasts 20–30 minutes at each departure, picked up again when you come home (ASPCA).
  • Moderate to severe cases: gradual desensitization and counterconditioning over several weeks. Practise departure cues without leaving; then out-of-sight stays behind a door; then very short absences, lengthened slowly and only when the dog is calm. Shorten sessions at signs of stress such as panting, yawning, drooling, trembling or pacing.
  • Don't leave the dog alone beyond what it can cope with while you train — the ASPCA suggests a sitter, daycare or taking the dog to work.
  • Exercise and enrichment: daily aerobic exercise, ideally just before you leave, plus puzzle toys and chews.
  • Medication: fluoxetine (Reconcile) and clomipramine (Clomicalm) are FDA-approved for canine separation anxiety (Bain). The ASPCA notes medication can speed up progress and is usually combined with behaviour modification.

The ASPCA describes progress as slow — often only seconds at a time — until a dog can manage about 40 minutes alone, after which increases can be larger. For moderate or severe cases it recommends a board-certified veterinary behaviorist, a certified applied animal behaviorist, or a trainer experienced in fear-based problems.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I tell separation anxiety from boredom?

The ASPCA says separation distress usually happens only when the dog is alone and often starts within minutes of being left; some dogs get agitated at departure cues such as keys or a coat. Boredom and juvenile destruction are among the problems it lists to rule out. Filming your dog alone is the clearest way to tell — veterinary behaviorist Melissa Bain calls a video imperative.

Should I crate a dog with separation anxiety?

It depends on the dog. The ASPCA notes crates help some dogs feel secure but stress others. If your dog pants heavily, drools, tries frantically to escape or barks constantly in the crate, try confining it to one room behind a baby gate instead. Confinement anxiety is itself one of the problems vets consider.

What has to be ruled out first?

The ASPCA lists medical causes of house soiling — incontinence from infections, hormone changes after spaying, bladder stones, diabetes, kidney disease, Cushing's disease, neurological problems and some medications — and behaviours such as incomplete house training, urine marking, excitement urination, juvenile destruction, boredom and barking at outside sights and sounds.

How is separation anxiety treated?

For mild cases, the ASPCA suggests a food-stuffed puzzle toy at each departure. Moderate to severe cases need gradual desensitization and counterconditioning over several weeks — departure cues without leaving, then very short absences — and the dog shouldn't be left alone beyond what it can cope with in the meantime.

How long does separation anxiety treatment take?

The ASPCA describes progress as slow, often only seconds at a time, until the dog tolerates about 40 minutes alone; after that, increases can be larger. It recommends a board-certified veterinary behaviorist, certified applied animal behaviorist or experienced trainer for the process.

Is there medication for separation anxiety?

Yes. Fluoxetine (Reconcile) and clomipramine (Clomicalm) are FDA-approved for canine separation anxiety, according to Melissa Bain in Today's Veterinary Practice. The ASPCA notes medication is usually combined with behavior modification and can speed up progress. Ask your vet.

🚪 Is It Really Separation Anxiety?

Boredom, confinement distress, isolation distress, incomplete house training and several medical problems all look like separation anxiety from the wreckage. They need different plans. This sorts that out first, then gives a realistic starting point if it is the real thing.

🐾 PetPawCalc
⚕️ This is a screening aid, not a diagnosis. Separation anxiety is diagnosed by a veterinarian or qualified behaviour professional from history and video, not from damage. Medical problems including urinary tract disease, gastrointestinal disease, pain and cognitive dysfunction can all mimic it, so a veterinary examination comes first. For moderate or severe cases, work with a veterinary behaviourist or a trainer who specialises in separation anxiety — medication is a legitimate part of treatment for many dogs and that is a veterinary decision.

The Great Imitator

Coming home to a wrecked room and concluding "separation anxiety" is understandable and often wrong. Several different problems produce similar wreckage and need genuinely different plans, so starting a months-long anxiety protocol for a dog who is simply under-exercised wastes everyone's time.

One framing worth discarding entirely: dogs do not do this out of spite or revenge. Destruction during absences is an emotional or environmental problem, and treating it as disobedience leads directly to punishment, which the Humane Society and every behaviour source agree makes anxiety worse rather than better.

Film It Before You Train Anything

You cannot treat what you cannot see, and almost every mistake in this area traces back to guessing. An old phone on a shelf pointing at the door is enough. What the video gives you:

  • When distress actually starts. This is the single most useful discriminator between anxiety and boredom.
  • Whether they settle. A dog who is upset for two minutes and then sleeps is in a very different situation from one who paces for four hours.
  • Your starting threshold. The number the entire training plan is built on — and it is frequently far shorter than owners assume.
  • Whether the crate is the problem. Veterinary behaviour guidance is to film the dog alone outside the crate as the first step in separating confinement distress from separation anxiety.

Some dogs show almost no visible damage and are still suffering badly — panting, trembling, refusing food, pacing without pause. Those dogs are only found on camera.

Why the Crate Usually Isn't the Answer

Crating is the standard first response to destruction, and veterinary behaviourists report that most separation anxiety patients do not improve in a crate — many get worse, because confinement distress frequently accompanies separation anxiety. Dogs in that state pant, salivate, howl, soil and can injure themselves seriously trying to escape, including breaking teeth and cutting their mouths. There is also a logical problem with the usual justification: destruction is the outward expression of panic, and a protocol that keeps the dog below its anxiety threshold never reaches the level where destruction occurs, so the thing the crate was protecting against does not arise. Some individual dogs genuinely do settle better in a crate — but that should be established from video, not assumed.

The Requirement Everyone Skips

If there is one thing that determines whether this works, it is suspending absences: not leaving the dog alone for longer than they can currently handle, for the duration of the training. It is the central tenet of the leading protocols and, by their authors' own account, the most controversial and most commonly abandoned part.

The reason is arithmetic, not dogma. Practising under-threshold departures three times a week and leaving for eight hours on the days in between is not slow progress — it is a treadmill. Every full panic episode refills the bucket you have been emptying. The training and the management have to run together or neither works.

It is a genuinely big ask, and pretending otherwise helps nobody. It is also temporary, and it does not require you personally to be there — a housemate, a neighbour, a sitter, daycare or taking the dog with you all count. The related trap is confusing desensitisation with flooding. Leaving a dog alone and waiting for them to get used to it is not desensitisation — it is exposure above threshold, and pushing through the crying tends to make the fear worse rather than better.

How Long This Actually Takes

Months, usually. Starting durations are measured in seconds — specialists describe starting at whatever the dog can handle without stress, "even if that is literally ten seconds", and treat reaching a one-minute absence as a milestone worth celebrating. That should calibrate your expectations, and it should also make you sceptical of anything advertising a fix in a fixed short window. The pace is set by the dog's emotional state, not by a schedule, and going backwards a step after a bad session is part of the method rather than a failure of it.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I tell separation anxiety from boredom?

Timing and context. True separation anxiety usually shows itself within the first 10 to 30 minutes of you leaving — pacing, panting, drooling, howling, scratching at exits. A bored dog typically sleeps for the first stretch, wakes up later, wanders, and then dismantles something out of sheer lack of anything to do. The other test is presence: boredom-driven chewing happens whether or not you are home, so a dog who destroys the sofa while you are in the next room is under-stimulated rather than panicking. Dogs with genuine separation anxiety are usually calm when you are there.

What is confinement anxiety and how is it different?

Confinement anxiety, also called barrier frustration, is panic about being shut in a crate or small room — and the giveaway is that it happens whether or not a person is home. Veterinary behaviour sources describe the first step in telling the two apart as filming the dog home alone outside the crate, loose or confined to part of the house. It matters because the treatment differs, and because crating a dog with confinement distress can lead to real injury: dogs break teeth and cut their mouths trying to escape.

Should I crate a dog with separation anxiety?

Generally no, and this surprises people because crating is the usual first response to destruction. Veterinary behaviourists report that most separation anxiety patients do not improve in a crate and often get worse, because confinement distress frequently travels with separation anxiety. The reasoning behind crating also falls apart under a proper protocol: destructive behaviour is the outward expression of panic, and a protocol that keeps the dog under its anxiety threshold never reaches the level where destruction happens. Some dogs do settle better in a crate, but that has to be established from video rather than assumed.

Why does my dog's separation anxiety training keep failing?

Almost always because absences above threshold are continuing alongside the training. Practising two-minute departures three times a week and then leaving for eight hours on the days in between is not progress — each full panic episode undoes the work. This is why suspending absences is the central requirement of the leading protocols, and it is also the part owners most often skip. The other common failure is confusing desensitisation with flooding: leaving the dog alone and waiting for them to get used to it is not desensitisation, and pushing through the crying tends to make things worse.

How long does separation anxiety treatment take?

Months, typically, and starting durations are measured in seconds rather than minutes. Trainers who specialise in this describe reaching a one-minute absence as a milestone worth celebrating, which tells you a great deal about the pace. Be sceptical of anything promising to fix it in a fixed short window — the protocol works at the dog's pace, and the primary data point is the dog's emotional state rather than the clock. Progress is not linear either; going backwards a step when a session goes badly is part of the method, not a failure of it.

Do I need a camera?

Yes — it is the single most useful thing you can do, and it comes before any training. You cannot treat what you cannot see. Video tells you when distress starts, whether the dog settles after a few minutes or stays distressed the whole time, and whether the behaviour matches separation anxiety or one of the mimics. It also gives you your starting threshold, which is the number the entire plan is built on. An old phone propped up facing the door is enough.

The heat index and its categories are the National Weather Service's, made for people; they're shown because UK research found dog heatstroke cases were about five times higher on human heat-health alert days. Heatstroke is an emergency — cool your dog and call your vet.

Walks Cause More Heatstroke Than Hot Cars

A Royal Veterinary College study of UK veterinary records (Hall et al., 2020) found that 74% of heatstroke cases with a known trigger followed exercise, and most of those followed nothing more strenuous than a walk. Hot cars accounted for about 5%. A 2024 study of UK emergency vet cases found daily heatstroke cases during official heat-health alerts were about five times those on other summer days, and that flat-faced breeds were about four times as likely to develop heatstroke as dogs with normal-length muzzles.

Cornell's Riney Canine Health Center advises walking in the early morning or evening, and avoiding strenuous exercise in the hottest part of the day (around 3–5 p.m.), on very humid days, and early in summer before dogs have adjusted to the heat.

Breeds and Dogs at Higher Risk

Compared with Labrador Retrievers, Hall et al. found these breeds had higher odds of exercise-related heatstroke: Chow Chow (about 7 times), Bulldog (3.7), French Bulldog (3.0), Greyhound (2.1), English Springer Spaniel (1.9), Cavalier King Charles Spaniel (1.7) and Staffordshire Bull Terrier (1.6). Dogs weighing 10–20 kg (about 22–44 lb) and over 50 kg (about 110 lb) also had higher odds than dogs under 10 kg, and dogs 12 or older were about three times as likely to develop heatstroke from sitting outside in the heat.

Cornell adds overweight dogs, older dogs, dogs with heart or breathing conditions, and dogs with thick or dark coats as needing extra caution, and notes that all dogs can get heatstroke.

Check the Pavement

Cornell's test: if you can't hold your hand on the pavement, or stand on it barefoot, for about 10 seconds, it's likely too hot for your dog's paws. Walk on grass or shaded paths instead.

Signs of Heatstroke and What to Do

Cornell lists the early signs as heavy panting, seeking shade, whining, reluctance to play and drooling. Difficulty breathing, bloody diarrhea, vomiting, weakness, confusion, seizures or collapse mean your dog needs a vet immediately.

Cool first, transport second. The Royal Veterinary College advises cooling your dog straight away with whatever water you have, as long as it's cooler than the dog — the old advice to use only tepid water isn't supported by evidence. Cold-water immersion suits young, healthy dogs; for older dogs or dogs with health problems, pour water over them and add airflow from a fan, breeze or air conditioning. Wet towels laid on the dog work much less well. Then call your vet and get there as soon as you can.

In earlier RVC research, 97% of dogs with mild heat-related illness survived, compared with 43% of dogs with full heatstroke — so acting early matters.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there a safe temperature for walking a dog?

No validated cutoff exists. Risk depends on humidity, exertion, the dog and how used to the heat it is. UK research found most heatstroke followed exercise, often just a walk, and that cases were about five times higher on human heat-health alert days. Cornell advises walking in the early morning or evening.

Why does humidity matter as well as temperature?

Humidity raises the heat index, the National Weather Service's measure of how hot it feels. Cornell lists very humid days, along with the hottest hours, as times to avoid strenuous exercise with a dog.

How do I know if the pavement is too hot for my dog's paws?

Cornell's test: if you can't hold your hand on the pavement, or stand on it barefoot, for about 10 seconds, it's likely too hot for your dog. Walk on grass or shaded paths instead.

What are the early signs of heatstroke in dogs?

Cornell lists heavy panting, seeking shade, whining, reluctance to play and drooling as early signs. Difficulty breathing, bloody diarrhea, vomiting, weakness, confusion, seizures or collapse need a vet immediately.

How should I cool a dog with heatstroke?

The Royal Veterinary College says cool first, transport second: use whatever water you have as long as it's cooler than the dog. Immersion suits young, healthy dogs; for older or unwell dogs, pour water over them and add airflow. Wet towels work much less well. Then get to a vet.

Which dogs are most at risk of heatstroke?

In UK veterinary records, Chow Chows, Bulldogs, French Bulldogs, Greyhounds, Springer Spaniels, Cavaliers and Staffordshire Bull Terriers had higher odds of exercise-related heatstroke than Labradors, and flat-faced dogs had about four times the risk of other dogs in emergency cases. Cornell adds overweight and older dogs, heart or breathing conditions, and thick or dark coats.

Where This Scale Comes From

Measuring itch is genuinely difficult — the animal cannot report it, and the behaviour is intermittent. The scale used here is the owner-assessed pruritus visual analog scale, developed by Hill, Lau and Rybníček and published in Veterinary Dermatology in 2007. They tested four different formats on 116 owners before settling on a visual analog scale carrying behavioural descriptions rather than bare numbers.

It was validated further by Rybníček and colleagues in 2009 in a study of 713 owners presenting dogs for veterinary attention. Scores from the 408 dogs with skin disease covered the entire 0 to 10 range. Notably, among 305 dogs with no evidence of skin disease, 90 owners still gave a score above zero — a useful reminder that some scratching is simply normal and a low score is not a failure.

The scale has since become the standard owner-reported outcome measure in canine dermatology trials, which is why it is worth using at home: it produces a number your veterinarian already knows how to interpret.

Why the Numbers Are Hidden Until You Commit

This is the detail most versions of this scale on the internet get wrong. The 2009 validation study directly compared the visual analog scale against one with visible numerical markings, and found that owners were heavily influenced by the numbers — the marked version pulled responses toward round figures and lost the scale's ability to generate continuous data. The authors concluded that a traditional visual analog scale was essential. So this tool shows you descriptions while you place the marker and only converts to a number afterwards. If you would rather use a straightforward 0–10 verbal scale, one was validated separately in 2022 in 251 dogs; it correlates well with this scale but the authors specifically cautioned that the two are not interchangeable, so pick one and stay with it across all your scores.

Scoring Cats

The original scale was built and validated for dogs. A separate visual analog scale for feline pruritus has been developed and shown able to track improvement following treatment, but cats are harder to score for a practical reason: much of their scratching and over-grooming happens out of sight, and the first sign is often the result rather than the act — thinning fur on the belly or inner thighs, bald patches behind the ears, scabs along the back. If you have a cat, factor in what the coat tells you as well as what you have actually watched them do, and weight indoor observation heavily since an outdoor cat's daytime behaviour is invisible to you.

Getting a Useful Trend

  • Score at the same time of day. Itching is worse in the evening for many pets, so an evening score one week and a morning score the next produces a change that is not real.
  • Weekly, not daily. Daily scoring mostly captures noise. Weekly captures trend.
  • Record a baseline before anything changes. A score taken after starting treatment has nothing to be compared against.
  • Write down what else changed. A new flea treatment, a course of steroids, a bath, or pollen season starting will all move the number, and without a note you will not remember which.
  • Keep prior scores in front of you. A 2019 study in Veterinary Dermatology examined how owners' scores behave at follow-up visits, and showing previous scores has been found to improve agreement between what owners perceive and what they record.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my dog is itching too much?

Some scratching is normal — in the validation research for this scale, a number of owners of dogs with no skin disease at all still gave a score above zero. The threshold that matters is not whether your dog scratches, but whether scratching, licking or chewing has started interrupting normal life: waking them at night, stopping play, or continuing while they eat. Itching that reaches that level, or that has visibly damaged the skin, is worth a vet visit rather than another shampoo.

Why doesn't the slider show numbers while I score?

That is deliberate, and it comes straight from the research. When the scale was further validated in 2009, the authors found that owners were heavily influenced by visible numerical markings — adding numbers pulled scores toward round figures and lost the fine gradation the scale is designed to capture. A true visual analog scale with descriptions rather than numbers produced better data, so this tool hides the number until after you have placed the marker.

What counts as a meaningful improvement in itch score?

In the context of an elimination diet trial, veterinary dermatologists generally look for roughly a 50% reduction in itching behaviour — licking, scratching, chewing — to call the trial a response. For medication, improvement is usually judged both by the drop in score and by whether the pet has come back under a level where the itching interferes with sleep and normal activity. Because a single score is just one day, a trend across several weeks is far more informative than any individual number.

Is this the same as a diagnosis?

No. This scale measures how severe the itching is, not what is causing it. The same score can come from fleas, environmental allergy, a food reaction, a skin infection, mites or several of these at once — and those are distinguished by veterinary examination and testing, not by severity. What the score gives you is a way to measure change over time, which is exactly the thing owners are worst at judging from memory.

How often should I score my pet's itching?

Weekly is enough for most situations, plus a baseline score on the day a treatment or diet trial begins. Daily scoring tends to capture normal fluctuation rather than real trend, and can be discouraging during the early weeks of a food trial when little has changed yet. Score at roughly the same time of day, since itching in many pets is worse in the evening.

🦷 Dental Cleaning Cost Calculator

Estimate the total cost of a professional dental cleaning for your dog or cat, including anesthesia, bloodwork, and possible extractions.

🐾 PetPawCalc

⚕️ This calculator provides general cost estimates based on national averages. Actual pricing varies by location, clinic type, and your pet's specific dental findings, which are only confirmed once your vet examines the mouth and takes dental X-rays. Always request a written estimate before scheduling.

Where the Numbers Come From

National averages from a 2025–2026 study of veterinary practices across all 50 states, run for CareCredit by Synchrony: a routine cleaning averages $379 for a dog and $395 for a cat, and a chemistry panel — the usual pre-anesthetic blood test — $164 and $144. For cats, the study also lists dental X-rays at $133 and extractions at $99 per tooth; it doesn't give those figures for dogs, so the calculator uses the cat figures as a default you can change. Dog extractions of large, multi-rooted teeth are often priced higher, so replace them with your clinic's estimate if you have one.

Why Extractions Drive the Final Bill

A quoted cleaning price usually assumes no extractions, because nobody knows how many teeth are affected until the pet is under anesthesia and the X-rays are taken. The 2019 AAHA Dental Care Guidelines strongly recommend full-mouth X-rays for every dental patient, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual — they're how problems below the gumline, such as tooth resorption in cats, are found. Ask your vet to quote X-rays and likely extractions as separate lines, based on what they can see at the awake exam.

Why Anesthesia Is Part of a Real Cleaning

The American Veterinary Dental College says cleaning inside the gum pocket — where periodontal disease is active — is impossible in an awake dog or cat, that removing visible tartar alone is "purely cosmetic", and that anesthesia with a breathing tube prevents pain and protects the airway. The Merck Veterinary Manual likewise notes that cleaning an awake animal improves how the teeth look but not periodontal health.

How Often Is a Cleaning Needed?

There's no fixed interval; it depends on what your vet finds at checkups. Periodontal disease starts early — the Merck Veterinary Manual says up to 80% of dogs and 70% of cats have some by age 2 — and toy breeds are at higher risk.

Home Care Between Cleanings

According to the Merck Veterinary Manual, brushing daily or at least every other day appears to be the most effective home care; in most pets only the outer surfaces of the teeth need brushing, and pets that won't accept a brush can have their teeth wiped with gauze. Firm, fibrous chews can help, but hard items such as bones and hooves can crack teeth.

Products that meet the Veterinary Oral Health Council's standard carry its seal. To earn it, a product has to show a statistically significant reduction in plaque or tartar of at least 15% in each of two trials and 20% on average across them, according to VOHC's trial protocol.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does dog teeth cleaning cost?

A routine dental cleaning averages $379 for a dog in a 2025–2026 national study of veterinary prices run for CareCredit by Synchrony. Pre-anesthetic bloodwork (a chemistry panel averages $164), dental X-rays and any extractions are usually billed on top, which is why the final bill is often higher than the estimate.

How much does cat teeth cleaning cost?

A routine cleaning averages $395 for a cat in Synchrony's study, with dental X-rays at $133 and extractions at $99 per tooth. Cats are prone to tooth resorption, which usually means extractions, so ask for X-rays and extractions to be quoted separately.

Is anesthesia-free dental cleaning a cheaper alternative?

Not for your pet's dental health. The American Veterinary Dental College says cleaning under the gumline is impossible in an awake dog or cat and that scraping visible tartar is purely cosmetic; it also notes that dental procedures by anyone other than a licensed veterinarian or supervised technician are unlicensed practice in the US and Canada.

How often should I brush my dog or cat's teeth at home?

Daily, or at least every other day, according to the Merck Veterinary Manual, which calls it the most effective home care for periodontal health. Only the outer surfaces of the teeth usually need brushing, and gauze can be used for pets that won't accept a brush. Use a pet toothpaste, not a human one.

What does the VOHC seal on a dental product mean?

The Veterinary Oral Health Council gives its seal to dental chews, diets, water additives and other products that show a statistically significant reduction in plaque or tartar — at least 15% in each of two trials and 20% on average across them, under VOHC's trial protocol. It's a useful way to sort products with evidence behind them from marketing claims.

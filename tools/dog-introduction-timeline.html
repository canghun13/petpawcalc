⚕️ If either dog has a history of fighting, the ASPCA recommends consulting a veterinary behaviorist or certified professional dog trainer before introducing them.

Before the First Meeting

  • Put away anything the resident dog might guard — food bowls, bones, chews, toys and beds — and clear narrow, crowded spaces (ASPCA).
  • Line up one helper per dog.
  • Choose neutral ground, such as a park, rather than your home or yard.

The First Meeting

  • Keep leashes loose during the greeting sniff — tension on the leash can cause scuffles.
  • Keep the first interactions brief, then walk the dogs together in parallel (ASPCA), in the same direction and far enough apart that neither is fixated on the other (Purdue). Swapping sides lets each sniff where the other walked.
  • If both stay relaxed, let them move closer.
  • With several resident dogs, introduce each one to the newcomer separately first.

Warning signs: raised hackles, growling, showing teeth, prolonged staring and snapping. Call the dogs apart early by luring each away with a high-value treat rather than pulling on the leash, and keep your own body language relaxed (ASPCA). Minor pushing, growling or standing over the shoulders during play is usually fine.

Bringing the New Dog Home

  • Walk home together.
  • Bring the new dog in first on a leash to explore, after checking no food or toys are out. Bring the resident dog in on a leash once the newcomer is relaxed, and let the resident dog off leash first (ASPCA).
  • Each dog gets its own food bowl, crate, bed and toys. Feed far apart or in separate crates; never crate the dogs together, and avoid small spaces such as cars until they're fully comfortable.
  • Supervise every interaction, and watch for flashpoints in the first few weeks: arrivals, guests, yard trips, walks, meals and play.
  • Keep routines the same, and give each dog one-on-one time with you.

The one-week rule: the ASPCA says not to leave the dogs alone together until they've had no tense or negative interactions for at least one week — some dogs need longer.

Puppies and Adult Dogs

A puppy will often lie down or roll over at the first meeting, and a well-socialized adult will investigate, then play or ignore it (ASPCA). Purdue's guide says a soft growl or grumble from the adult is an acceptable correction as long as there's no contact that hurts the puppy. Give the adult regular breaks, make sure the puppy gets enough exercise, and use a gate or crate when you can't supervise. Don't hold a small puppy up for the resident dog to greet — stand with your feet apart so the puppy can retreat between them.

If the Dogs Fight

Don't let them "fight it out." Interrupt it without putting your hands between them — the ASPCA lists a loud noise, water, an air horn or citronella spray, or carefully separating them by the leash or hind ends. Get veterinary care if needed and separate them for several hours. Then keep them completely apart with crates or secure gates and get help from a behavior professional before trying again.

Sources: ASPCA, Introducing Dogs to Each Other; Purdue University Center for Canine Welfare Science, Introducing Dogs to Other Dogs. Full list on our sources page.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where should two dogs meet for the first time?

On neutral ground. The ASPCA advises an open area such as a park rather than the home or yard, where the resident dog may feel territorial, with one person handling each dog on a loose leash. Introduce multiple resident dogs to the newcomer one at a time.

How long does it take to introduce two dogs?

There's no fixed timeline. The ASPCA's benchmark is that dogs shouldn't be left alone together until they've gone at least one week with no tense or negative interactions — and some dogs need longer.

Why walk the dogs side by side?

The ASPCA suggests keeping the first greeting brief and then walking the dogs together in parallel, which takes pressure off them. Purdue's Canine Welfare Science guide adds walking them in the same direction far enough apart that neither is fixated on the other.

Which dog should go into the house first?

The ASPCA suggests bringing the new dog in first on a leash to explore, after checking that no food or toys are left out, then bringing the resident dog in on a leash once the newcomer is relaxed, and letting the resident dog off leash first.

What if the dogs fight during the introduction?

Interrupt it without putting your hands between them, get veterinary care if needed, and separate the dogs. The ASPCA advises keeping them completely apart with crates or secure gates and getting help from a behavior professional before trying again.

🐱 Cat Carrier Training Planner

Enter your vet appointment date to get a countdown built on the American Association of Feline Practitioners' carrier training steps — what to start now, what to do on the day, and how to bring your cat home calmly.

🐾 PetPawCalc

⚕️ Ask your veterinarian before using any calming medication or supplement. If your cat vomits or drools on car trips, ask about an anti-nausea medication.

The Right Carrier

  • The AAFP recommends a hard plastic carrier with a top that comes off, so the vet can examine your cat in the bottom half. VCA adds that removing the top lets you reach your cat while it stays where it feels secure.
  • Soft carriers need a large top opening and one or, better, two end doors that don't collapse when open.
  • Avoid backpack-style carriers, and never travel with only a harness and leash.
  • Each cat needs its own carrier, even cats that get along.

Make the Carrier Ordinary

Keep the carrier where your cat likes to spend time — not in the garage or a closet — with a familiar blanket and some treats inside (AAFP). VCA suggests leaving it out all the time so your cat naps and eats in it.

The AAFP's Six Training Steps

  1. Encourage your cat to lie on a security blanket.
  2. Put the blanket in the bottom of the carrier.
  3. Add the top and encourage calm behaviour.
  4. Encourage your cat to stay inside with the door closed.
  5. Gradually increase the time in the closed carrier.
  6. Lift the carrier from the bottom — never just by the handle — and help your cat get used to being carried.

Spend enough time on each step before moving to the next. The AAFP's Cat Friendly site has videos of each step.

On the Day

  • Spray a calming pheromone on the blanket 15 minutes to 4 hours before travel, with your cat out of the room (AAFP).
  • Ideally your cat walks in by itself for a treat or toy. If not, place it in gently and calmly; VCA suggests removing the top, laying your cat on its blanket and putting the top back on.
  • Cover the carrier with a towel before carrying it to the car, and secure it on the floor between the front and back seats.
  • Keep the car ventilated and quiet, and don't open the carrier during the trip, even if your cat cries.

Bringing Your Cat Home

Keep the returning cat in the closed carrier at first and watch how the other cats react. If there's hissing, give it a separate room with food, water, a litter box and a bed, and swap bedding between the cats. Allow contact slowly after a few hours — longer after surgery, as your vet advises (AAFP).

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my cat panic as soon as they see the carrier?

Often because the carrier only appears before a car ride and a vet visit. The AAFP suggests keeping it out where your cat likes to spend time, with a familiar blanket and treats, and VCA suggests leaving it out all the time so your cat can nap and eat in it.

Does the type of carrier matter?

Yes. The AAFP recommends a hard plastic carrier with a top that comes off, so the vet can examine your cat while it stays in the bottom half. Soft carriers need a large top opening and ideally two end doors. Avoid backpack-style carriers, and never travel with only a harness and leash.

How far in advance should I use a calming pheromone spray?

The AAFP says calming pheromone products can be sprayed on blankets 15 minutes to 4 hours before travel, keeping your cat away while you spray. VCA says to spray 15 minutes before your cat goes into the carrier.

What if the vet visit is only a few days away?

Leave the carrier out now with a familiar blanket and treats, and work through as many of the AAFP's six steps as your cat is comfortable with. If your cat is very stressed, ask your vet about medication before the visit — the AAFP says gabapentin is the one most commonly used.

How do I bring my cat home to my other cats after the vet?

The AAFP suggests keeping the returning cat in the closed carrier at first and watching the other cats' reactions. If there's hissing, give it a separate room with food, water, litter and a bed, swap bedding, and allow contact slowly after a few hours — longer after surgery, as your vet advises.

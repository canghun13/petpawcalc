Dog Prep vs. Cat Prep

Dogs Cats
Main focus Obedience skills, stroller/gear exposure, sound desensitization Sound desensitization, protecting resource access and vertical/hiding space
Gear-specific practice Stroller walks, carrying a weighted object like the baby will be carried Not usually needed - cats aren't walked alongside gear the way dogs are
Space planning Baby gates to control room access Make sure the nursery doesn't block a litter box, food, or a favorite perch - cats need an unobstructed retreat
First introduction On leash, calm and controlled, rewarding relaxed behavior near the baby Let the cat approach at their own pace rather than bringing the baby to them

Why Routine Changes Belong Before the Baby, Not After

One of the easiest mistakes to make is keeping everything exactly the same until the baby comes home, then changing it all at once. If a dog's long morning walk suddenly becomes a quick five-minute trip the same week a baby arrives, or a cat goes from constant lap time to being mostly ignored, the pet can end up associating the baby with the disappearance of everything good in their routine.

Shifting these things gradually in the months beforehand — walk timing and length, how much undivided attention your pet gets, who else might help with walks or play — means the change happens on its own timeline, not the baby's. By the time the baby arrives, the "new normal" is already normal.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far in advance should I start preparing my pet for a new baby?

About four months before the due date, if possible - starting in the second trimester gives enough time to address any existing behavior issues, desensitize your pet to new sounds, and shift routines gradually rather than all at once right before the baby arrives. Dogs and cats both need repetition to build new habits, and starting just a few weeks out makes everything feel sudden and stressful instead.

Why should I change my pet's routine before the baby arrives instead of after?

If a dog's long morning walk or a cat's usual attention suddenly disappears the same week the baby comes home, the pet can associate the baby with the loss of everything good. Shifting walk times, attention levels, and routines gradually in the months before means the baby isn't what "caused" the change from your pet's perspective.

How do I desensitize my pet to baby sounds?

Play recordings of crying, cooing, and other baby sounds starting at a low volume, well below anything that causes a reaction, and reward calm behavior. Gradually increase the volume over multiple sessions across several weeks until your pet stays relaxed even at realistic volume. Rushing the volume up too quickly can create the exact fear response you're trying to prevent.

Is it safe to let my dog or cat sniff the baby?

Yes, in a controlled way - have one person hold the baby securely while another manages the pet on a leash or calmly nearby, and let the pet approach and sniff from a comfortable distance. Reward calm, relaxed behavior. Avoid holding the baby down to the pet's level and don't allow face licking, and if the pet seems overly excited, increase distance and try again once they've settled.

What if my pet seems anxious or fearful about the baby's sounds or presence?

Mild curiosity or brief wariness that fades is normal, but shaking, hiding, house-soiling, or an inability to settle during exposure sessions isn't something to push through on your own - it's a signal to slow down and consult a certified trainer or veterinary behaviorist. These reactions typically worsen rather than resolve on their own without proper support.

Should I ever leave my pet alone with the baby?

No - always supervise interactions between any pet and an infant, no matter how calm or trustworthy the pet has been in the past. This isn't about distrust of your specific pet; unpredictable situations can happen quickly with any animal and any baby, and supervision is the standard safety practice regardless of temperament.
